The "Mānasollāsa" of Sureśvarācārya — A Commentary on the "Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra"

A complete reference: what this text is, when it was written, what it contains, which manuscripts and editions exist, and what exactly it says about āsanas

Compiled 31 October 2026.

Advaita Vedānta · 1895 edition, pages 156–157 · 2008 edition, page 119

0. How to Read This Reference

This reference is written so that it can be read without specialist knowledge: every term is explained either along the way or in the glossary (§9), and every person mentioned is listed in §1.6.

Conventions that apply throughout the text:

  • All Sanskrit quotations have been checked against photographs of the printed pages at original resolution (JPEG 2000 image files from the Internet Archive). The passage on āsanas is given according to the 2008 edition and collated with the 1895 edition; for verses 24–26 the two editions agree word for word.
  • In the 2008 edition (page 119) verse 24 is set as …ब्राह्ममासनम्॥२४॥ — the number follows the word immediately. Some settings of the same text show the separate form with two daṇḍas (…ब्राह्ममासनम् ॥२४॥). This is a typographical detail; it does not affect the reading.
  • Why quotations must not be taken from the text files supplied with the scans: optical recognition of Devanāgarī regularly confuses similar letters and loses diacritics. This has been verified on fresh downloads: in the text files of the 1895 edition one indeed finds खरस्तिकं for स्वस्तिकं, पश्यं for पद्मं, धारं for वीरं, and नृपिहादि! for नृसि#ंहादि! — although the book itself prints the correct reading on page 156.
  • Crops of every quoted page are kept in the folder sources/scans/mss/, so that each quotation can be checked by eye.
  • Page references are given by the printed page numbers (the ones in the running head), not by the leaf number of the scan. Where a leaf number is used, this is stated explicitly.
  • Translations from Sanskrit are my own; where a passage is disputed, the English translation of A. Mahadeva Sastri (editions of 1899/1978) is placed alongside for comparison.
  • Sanskrit is given in Latin transliteration in full IAST: the diacritics of the Sanskrit spelling are written throughout — in headings, in the running text, in the names of the āsanas and in the bibliography alike: Śaṅkara, Sureśvarācārya, Maṇḍana Miśra, Mānasollāsa, Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra, āsana, vārttika. Devanāgarī quotations are left in the original script.
  • Names of deities and words long naturalised in English — Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra, Śiva, Śakti, Brahman, yoga, Sanskrit — are transliterations of Sanskrit and keep their diacritics. Sanskrit place names keep them as well (Vārāṇasī, Śṛṅgeri, Kāñcī, Dvārakā), while the English forms of modern place names and the official names of institutions and libraries are kept as printed (Mysore, Madras, Poona, Chennai, Ramtek, Jammu).
  • Sources written in English are quoted in the original, since in this edition the original is English. Sources in Sanskrit and in Russian are quoted in English translation; where the wording matters, the original is given in a footnote.
  • Everything marked "not established" could not be determined from the available sources. Such places are never filled in with guesses.
  • Where a quotation is given from another author's paraphrase rather than from the work itself, this is stated.

1. What This Text Is

1.1. Vārttika — What the Word Means

The "Mānasollāsa" is not an independent treatise but a vārttika. The definition is given by the editor of 1895:

"'Vārttika' is defined thus: Uktānukta-duruktārtha-vyaktikāri tu vārttikam. A vārttika is that which clearly explains what has been said, what has not been said, and what has been badly said."

— A. Mahadeva Sastri, preface to the first edition, 24 May 1899 (quoted from the 3rd edition, page 5 of the preface; full description in §10)

A vārttika, then, is a genre of commentary that does not merely interpret another text but supplements and corrects it. The "Mānasollāsa" interprets a hymn of ten stanzas attributed to Śaṅkara.

The title मानसोल्लास literally means "the play of the mind" or "the rejoicing of the mind". The English translator renders it as "the brilliant play of thought" (there).

1.2. What the "Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra" Is

The hymn is known as the "Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra", after the name that stands at the end of every stanza. But the title is misleading, and the editor expressly says so:

"The hymn is termed Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra merely because the name Dakṣiṇāmūrti occurs in the last line of each verse as the burthen of the hymn. It is not dedicated to the worship of any form of Śiva as the title may at first sight imply. The chief object of the hymn is, as stated in the Vārttika, to expound briefly the nature of the Ātman — the highest self of man as of all other beings, its relation to the Universe, the means by which it may be realised, and the final bliss resulting from this realisation."

— Preface to the 1895 edition, page 4 (text keyed by volunteers of the Ambuda portal)

He then explains how such a text is to be read:

"In meditating upon the truths embodied in this short hymn with a view to their complete realisation, the devotee has to bear in mind the fact that the Ātman is Parameśvara, the Supreme Lord Himself, and to look upon his Guru, the spiritual teacher, as the embodiment of the Supreme Lord. The problems to which the hymn is intended to offer solutions are stated in the initial verses of the Vārttika and at the commencement of the several sections thereof. They comprise the main heads of the subject-matter of the Brahma-sūtras and are arranged in a similar order."

— there

And one further observation, directly relevant to our subject (āsanas):

"Though the hymn discovers no mark of preference on the part of its author for the Śaiva religion in general or any particular form of it, the author of the Vārttika cannot be said to have been completely free from the influence of the Śaiva cult. He invokes Śaivic gods and goddesses at the commencement of his work and draws [on] Śaiva-Āgamas, or similar works dedicated to the Śaiva cult, for the classification and nomenclature of the principles constituting human nature and the cosmos. The details connected with the mystic practices recommended in the work may also for the most part be traced to the same source."

— there, pages 4–5

This is the key to the section on āsanas: the classification of āsanas by deity (brāhma, vaiṣṇava, raudra, śākta, śaiva) is part of that same Śaiva scheme of which the editor speaks.

The content of the hymn itself, as it reads in the text, is a contemplation of the Ātman through the image of Dakṣiṇāmūrti. The ninth stanza of the hymn (in the printed edition it carries the number 19) enumerates eight principles:

चन्द्रार्कौ कालनेतारौ प्राणापानौ शरीरिणाम् ।साक्षी पुरुष इत्येवं मृत्योष्टकमिदं वपुः ॥१९॥

(1895 edition, page 154)

"The moon and the sun, the movers of time, are the prāṇa and the apāna of embodied beings; the witness is Puruṣa. Thus [is contemplated] this body as eightfold, [and thus is] death [overcome]."

The commentary on the next stanza explains the sense:

एवमष्टमूर्तिस्वरूपं देहं एव सम्पाद्य ध्यायतः फलमाह — समनस्कमिति ॥

(1895 edition, page 154)

"Such is the fruit for one who, having made the body itself, in the form of eight forms, [the object of contemplation], meditates…"

1.3. The Place of the "Mānasollāsa" among the Works of Sureśvara

"There is a Vārttika or commentary by Sureśvarācārya on this hymn, named Mānasollāsa. It is one of the five Vārttikas attributed to Sureśvarācārya."

— Preface to the 1895 edition, page 3

"Five works of the kind are attributed to Sureśvarācārya, all of them having been recently published in India: (1) Mānasollāsa (Mysore). (2) Praṇava-vārttika ("). … (4) An exposition of Śaṅkarācārya's commentary on the Taittirīya-Upaniṣad (Poona, Ānandasrama Series). (5) An exposition of Śaṅkarācārya's commentary on the Bṛhadāraṇyaka-Upaniṣad (Poona, Ānandasrama Series)."

— Preface to the first edition, 1899 (1978 edition, pages 6–7 of the preface)

From the same series: the "Mānasollāsa" and the "Praṇava-vārttika" were published in Mysore, and the "Naiṣkarmya-siddhi" in the "Bombay Sanskrit Series" (footnote there).

1.4. The Structure of the Text and Its Numbering (Important for References)

The text is divided into ullāsas (उल्लास, "wave") — one for each of the ten stanzas of the hymn. Within each ullāsa the verses are counted separately.

It is easy to go wrong here, so let me take an example. In the printed edition the stanzas of the hymn itself do not carry separate numbers 1–10: they are included in the continuous numbering of the ullāsa's verses. Hence the ninth stanza of the hymn (the one the ninth ullāsa interprets) appears as verse 19, and the verses of the vārttika that follow it are 20, 21, 22, 23 and so on. The section on āsanas is verses 24–26.

Hence the rule for references: 9.24 is read as "ullāsa nine, verse twenty-four", not as "verse 24 of the hymn".

The numbering in the different editions almost coincides, but not entirely. In the "Mādhurī" edition (1987) the stanza boundaries are shifted by half a verse: the half-verse which in the 1895 edition belongs to verse 23 is marked there as ॥२४ क॥ ("24a"). In the English translation (1899/1978) the section on āsanas appears as "24-26", while the closing verse निरालम्बतया ध्यानं is already assigned to number 27. A reference must therefore always state the edition.

Length. According to Swami Harshananda the text contains 367 ślokas:

"an exhaustive gloss (vārttika) by Sureśvara (of the Common Era 800) on the famous hymn … of Śaṅkara (of the Common Era 788-820)"

— Hindupedia, article "Mānasollāsa": the same source states that the text contains 367 ślokas and that Rāmatīrtha's commentary is dated to the 17th century.

This is a figure taken from a reference work, not a fact verified against the editions: the available editions contain no continuous count of ślokas.

1.5. Different Texts Bearing the Same Title

Several different works bear the word "Mānasollāsa", and catalogues confuse them. They are listed here so that the reader does not mix them up:

  1. Our text — Sureśvara's vārttika on the "Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra".
  2. "Mānasollāsa-vardhinī" (मानसोल्लासवर्धिनी) — the commentary of Anantakrishna Shastri (20th century), published in 2008.
  3. "Mādhurī" (माधुरी) — the commentary of Swami Maheshananda Giri (edited by 1987).
  4. The "Mānasollāsa" of Someśvara 3 — an encyclopaedia of the Western Chalukya court, 1131. A completely different work.
  5. The "Mānasollāsa" of Appayya Dīkṣita — 17 verses.
  6. The "Mānasollāsa" of Krishnananda and the "Mānasollāsa" of Govinda — other texts.
  7. "Jayamādhava-mānasollāsa" — a work on architecture.

In Aufrecht's "Catalogus Catalogorum" our text appears not under its own title but under the heading "Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra": "Mānasollāsa … — by Sureśvara. See Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra" (quoted from wisdomlib, article "Mānasollāsa"). This explains why a search for the word "Mānasollāsa" in manuscript catalogues turns up mainly the encyclopaedia of Someśvara.

3. What the Treatise Describes

3.1. The Design: Yoga Here Is a Means, Not an End

The "Mānasollāsa" is a philosophical commentary. Yogic material occupies a subordinate place in it, and the text says so itself:

बिना केवलात्मोपासनमविद्यानिवर्तकमात्मानं लभते।अतो योगाभ्यासाङ्गम्, तदुपायो वाऽनेन प्रतिपाद्यते।

(2008 edition, page 119)

"Without [the practice of] the worship of the Ātman alone, one does not attain the Ātman who removes ignorance. Therefore [yoga] is set out here as a limb of yogic practice, or as its means."

3.2. The Ninth Ullāsa: Verse by Verse

Below is the composition of the ninth ullāsa according to the printed edition of 1895. Verse numbers and pages are those of this edition; all quotations are taken from the scans.

Verses 17–18, pages 153–154 — the five breaths and space. The five vāyus are enumerated:

नागो हिक्काकरः कूर्मो निमेषोन्मेषकारकः ।क्षुतं करोति कृकरो देवदत्तो विजृम्भणम् ॥१७॥

"Nāga causes hiccups, Kūrma the closing and opening of the eyes, Kṛkara sneezing, Devadatta yawning." Verse 18 concerns ākāśa: आकाशो बहिरप्यन्तरवकाशं प्रयच्छति ॥१८॥.

Verse 19, page 154 — the stanza of the hymn (see §1.2): the moon and the sun as prāṇa and apāna, the witness being Puruṣa.

Verse 20, page 154 — the transition to the eightfold yoga:

समनस्कमिदं योगी सेवमान उपासनम् ।अष्टाङ्गयोगयुक्तस्तन्मनस्कं स गच्छति ॥२०॥

English translation (1978 edition, page 168): "20. Practising samanaska-yoga — this worship with the manas — the yogin, perfected in eight-limbed yoga, goes to Amanaska (Īśvara), to That which has no manas". There follows the enumeration of the eight limbs themselves: yama, niyama, āsana, prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, dhyāna, samādhi.

Verses 21–22, page 155 — yama:

मनःप्रसादस्सन्तोषो मौनमिन्द्रियनिग्रहः ।दया दाक्षिण्यमास्तिक्यमार्जवं मार्दवं क्षमा ॥२१॥भावशुद्धिरहिंसा च ब्रह्मचर्यं स्मृतिर्धृतिः ।इत्येवमादयोऽन्ये च मनस्साध्या यमाः स्मृताः ॥२२॥

Verse 23, page 155 — niyama:

स्नानं शौचं क्रतुस्सत्यं जपो होमश्च तर्पणम् ।तपो दानं तितिक्षा च नमस्कारः प्रदक्षिणम् ॥२३व्रतोपवासाद्याश्चान्ये कायिकाः नियमाः स्मृताः ।

Verses 24–26, page 156 — āsanas. Fully analysed in §6.

Verse 27, page 157 — prāṇāyāma:

रेचकः पूरकश्चैव कुम्भकः प्राणसंयमः ॥२७॥

"Restraint of the breath [consists of] exhalation (recaka), inhalation (pūraka) and retention (kumbhaka)".

Verses 28–30, page 157 — pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, dhyāna, samādhi:

इन्द्रियाणां समस्तानां विषयेभ्यो निवारणम् ।

प्रत्याहार इति प्रोक्तं प्रत्याहारार्थवेदिभिः ॥२८॥आधारे क्वापि मनसस्स्थापनं धारणोच्यते ।ब्रह्मविष्णुशिवादीनां चिन्ता ध्यानं प्रचक्षते ॥२९॥ध्यानादस्पन्दनं बुद्धेः समाधिरभिधीयते ।अमनस्कसमाधिस्तु सर्वचिन्ताविवर्जितम् ॥३०॥

Verses 34–37, pages 159–160 — the four signs of yoga (yoga-lakṣaṇas), by stages:

मलमूत्रकफाल्पत्वमारोग्यं लघुता तनोः ।सुगन्धस्स्वरवर्णत्वं प्रथमं योगलक्षणम् ॥३४कण्टकाग्रेष्वसङ्गत्वं जलपङ्केष्वमज्जनम् ।क्षुत्पिपासादिसहिष्णुत्वं द्वितीयं योगलक्षणम् ॥३५॥बह्वन्नपानभोक्तृत्वमातपाग्निसहिष्णुता ।दूरान्न श्रवणं दूरात्तृतीयं योगलक्षणम् ॥३६॥मण्डूकह्रवनं भूमौ मर्क टह्रवनं द्रुमे ।आकाशगमनं चेति चतुर्थं योगलक्षणम् ॥३७॥

The signs are: (1) a decrease of impurities, health, lightness of body, a pleasant smell, a clear voice; (2) insensitivity to the tip of a thorn, not sinking in water and mud, endurance of hunger and thirst; (3) the ability to eat and drink much, to endure heat and fire, to hear at a great distance; (4) the "leap of a frog" on the ground, the "leap of a monkey" in a tree, movement through the air. This is the same set as in the later haṭha texts — but here it belongs to the eight-limbed yoga of a Vedantic commentary.

3.3. What the Treatise Does Not Contain

So as not to attribute to the text more than it contains:

  • There are no techniques of āsanas. Only names are listed. According to the 1895 edition, the description is placed outside the text: स्वस्तिकाद्यासनानां लक्षणानि योगग्रन्थेभ्योवगन्तव्यानिQ — "the characteristics of the āsanas beginning with svastika are to be learned from yoga-texts" (page 157).
  • There is no kuṇḍalinī, no bandhas and no mudrās in the section on āsanas — in none of the ullāsas containing yogic material.
  • There is no Russian translation — neither complete nor of the āsana passage.
  • There is no critical apparatus to the vārttika apart from the 1895 edition. In the 2008 edition the whole volume contains two marks पाठान्तरम् ("variant reading"), and both are outside the section on āsanas.

And three further things that should be stated plainly, so that the reader does not look for them in vain:

  • This is not a manual of yoga. There are no descriptions of technique, no rules of performance, no contraindications; the enumeration of āsanas occupies three verses and serves as a support for the argument about the Ātman, not as a practical guide.
  • This is not haṭha yoga. The word "haṭha" does not occur in the text; the complex of āsanas, bandhas and mudrās usually associated with haṭha is absent here. The resemblance to later haṭha texts lies only in the names of individual postures.
  • The numbering of the ullāsas in the manuscripts does not match the printed one. The ullāsa that is the ninth in the editions is the seventh in the Ramtek manuscript and the sixth in the Jammu manuscript (§4.4, §4.5). A reference such as "ullāsa 9" is a reference to the printed editions.

4. Manuscripts

4.1. The Seven Copies Used in the 1895 Edition

This is the only corpus described by the editor himself. The preface lists all seven copies:

"The works comprised in this volume have been edited from the following manuscripts:— I. The Text with Svayamprakāśa's commentary. — A Telugu MS. belonging to Paṇḍit Dakṣiṇāmūrti Śāstri of the Oriental Library. — A Nāgarī MS. procured by Paṇḍit Narahari Jośyar of the Oriental Library.

— A Telugu MS. kindly lent by Paṇḍit Gangādhara Śāstri of H. H. the Mahārāja's Sanskrit College. 2. The Vārttika with Rāmatīrtha's commentary. — A Grantha MS. of the Vārttika belonging to Sir K. Śeṣādri Iyer, Dewan of Mysore. This codex is very much injured, and in consequence portions of the work have also disappeared. — A Telugu MS. copy of the Vārttika kindly lent by Paṇḍit Kāma Śāstriar of Madras. — A Grantha MS. of the Vārttika with Rāmatīrtha's commentary thereon, belonging to the Oriental Library."

— Preface to the 1895 edition

On the quality of the copies:

"The MSS. of Svayamprakāśa's commentary are indifferent; and all of them exhibit errors of commission and of omission, not purely clerical. The copies of the Vārttika, on the other hand, are good, the last being by far the best. Free as this last is from errors except a few clerical ones … it has been found a very valuable guide as furnishing a commentary which has enabled us to restore the text of the Vārttika to the form in which it was at the time of the commentator. The readings of the other two, where suggestive, have been given as foot-notes."

— there

And a "Postscript" — the seventh manuscript, obtained already at the final proof stage:

"Just as I was going to send to the press the final proof of the pages of the Preface …, I came by a recently written Telugu MS. of the Mānasollāsa with Rāna-tīrtha's commentary, belonging to Paṇḍit Sītārāmaśāstri of the Bangalore Sanskrit College. Though, as regards general accuracy, this MS. can hardly compare with the Grantha MS. on which the present edition is mainly based, its readings are in a few cases found decidedly superior to those of the other MS. or manuscripts I note at the end of the volume not only all these readings and such variae lectiones as are otherwise deserving of notice, but also some typographical errors…"

— there; postscript dated 15 April 1895

What follows from this. The critical edition of 1895 is an assemblage of seven copies, and the principal copy was defective, part of the text having been lost in it. All the variants that the editor considered worthy of attention he placed at the end of the volume — and that is the only published corpus of variants of this text.

The editor gives no dates for the manuscripts: only script, material and owner. There are neither colophons nor palaeographic estimates in the preface.

4.4. Manuscript M-1192 (Ramtek): the Colophon Read

Leaf 18, the last two lines:

… इति श्रीदक्षिणामूर्तिस्तोत्रवार्तिके प्रपञ्चोपशमे सप्तमोल्लासः समाप्तः ॥२५॥॥ इति श्रीमच्छङ्कराचार्यविरचितमानसोल्लासप्रकरणं ॥१८॥

"Thus in the 'Vārttika' on the 'Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra', [called] 'The Pacification of the Manifested', the seventh ullāsa is completed. Thus [ends] the work 'Mānasollāsa', composed by Śrī Śaṅkarācārya."

Three conclusions:

  • there is no year — neither era nor number; the "18" at the end is the leaf number;
  • the author of the vārttika is not named: the colophon names Śaṅkara, that is, the work is ascribed to the author of the hymn, not to the author of the vārttika;
  • the count of ullāsas is different: here the same ullāsa is called the seventh, whereas in the printed editions it is the ninth.

On the first leaf there is a handwritten library card, filled in in English:

Accession number 7202 M 1192

Title – मानसोल्लास

Author – ? Date – ?

That is, the library itself left the fields "author" and "date" empty. The stamp "Checked 2012" is the year of the stock check, not a date.

What the manuscript yielded in addition. First, it is not only a vārttika: the incipit begins श्रीगणेशाय नमः ॥ मङ्गलं दिशतु मे विनायको…, that is, both the hymn and the vārttika are copied. Second, in verse 24 stands पद्मं — the same reading as in both printed editions, and not पश्यं, which is what machine recognition of the 1895 scan produced. This is an independent confirmation of the correct reading.

4.5. The Jammu Manuscript: a Date, but the Number Not Read

Leaf 120. In the last lines the formula … शके … can be read — a Śaka-era year is given. Before it come metrical particulars about the number of granthas, after it a wish for well-being.

The year number has not been read. The type has faded, the ink is uneven, at magnification the digits lose their shape, and different passes give different readings. The number is therefore not given here: a guess must not look like a fact. Two crops at natural resolution are supplied for the reading: sources/scans/mss/jammu-6029-kolofon-celekom.png and jammu-6029-kolofon-dve-stroki.png.

The conversion, once the number is read: the Śaka era begins in 78 CE, so Śaka year + 78 = CE (for example, 1400 Śaka is 1478 CE). A dated manuscript would give an upper bound for the text: the vārttika could not have been composed later.

On the count of ullāsas. In the Jammu manuscript the same ullāsa is called the sixth, in the Ramtek one the seventh, in the printed editions the ninth. Three different counts for one text: one cannot refer to the manuscripts by ullāsa numbers without collating them.

4.6. Where Else Copies Exist: Aufrecht's Catalogue

The seven copies of the 1895 edition are not the whole corpus. Theodor Aufrecht's "Catalogus Catalogorum" (1891–1903), in the article "Dakṣiṇāmūrtistotra", lists copies of the work "Mānasollāsa or Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotravarttika by Sureśvara" under library shelf marks:

"C. Mānasollāsa or Dakṣiṇāmūrtistotravārttika by Sureśvara" IO. 443. W. page 179. Hall page 110. L. 1763. 1783. B. 4, 82. Ben. 67. Tüb. 16. Pheh 12. Rādh 6. NW. 280. Oudh VIII, 24. Burnell 91b. Bhr. 258. 259. Taylor 1, 1. Oppert 3454. II, 10042.

In the same place, in a separate nest, copies of Rāmatīrtha's commentary:

"Mānasollāsavṛttāntavilāsa by Rāmatīrtha" IO. 443. W. page 179. Hall page 110. L. 141. 1763. 1783. K. 126. Oudh X, 22.

And in a third nest, copies of the hymn itself with this commentary:

"C. Mānasollāsa or Dakṣiṇāmūrtistotravārttika by Sureśvara" BL. 184. Fl. 228. IO. 443. 968. 1998. Peters. 4, 21 (and C.). Stein 120.

What these abbreviations mean — and what I do not know. The marks IO, L., B., Hall, Burnell, Oppert, Stein and others refer to printed catalogues of individual collections, and are deciphered by a key printed in the preface to the first volume of the "Catalogus Catalogorum". I have not opened that key, and therefore do not supply cities and collections here: such a decipherment must not be done from memory, since it is easy to confuse which catalogue is hidden behind a single letter. Only IO is securely identified — India Office Library, now the Oriental Manuscripts collection of the British Library in London — together with BL, the British Library itself.

What this list does not give. Aufrecht's catalogue is a summary of printed catalogues of the 19th and early 20th centuries. It does not confirm that a copy has survived: over a century manuscripts are lost, collections move, shelf marks change. And as a rule it says nothing about dating.

The practical conclusion. Until they are collated, it is premature to assert anything about the manuscript tradition of the text.

6. Āsanas: What the Text Says and Which Edition to Quote

6.1. Which Edition to Quote — the Answer

The principal edition for quotation is that of 1895 (Mysore, Bibliotheca Sanskrita number 6), printed page 156. The reasons:

  1. It is the first critical edition and the only one in which the vārttika is provided with an apparatus of variants.
  2. It is freely available in at least two independent scans.
  3. The numbering of ullāsas and verses coincides with that of the 2008 edition, so the reference 9.24 works for both.

The reading was verified against the page image: sources/scans/mss/1895-str156-stihi24-26.png.

6.4. Where to Look for Descriptions of the Āsanas — and the Divergence between the Editions

For verse 24 the two editions give different instructions.

1895 edition, page 157 (sources/scans/mss/1895-str157-yogagranthebhyah.png):

स्वस्तिकाद्यासनानां लक्षणानि योगग्रन्थेभ्योवगन्तव्यानि ॥

"The characteristics of the āsanas beginning with svastika are to be learned from yoga-texts."

2008 edition, page 119 (sources/scans/mss/2008-str119-gurupadesha-yogopanishatsu.png):

अथासनानि विभजते — स्वस्तिकमिति ।इम आसनप्रकाराः गुरूपदेशगम्याः पद्मवज्रासनादयः केचन योगोपनिषत्सु निर्दिष्टाः ।

"Then he divides the āsanas: 'svastika' and the rest. These kinds of āsanas are to be reached through the instruction of a guru; some, beginning with padma- and vajrāsana, are indicated in the yoga-upaniṣads."

How this is to be assessed — without forcing:

  • both phrases are set within the section मानसोल्लासवार्तिकम्, that is, both editors regarded them as the text of the vārttika;
  • in the 2008 edition the main text is set in bold and the interpretation in roman type; the disputed phrase is set in bold, that is, it is not an insertion by the editor;
  • the difference concerns two points: गुरूपदेशगम्याः ("to be reached through the instruction of a guru") against योगग्रन्थेभ्योवगन्तव्याः ("to be learned from yoga-texts"), and the presence of पद्मवज्रासनादयः … योगोपनिषत्सु निर्दिष्टाः ("beginning with padma- and vajrāsana … indicated in the yoga-upaniṣads"), which is entirely absent from the 1895 edition.

The cause of the divergence has not been established. The possible explanations — all three as yet unverified — are: (a) different readings of different manuscripts (recall that the principal Grantha manuscript was defective and part of the text lost in it); (b) in 2008 the text of a later commentary has been mixed into the vārttika; (c) in 1895 the phrase is abridged, and in 2008 given in full. The question can be settled only by collation with the Ramtek M-1192 and Jammu number 6029 manuscripts.

Why this matters. The statement "the Mānasollāsa refers to the yoga-upaniṣads" rests on the 2008 edition, not on the 1895 edition. Until the manuscripts are collated, the correct formulation is:

"In the 2008 edition (Vārāṇasī) this verse is commented upon with a reference to the yoga-upaniṣads; in the 1895 edition (Mysore) the reference at this point is to yoga-texts."

6.5. Collation of the Verses Themselves: 1895 against 2008

Checked against the scans of both books. There are no divergences of substance. The only difference that could be found:

  • 1895: निरालम्बस्सदाशिवः
  • 2008: निरालम्बः सदाशिवः

This is a variant of sandhi (the rules of word junction in writing); the meaning is the same.

For completeness: in the 1987 edition ("Mādhurī") the same stanza, in the word analysis (padaccheda), is set as विदुः शाक्तम् (separately), and the preceding half of the stanza is marked ॥२४ क॥. This means that the stanza boundaries there are shifted by half a verse: the verse number will coincide, but the boundaries will not.

6.6. Separately: पश्चिमतानक — Not "Paścimottānāsana"

पश्चिमतानक (paścima-tanaka, literally "that which stretches the back [of the body]") is classified in the text as a śaiva posture. In the later haṭha tradition the name has not survived in this form, and the modern "paścimottānāsana" is a different formation ( पश्चिमोत्तान). They must not be identified. This is a case where "similar" does not mean "the same".

7. What Remains Unestablished

The list of honest gaps is at the same time a programme of work:

  1. The dating of the manuscripts. No verified source offers a dated manuscript of the "Mānasollāsa". The colophons of Ramtek M-1192 and Jammu number 6029 are needed, together with a search in the New Catalogus Catalogorum.
  2. The cause of the divergence in the commentary on verse 24 (yoga-texts against guru and yoga-upaniṣads).
  3. The literal argument of Thrasher 1979 and Nakamura 1983 — their texts are not available in the public domain; there is only the paraphrase in Dragonetti–Tola and in the Stanford Encyclopedia.
  4. The complete apparatus of variants of the 1895 edition for verses 24–26 — the list is placed at the end of the volume and reads badly in the available text files; an examination of the last pages of the scan is required.
  5. The number of ślokas (367) — a figure from a reference work; the available editions contain no continuous count. 6.

The precise date of Svayamprakāśa's "Tattvasudhā" — the 16th century is repeated in the literature, but no substantiation of it is to be found either in the prefaces to the editions of 1895, 1978 and 2008 or in the accessible catalogues. The statement is left without support in a source and requires verification against the colophons of the "Tattvasudhā" manuscripts.

8. How to Verify Everything Said Here Yourself

Instructions for anyone who wishes to check the quotations:

  1. Open the page of the edition on the Internet Archive and download the …_jp2.zip file (the SHOW ALL button in the downloads section). These are the original scans.
  2. To establish which leaf corresponds to which page, download …_scandata.xml and look for pageNumber. For example, in the scan of the 1895 edition leaf 166 = printed page 155, leaf 167 = page 156, leaf 168 = page 157. In the scan of the 2008 edition leaf 170 = page 119, leaf 171 = page 120.
  3. Extract the required leaf and look at it:
    import zipfile
from PIL import Image
z = zipfile.ZipFile("downloaded_jp2.zip")
name = [n for n in z.namelist() if n.endswith("_0167.jp2")][0]
open("leaf.jp2", "wb").write(z.read(name))
im = Image.open("leaf.jp2")
im.resize((im.width // 3, im.height // 3)).save("view.png")
  4. Ready-made crops of all quoted pages are already in sources/scans/mss/.

If other manuscripts need to be sought. The National Mission for Manuscripts database of India, NAMAMI (pandulipipatala.nic.in), is the register of all the manuscripts of the country; its filters include switches "Digitization = Yes" and "Public View = Yes", which select what has been digitised and is open. The database may be inaccessible from some countries — that is a restriction of access, not an absence of records. The printed union of copies by library is Aufrecht's catalogue, analysed in §4.6.
