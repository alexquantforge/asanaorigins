PART III. COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS AND HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE

Śārṅgadharapaddhati

Haṭhapradīpikāverses 1.45–46 · right here

This is how the verse is printed in the critical edition; below are the translation and every manuscript named in its apparatus, so that nothing has to be looked up in another section.

उत्तानौ चरणौ कृत्वा ऊरुसंस्थौ प्रयत्नतः ।ऊरुमध्ये तथोत्तानौ पाणी कृत्वा ततो दृशौ ॥नासाग्रे विन्यसेद्राजदन्तमूलं च जिह्वया ।उत्तभ्य चिबुकं वक्षस्यास्थाप्य पवनं शनैः ॥

uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā ūrusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ /ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī kṛtvā tato dṛśau //nāsāgre vinyased rājadantamūlaṃ ca jihvayā /uttabhya cibukaṃ vakṣasy āsthāpya pavanaṃ śanaiḥ //

Edition translation Carefully put the upturned feet on the thighs and the upturned hands in the middle of the thighs, fix the eyes on the tip of the nose, raise the root of the uvula with the tongue, place the chin on the chest, gently [draw in] the breath [...].

Our rendering The feet turned up on the thighs and the hands turned up in the middle of the thighs; the gaze on the tip of the nose; the tongue raises the root of the uvula; the chin on the chest; the breath drawn gently.

Conclusion

The convergence of scribal colophons, genealogical verses 1.2–1.6, the documented reign of King Hammīradeva of Ranthambhore (1282–1301 CE), and contemporary Persian accounts provides unassailable certainty for the composition date of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati in 1363 CE.

For the historian of yoga, this proves that precise physical descriptions of Padmāsana, Ardhāsana, and Siddhāsana, alongside the five fundamental mudrās (Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandhara, Uḍḍīyāna, and Mūlabandha), were already canonized and circulating across courtly and scholastic milieus in Northern India by the mid-14th century. This firmly anchors the earlier composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra to the early 13th century and demonstrates the unbroken transmission of early Haṭhayoga somatic practices.
