HAṬHATATTVAKAUMUDĪ OF SUNDARADEVA

CHAPTER 7: ATHĀSANĀNI (TEACHING ON ĀSANAS)

Haṭha yoga · critical edition · after M. L. Gharote's printed edition (Lonavla, 2007/2020) · verses 1–45

Academic Critical Textological Edition, Word-by-Word Grammatical Analysis, and Philological Study

SECTION I. HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL, AND TEXTOLOGICAL INTRODUCTION

1.1. Author's Identity and Socio-Cultural Context of the Text

The treatise «Haṭhatattvakaumudī» (Haṭhatattvakaumudī, literally "Moonlight on the Fundamental Principles of Haṭha [Yoga]") was composed by the distinguished Indian encyclopedist and scholar-physician Sundaradeva (Sundaradeva). Sundaradeva belonged to the prestigious Brahmin lineage of the Kāśyapa clan (Kāśyapa gotra) and resided in the holy city of Vārāṇasī (Vārāṇasī, Kāśī / Benares) — the foremost center of traditional Sanskrit learning, manuscript production, and philosophical disputation in late pre-colonial Northern India. From the author's colophons preserved across the chapters of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī as well as his medical writings, the precise genealogy of his family is documented:

  • Grandfather — Śivadeva (Śivadeva), a revered paṇḍita and scholar;
  • Father — Govindadeva (Govindadeva), a celebrated hereditary Āyurvedic physician (vaidya), preceptor, and spiritual guru of Sundaradeva;
  • Sundaradeva himself represented an extraordinary synthesis of deep esoteric initiations into diverse yoga traditions (Haṭha, Rāja, Mantra, Laya, and Nāda yoga) combined with professional expertise in classical Indian medicine (Āyurveda). Beyond the monumental compendium of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī (comprising 56 chapters), Sundaradeva authored several authoritative medical works:
  1. «Bhūpalavallabha» (Bhūpalavallabha / Bhūpālavallabha) — a comprehensive Āyurvedic compendium on clinical medicine and therapeutics;
  2. «Caityapradīpikā» (Caityapradīpikā);
  3. Scholarly commentaries on the classical medical samhitas of Vāgbhaṭa (Vāgbhaṭa) and Śārṅgadhara (Śārṅgadhara-saṃhitā). This medical training decisively shaped the character of Chapter 7: unlike most medieval yoga anthologies where āsanas (āsana) are listed solely for bodily steadiness or awakening kuṇḍalinī (kuṇḍalinī), Sundaradeva analyzes each posture through the lens of a clinical physician. He meticulously records the physiological mechanisms acting upon the bodily humors (doṣa — vāta, pitta, kapha), the cure of 80 vātic disorders, the dissolution of glandular tumors and spasms in the chest (vātagaṇḍa), and the stimulation of the gastric digestive fire (jaṭharāgni).

1.3. Textual Foundations, Limitations of Gharote's Edition, and Uncollated Manuscripts

1.3.1. The Printed Edition by Doctor M. L. Gharote (Lonavla, 2007/2020) as the Primary Study Source

The present research, word-by-word grammatical analysis, and critical translation of Chapter 7 are established upon the sole printed edition of the text currently available in global Indological scholarship:

  • Bibliographic Record of the Edition:
  • Full Title: Hathatatvakaumudi (A Treatise on Hathayoga by Sundaradeva).
  • Critical Edition, Transliteration, English Translation, Notes & Appendices: Doctor M. L. Gharote, Doctor Parimal Devnath, Vijay Kant Jha.
  • Publisher: The Lonavla Yoga Institute (India), Lonavla, Pune, Maharashtra.
  • Publication History: First Edition — 2007; Second Impression — 2012; Third Impression — 2020.
  • ISBN: 81-901617-5-X. Total Extent: 834 pages.
  • Investigated Corpus: Chapter 7 (Āsananirūpaṇa — "Determination of Āsanas"), occupying pages 58–69 of the printed volume, comprising 45 verses (śloka) accompanied by brief editorial notes and textual footnotes.

This monumental volume is the exact work displayed in the photographic records of the book's cover and imprint pages that served as the reference for this project. Published under the direction of Doctor M. L. Gharote (1931–2005), this landmark publication successfully brought Sundaradeva's 56-chapter encyclopedic work into scholarly circulation. Nevertheless, rigorous stemmatic scrutiny reveals profound philological limitations that must be explicitly evaluated.

1.3.2. Critical Scrutiny of Gharote's Apparatus: The Illusion of Two Witnesses and Eliminatio codicum descriptorum

In the Introduction to the edition (pages i–ii), the editors state that their critical text was prepared by collating two manuscript witnesses:

  1. Manuscript «a»: Scindia Oriental Institute (SOI), Ujjain, Acc. number 4235. Country-made paper, 370 folios (Devanāgarī, 12 lines per page, 37 letters per line). Dated in the scribal colophon to Saṃvat 1904 (1847 CE).
  2. Manuscript «b»: Kaivalyadhama S.M.Y.M. Samiti Library, Lonavla, Acc. number 13247. However, on page ii of the Introduction, the editors make a crucial admission:

«Accession number 13247, Kaivalyadhama, Lonavla. This is a photocopy of a transcript prepared from the manuscript of the Scindia Oriental Institute, Ujjain.»

In classical Lachmannian stemmatics, Manuscript «b» is not an independent witness, but a direct apograph (modern transcript) derived from Manuscript «a». According to the established philological rule of eliminatio codicum descriptorum (the elimination of derived copies when the exemplar is preserved), Manuscript «b» possesses zero independent genealogical weight. Every variant recorded in the apparatus between «a» and «b» represents either an oversight of the twentieth-century transcriber, a typographical error in the transcript, or an arbitrary editorial normalization. Consequently, the 2007 Lonavla edition is de facto an edition of a single manuscript (codex unicus) copied in 1847 CE, almost a century after Sundaradeva completed his composition.

1.3.3. Uncollated and Excluded Manuscripts of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī

Far from being preserved in a single codex, several other significant witnesses of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī survive in international and Indian archives that were excluded from the 2007 edition:

  1. The Berlin Manuscripts (Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin — Preußischer Kulturbesitz):
  • MS Chambers 455 b (Weber Cat. number 648 b; Qalamos ID: DE1Bookmanuscript00005370): 33 folios in cursive Devanāgarī. Contains the concluding sections (including Rāja-yoga) and the extensive authorial colophon. Copied by the scribe Śivadatta in Saṃvat 1895 / 1839 CE (śake 1761) — 8 years older than the Ujjain exemplar.
  • MS Chambers 455 a (Weber Cat. number 648 a): 46 folios in Devanāgarī, copied circa Saṃvat 1891 / 1835 CE — 12 years older than Gharote's primary exemplar.
  • Reason for Editorial Rejection: Doctor Gharote writes (Introduction, page ii): «Photocopies of the two manuscripts of Haṭhatattvakaumudī from Berlin Library were obtained, but could not be used because they contained incomplete and disordered folios». The editors had photocopies in hand, but chose not to reconstruct the scrambled leaves (folia disjecta), thereby discarding invaluable independent textual witnesses.
  1. The Varanasi Repositories (Sarasvati Bhavan Library / Sampurnanand Sanskrit University):
  • Since Sundaradeva's family lived and practiced in Vārāṇasī for generations (kāśistha), the codices preserved in the Benares Sanskrit College collection represent the primary local recension closest to the author's milieu.
  • Manuscripts Dated Saṃvat 1846 (1789 CE): Kaivalyadhama's updated Descriptive Catalogue of Yoga Manuscripts (2005, pages 494–495) records two manuscripts under the title Haṭhatattvakaumudī housed in the Sanskrit College, Varanasi, bearing the date Saṃvat 1846 (1789 CE). These codices are 58 years older than the Ujjain manuscript and date directly to the lifetime of Sundaradeva or his immediate disciples.
  • MS number 29853: The official descriptive catalog of the Sarasvati Bhavan collection (A Descriptive Catalogue of the Sanskrit Manuscripts... Sarasvati Bhavana, volume 7, 1961, pages 294–295) lists an undated paper manuscript of Haṭhatattvakaumudī under Acc. number 29853. It was completely ignored in Gharote's collation.
  • The Abhinava Saṅgraha Collection (number 93896): Earlier handlists and registry records in Benares list further accessions containing excerpts and related materials that remain unexamined in modern scholarship.

1.3.4. The Sister Corpus: Manuscripts of Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā

Sundaradeva authored a closely related twin compendium: the «Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā» (Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā). In this work, the author reproduces verbatim extensive passages, classification schemes, āsana definitions, and shared citations from foundational authorities (Haṭhapradīpikā, Yogabīja, Amanaska, Dattātreyayogaśāstra, Śivasaṃhitā, Yogacintāmaṇi). The surviving manuscripts of the Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā serve as prime indirect witnesses (testimonia):

  • Cambridge University Library: Shelfmark MS Add. 2145, dated Saṃvat 1888 / 1831 CE (16 years older than the Ujjain codex).
  • Government Oriental Manuscripts Library (Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, manuscript Chennai): Shelfmark MS R. 3239 (Acc. number 4390). A complete, well-preserved paper codex.
  • Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash Library (Jodhpur): Shelfmark MS number 2244, preserving the intact genealogy of Sundaradeva's lineage.
  • Sarasvati Bhavan Library (Varanasi): Local Benares copies of the text.

1.3.5. Reasons for the Lack of Open Digital Access to Manuscripts

Scholars and researchers cannot currently consult these witnesses freely via open-access web repositories due to specific archival constraints:

  1. Sarasvati Bhavan Library (Varanasi): Houses over 100,000 un-digitized paper and palm-leaf manuscripts (pothī) preserved in traditional cloth bundles (vastra-bandha). The library does not operate an open-access digital IIIF repository. While selective microfilming was conducted by the National Mission for Manuscripts (NMM) and Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts, New Delhi, access to these records requires official institutional permission or in-person archival consultation in Varanasi.
  2. Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin (Berlin State Library): While detailed metadata for Chambers 455 a and 455 b have been incorporated into the German national portal Qalamos (the Orient-Digital project), full high-resolution digital facsimiles have not yet been released for public open-web viewing. Access currently requires a paid digitization-on-demand request (Digitalisierung on Demand) or consultation in the Oriental Reading Room at Potsdamer Straße in Berlin.

1.3.6. Philological Consequences for the Text of Chapter 7

Confining the textual foundation to a single 1847 exemplar resulted in the preservation of unresolved textual cruces (cruces interpretum):

  • Verse 11 (Kukkuṭāsana): The reading of the Ujjain manuscript sūcībhira (with the transcript variant «b»: sūcībhikaram) represents a scribal corruption of North Indian Devanāgarī (sūcī- for suśī- or kṣurī-). Access to Berlin or Varanasi witnesses would allow scholars to restore the genuine reading without conjectural emendation.
  • Verses 14 (Prasāritāsana) and 17 (Padmāsana): Metric anomalies in complex meters (Śārdūlavikrīḍita, Vasantatilakā), where the Ujjain scribe dropped syllables or duplicated consonants.
  • Verse 35: A metric deficiency in pāda c (dharmasya ca vidhā[na]taḥ), left unrestored in the 2007 edition.
  • The Illusion of Witness Consensus: When the 2007 apparatus reports no variant readings, readers assume an established consensus of witnesses; in reality, it reflects only the unverified reading of a solitary 1847 copyist.

1.4. Principles of Transliteration and Editorial Presentation

  • Devanāgarī: Displayed in traditional Indic script.
  • IAST: Standard International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration with full diacritical marks.
  • Word-by-Word Grammatical Analysis: Each lexical item is isolated with root/stem, morphological category (part of speech, gender, case, number, or verbal form), and contextual meaning.
  • Translations: Provided in both literal (word-for-word) and literary/scholarly English.
  • Textological Commentary: Evaluates the variants between manuscripts «a» and «b», diagnosing semantic shifts, scribal corruptions, haplographies, and metrical regularizations.
