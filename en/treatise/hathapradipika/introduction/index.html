Introduction: why āsana comes first

Verses: 1.17, 1.18

Verses 1.17–1.18 frame the section: āsana as the first step and the announcement of the list. Edition footnote: after 1.18 the manuscript “epsilon-1” adds two verses of its own with its own list of āsanas (see below).

Verse 1.17

Verse page in the electronic edition: #hp01_017

हठस्य प्रथमाङ्गत्वाद्आसनं पूर्वमुच्यते ।तत्कुर्यादासनं स्थैर्यमारोग्यं चाङ्गपाटवम् ॥

haṭhasya prathamāṅgatvādāsanaṃ pūrvam ucyate /tat kuryād āsanaṃ sthairyam ārogyaṃ cāṅgapāṭavam //

Edition translation (English)

Because it is the first auxiliary of Haṭha, āsana is taught first. This type of āsana brings about steadiness, good health and physical fitness.

Our rendering

Āsana is the first limb of Haṭha yoga, and so it is taught first: it brings steadiness, health and suppleness of the limbs.

Literal translation

Because [āsana] is the first constituent part of Haṭha [yoga], it is taught first. That āsana one should perform [for the sake of] steadiness, health and suppleness of the limbs.

Some āsanas accepted by sages — by Vasiṣṭha and others — and by yogis — by Matsyendra and others — are taught by me.

Literary translation

In Haṭha yoga āsana is the first step, and so it is taught before everything else. Āsanas are practised for the steadiness of the body, for health and for suppleness of the limbs.

Those āsanas that were adopted by the sages headed by Vasiṣṭha and by the yogis headed by Matsyendra, I shall now — at least some of them — describe.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.17 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The reading aṅgapāṭavam is attested among many of the early manuscripts, including the “alpha” group. Although this compound rarely appears in other yoga texts, a similar term, śarīrapāṭava, occurs in the Śivasaṃhitā (2.35) as one of the benefits bestowed by digestive fire (vaiśvānarāgni), which indicates that the word pāṭava was used in relation to the body and the benefits of yoga. The compound aṅgapāṭava seems to imply the optimal functioning of the body. The variant reading, aṅgalāghava (‘lightness of the limbs’ or ‘dexterity’) is more common in yoga texts and similar formulations occur even in works known to Svātmārāma, such as the Dattātreyayogaśāstra (śarīralaghutā) and the Amanaska ([...] laghutvaṃ ca śarīrasyopajāyate). It appears that the less common term aṅgapāṭavam was changed to the more widely used notion of aṅgalāghava, perhaps early in the transmission, as the latter is attested by manuscripts in several early groups (that is “gamma”, “delta”, and “pi”).

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.18 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

On the historical implications of these two traditions of postural practice in early Haṭhayoga, see Mallinson 2016: 119–122 and Birch 2018a: 45–46.

Literal translation

Eighty-four āsanas were taught by Śiva; from them, taking a four, the essential [ones] I tell. Siddha, padma, siṃha and bhadra — this four is the best; and among them, O friend, always stay in Siddhāsana.

Literary translation

Śiva proclaimed eighty-four āsanas. From them I have chosen four principal ones and shall describe them. The four [poses] — siddha, padma, siṃha and bhadra — are held to be the best. And above them all, O friend, stands Siddhāsana: in it, remain always.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.33 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The word tu is often used to introduce a new posture, but in this case seems to be a verse filler. In the first and third verse quarters, Svātmārāma appears to have rewritten Śivasaṃhitā 3.96 to include the information that it was Śiva (śivena) who taught the eighty-four āsanas, whereas in the source Śiva is himself speaking. Svātmārāma also changes the meaning of the second half of the verse, as the Śivasaṃhitā states that Śiva picked out the four best postures and taught them, whereas in the Haṭhapradīpikā it reads as though Svātmārāma himself is responsible for picking out the four best postures and teaching them. There are other instances in the Haṭhapradīpikā where Svātmārāma borrows a verse with a first person verb (for example 3.43). However, in this instance, he may have intended to indicate that he chose the four postures coming after this verse (that is siddha, padma, siṃha and bhadra) because the Śivasaṃhitā follows 3.96 with teachings on the postures called siddha, padma, paścimottāna and svastika. The vocative here (sakhe) is also found in Haṭhapradīpikā 4.56, which is also likely to be an authorial verse.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.34 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

It is likely that the original version of this verse contained the vocative with the imperative form of the verb (sakhe tiṣṭha). There are other instances where Svātmārāma included a verse with the vocative (for example 4.7c, 4.56b, 4.72d, 4.74c) as though the text were a dialogue. Other versions of this verse are transmitted by some manuscripts of the Haṭhapradīpikā, in which the vocative and imperative verb have been removed. In these cases, sukhe and sukham are difficult to construe because the context suggests that the intended meaning was that one should always sit in siddhāsana (as opposed to the other three āsanas), rather than the prescription to always sit in a comfortable siddhāsana.

Literal translation

Thus, O lord of yogis, having overcome fatigue in āsanas and locks, one should then practise the purification of the channels — breath practices with mudrās and the rest.

Āsana, manifold breath-retention, the technique called mudrā, then attending to the sound — such is the order of practice in Haṭha [yoga].

Literary translation

Thus, having mastered the āsanas and the locks and overcome fatigue, the lord of yogis takes up the purification of the channels — the breath practices with mudrās and the rest.

First āsana, then manifold kumbhakas, then the “doing” called mudrā, and finally listening to the [inner] nāda — such is the order of practice in Haṭha yoga.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.55 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The second line can be interpreted in different ways. One possibility is to understand nāḍiśuddhiṃ as a bahuvrīhi qualifying mudrādipavanakriyām in the sense that the yogi should practise the breathing techniques by way of the relevant mudrās and locks (taught in the third chapter), from which purification of the channels arise. Alternatively, one could separate mudrādi from pavanakriyām and understand three different techniques here, namely, the practice of purifying the channels (perhaps by the alternative nostril method mentioned at the beginning of the second chapter), the mudrās and the breathing techniques of prāṇāyāma. The absence of a conjunctive particle, such as ca, makes the second interpretation less likely. The version of this verse in the Yogacintāmaṇi (see the testimonia) was changed to make it clear that nāḍiśuddhi and pavanakriyā with mudrās are two distinct things.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.56 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

This verse was omitted from Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī, the oldest dated manuscript. The omission is probably deliberate as that manuscript does not have chapter four, which teaches nādānusandhāna and the numbering in Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī indicates that its exemplar included the verse. This verse is similar to 1.65, which has mudrādikaraṇāni ca, so perhaps it was through confusion with 1.65 that the same reading is found in some witnesses of 1.56. It seems that each of the four auxiliaries (aṅga) of Haṭhayoga is being referred to in the singular (hence āsanaṃ), whereas in 1.65 the plural is used (that is pīṭhāni) which is why we have adopted the reading citraṃ [...] karaṇaṃ tathā.
