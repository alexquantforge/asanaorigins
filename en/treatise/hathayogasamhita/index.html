The Haṭhayogasaṃhitā (17th Century): A Probable Precursor to the Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā

Haṭha yoga · the Hindi edition: Śrī Bhārata Dharma Mahāmaṇḍala, Vārāṇasī · edited by Yogirāja Svāmī Jñānānanda Ji Mahārāja

Translation of the Āsana Section

Introduction

In both academic and practical discussions, Haṭhayoga is traditionally associated with the “great three” texts: the Haṭhapradīpikā, Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā and Śivasaṃhitā. These works have long been treated as a comprehensive account of the yoga canon of the late medieval period. Recent philological research, however, shows that they form only part of a much larger manuscript tradition, many links of which remain little studied.

This article translates the section on āsanas from the rare Haṭhayogasaṃhitā. The translation is based on the Hindi edition published in Vārāṇasī by Śrī Bhārata Dharma Mahāmaṇḍala and edited by Yogirāja Svāmī Jñānānanda Ji Mahārāja. The text’s structure and content support the hypothesis that it represents a version of a work that likely served as a direct basis for the celebrated Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā.

The Missing Link: Jason Birch’s Research

A defining feature of later yoga, particularly the tradition of Gheraṇḍa, is the structure of Saptāṅgayoga, or seven-limbed yoga, in which practice begins with the purificatory actions (ṣaṭkarma) rather than with āsanas or yama and niyama. The origins of this system were previously not fully understood.

In “Haṭhayoga’s Floruit on the Eve of Colonialism” (2020), the Indologist and yoga historian Jason Birch discusses the seventeenth-century Haṭhayogasaṃhitā. According to Birch, the text predates the Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā and appears to have provided the basis for that influential compendium.

The following description is translated back into English from the Russian rendering in this article; it is not presented as a verbatim quotation of Birch’s English. For the original wording, see Birch, “Haṭhayoga’s Floruit on the Eve of Colonialism,” page 458, note 17. Read Birch’s original chapter

The Haṭhayogasaṃhitā is a compilation that borrows extensively from the Haṭhapradīpikā. Its opening verses (1.2–3) acknowledge seven sages—Mārkaṇḍeya, Bharadvāja, Marīci, Jaimini, Parāśara, Bhṛgu and Viśvāmitra—for spreading Haṭhayoga throughout the world.

The stated aims of Haṭhayoga are purification (śodhanaṃ), firmness (dṛḍhatā), steadiness (sthairya), constancy (dhairya), lightness (lāghava), direct perception (pratyakṣa) and liberation (nirlipta) of the body (ghaṭa).

Its Haṭhayoga has seven auxiliary practices: ṣaṭkarma (six purificatory actions), āsana (posture), mudrā (seal), pratyāhāra (withdrawal of the senses), prāṇasaṃyāma (breath control), dhyāna (meditation) and samādhi (absorption).

The Haṭhayogasaṃhitā appears to have provided the basis for the eighteenth-century Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā, which adds a new frame narrative—a dialogue between the teacher Gheraṇḍa and the student Caṇḍakāpāli—along with several elaborate visualisation practices and a six-stage Rājayoga. The Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā calls its yoga ghaṭasthayoga, omits the Haṭhayogasaṃhitā’s teaching on vajrolī and redefines this mudrā as a handstand, suggesting reluctance to adopt transgressive Haṭhayoga practices.

Textual Similarities and the Edition Used

The edition of Svāmī Jñānānanda Ji, which served as the source for this translation, shows significant structural similarities to Birch's description. The table of contents of the book strictly follows the logic of Saptāṅgayoga:

  • Ṣaṭkarma - the text opens with a section on purification, which is a characteristic marker of this lineage (unlike Haṭhapradīpikā, which begins with āsanas).
  • Āsana - follows and includes a classic set of poses.
  • Mudra - section contains 25 mudras. It is important to note that the table of contents of this edition contains Vajrolī mudrā (item 11 in the list of mudras), which correlates with Birch's observation that the original Haṭhayogasaṃhitā contained this teaching before its modification in Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā.
  • This is followed by the sections Pratyāhāra, Prāṇāyāma, Dhyāna and Samādhi.

The combination of these factors allows us to assume with a high degree of probability that the published translation gives the reader access to a text close to the “proto-version” of one of the most influential schools of yoga. This provides a unique opportunity to look at the formation of the canon of āsanas in the period preceding its final codification in the 18th century.

Conclusion

The presented translation of the Āsana section from “Haṭhayogasaṃhitā” allows us to take a deeper look at the evolution of the physical practice of yoga in the period preceding its widespread popularization. Textual parallels between this edition and Jason Birch's description of a 17th-century manuscript suggest that the text in question is very likely a continuation of the very lineage that formed the basis of the Gheraṇḍasaṃhitā.

The obvious structural similarity (the Saptāṅgayoga system) and the specificity of the set of practices confirm the significance of “Haṭhayogasaṃhitā” as a connecting link in the history of yoga. Access to detailed descriptions of āsanas from this source helps fill gaps in the understanding of how the canon of poses was formed before its final fixation in the 18th century. We hope that this material will serve as an incentive for further comparative analysis and study of the primary sources of the Haṭhayoga tradition.
