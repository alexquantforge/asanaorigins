A Description of the Āsanas in Chapter 31 of the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā

Author: Alexander Vijaya (t.me/alexvijaya)

Pāñcarātra · Adyar Library Series 4 · Adyar, Madras, 1916 · volume II, pages 293–296

Preface

This article presents a translation and word-by-word analysis of the eleven principal āsanas listed in chapter 31 of the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā, a work of the Pāñcarātra tradition. It was prepared from the supplied file Ahirbudhnya_samhita.txt and checked against the printed text on pages 293–296 of volume II of the 1916 edition.

Editorial policy: Readings clearly transmitted in the edition are translated without adding details from modern descriptions of poses with the same names. Unclear passages are not silently emended: they are marked with a question mark, square brackets, or an explanation. Where the transcription file contains an evident OCR error, the reading has been checked against the printed page; for example, the Kūrmāsana verse prints निपीड्य (nipīḍya), not the erroneous OCR reading निपीय.

The translation distinguishes three levels: the Sanskrit reading, a literal translation, and a cautious literary translation. Where the Sanskrit does not permit the body’s position to be reconstructed unambiguously, this is stated explicitly. Claims about the benefits of poses are presented as statements made by the text, not as verified medical information.

The 1916 edition provides manuscript variants alongside the text. The main printed reading is therefore retained below, with variants relevant to this passage noted separately.

Dating and Probable Place of Composition

The date of the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā is disputed; the dates of the manuscripts used for the printed edition do not in themselves establish the date of composition.

In the section “Provenience and Age” of his introduction, F. Otto Schrader associated the text with Kashmir. Among the arguments he adduces are the mention of yantra diagrams on birch bark in chapters 26 and 45; the image in chapter 39 of the sun emerging from an obstacle of snow; and the account of Muktāpīḍa in chapter 48. Schrader stressed that little can be said with certainty about the text’s age: he placed it in a group of Saṃhitās for which he proposed the eighth century CE as an upper limit. As a possible lower limit, he considered the text’s presentation of Buddhism as divided into three major schools; in Schrader’s view, this implies a date after the establishment of Mahāyāna, approximately after 300 CE. These are Schrader’s dating arguments, not an established date for the text. See the sections of his introduction on the manuscripts, provenance, and age, pages 94–99 6.

In a 2017 article, D. Wujastyk proposes a date after 1000 CE, and possibly South India, for the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā 5. Marion Rastelli’s 2018 article abstract likewise associates the text’s composition with South India, narrowing the hypothesis to the twelfth–thirteenth centuries 3. These later proposals differ substantially from Schrader’s earlier dating framework; without additional evidence, they cannot be collapsed into one precise date or presented as an agreed consensus. Accordingly, this article does not assign a date to the āsana section itself.

The Manuscripts Used by the Editor

According to Schrader’s introduction, over the three years spent preparing the edition, he was able to obtain “six (nine)” manuscripts. The editor noted that all descend from a single already-corrupt archetype, although he considered the text of the Saṃhitā generally well preserved. The introduction does not assign the manuscripts one absolute date; Schrader speaks primarily of their relative age and quality.

Of the two witnesses he identifies as oldest and best, E is a Grantha-script manuscript from Kalale in Mysore; D is a Malayalam-script manuscript belonging to the Mahārāja of Travancore. Schrader judges E to be more accurate than D. Several manuscripts from Melkote, descendants of E and almost identical to one another, were grouped by the editor under the common siglum F. Among the other witnesses he describes are a palm-leaf Grantha manuscript at the Adyar Library (A), a paper Grantha manuscript from the same library with large omissions (C), and a Telugu-script manuscript belonging to the Government of Mysore (B). He did not include a severely damaged manuscript from Tanjore.

Thus, this passage is not based on a single manuscript: the printed apparatus cites sigla for several witnesses. At the same time, according to Schrader, these witnesses are not fully independent, since they descend from a common corrupt source. Manuscript details are from Schrader’s introduction, pages 94–95 6.

The Chapter and Its Numbering

Chapter 31 is entitled in the colophon योगाङ्गयमनियमासननिरूपणम् — “Exposition of the limbs of yoga, yama, niyama, and āsana.” The supplied excerpt does not begin at the chapter’s opening: it continues a discussion of yama and niyama, then describes the āsanas and moves on to the purification of nāḍīs (bodily channels) before prāṇāyāma. In Schrader’s synopsis, from 31.14 to the end of chapter 32 the text presents the eight limbs of yoga: ten yamas, ten niyamas, eleven principal āsanas, prāṇāyāma with an introduction to the nāḍīs and the ten bodily vāyus, and a three-month purification of the nāḍī system; the remaining limbs follow. This is provided here only as context: the adjacent sections are not translated in full 6. The appendix includes the concluding lines on the nāḍīs and the colophon.

The text lists eleven principal āsanas: Cakra, Padma, Kūrma, Mayūra, Kukkuṭa, Vīra, Svastika, Bhadra, Siṃha, Mukta, and Gomukha. This is the list given by the Saṃhitā; it does not mean that the work claims there are only eleven poses.

Printed verse numbers have been retained wherever legible. Some numbers are not recognized in the supplied transcription. In particular, the Mayūrāsana passage is treated as verses 36–37; the Kukkuṭāsana passage falls between 37 and 39; and the Muktāsana description continues to half of verse 45. D. Wujastyk marks Muktāsana as 44–45ab and Gomukhāsana as 45cd–46; that division is followed here. Thus, in the printed layout, the heading for Gomukhāsana falls in the middle of verse 45; the text is not rearranged editorially to remove this feature 5.
