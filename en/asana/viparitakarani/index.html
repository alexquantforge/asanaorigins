Dating of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra (around 1200 CE / 13th Century): Philological, Historical, and Stemmatic Evidence

The Dattātreyayogaśāstra («Dattātreyayogaśāstra») holds a singular place in the history of Indian thought as the earliest surviving Sanskrit treatise to systematically teach physical techniques under the name of Haṭhayoga and to integrate Patañjali’s eight-limbed system (aṣṭāṅgayoga) into an overarching hierarchy of four yogas.

In early 20th-century Indology, estimates of the text’s date fluctuated widely between the 13th and 15th centuries (J.N. Farquhar, P.K. Gode, H.D. Sharma, C. Bouy). However, the philological discoveries of the Haṭha Yoga Project (SOAS University of London / Oxford) and James Mallinson’s 2024 critical edition (Collection Indologie, number 162, École française d’Extrême-Orient & IFP) have definitively anchored the text: the Dattātreyayogaśāstra was composed in about 1200 CE (late 12th to early 13th century, around 1200–1250 CE).

Below is an exhaustive breakdown of the philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence supporting this dating.

1. The Upper Bound (Terminus Ante Quem: 1363 CE)

The upper chronological limit is firmly secured by external textual witnesses:

  1. The Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE): The anthological compendium of Śārṅgadhara is explicitly dated to 1420 «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» (1363 CE) in its manuscript colophons. In chapters 157 and 158, Śārṅgadhara borrows directly from the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra»:
  • «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verse 35 (Padmāsana) is quoted verbatim twice («Śārṅgadharapaddhati» verses 4376 & 4435: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam //);
  • «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verses 131–145 are borrowed almost word-for-word, including prāṇāyāma and five cardinal mudrās (Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandhara, Uḍḍīyāna, and Mūlabandha);
  • This proves that by 1363 CE, the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» was already widely renowned and circulated as far north as Rajasthan.
  1. Svātmārāma’s Haṭhapradīpikā (around 1400–1450 CE): Svātmārāma constructed large portions of the Haṭhapradīpikā directly from the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra»:
  • «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verses 34–38 (Padmāsana) became «Haṭhapradīpikā» 1.45–47;

2. The Lower Bound (Terminus Post Quem: around 1150–1200 CE)

The lower chronological limit is established by the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra»’s relationship with earlier yogic and tantric corpora:

  1. Reception and De-Buddhicisation of the Amṛtasiddhi (11th–12th century):
  • The Amṛtasiddhi is the earliest surviving Sanskrit text to introduce the somatic physiology of bindu, the sun in the abdomen, the moon in the palate, and the three basic physical locks (Mahāmudrā, Mahābandha, Mahāvedha). As shown by Mallinson and Szántó (2021), the Amṛtasiddhi originated within a Buddhist Tantric (Mahāyāna/Vajrayāna) environment.
  • The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» adopts this somatic system of bindu preservation and physical seals, but thoroughly adapts it to a Purāṇic Hindu and Vaiṣṇava framework, subordinating it to the worship of Viṣṇu/Hari and the sage Kapila. The assimilation and theological translation of a Buddhist tantric text into mainstream Brahmanical yoga required several decades, placing the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» no earlier than the late 12th century.
  1. Systematisation of the Fourfold Yoga of the Amaraughaprabodha (12th century):
  • The Amaraughaprabodha, attributed to Gorakṣanātha, first briefly enunciated the fourfold hierarchy of Mantrayoga, Layayoga, Haṭhayoga, and Rājayoga.
  • The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» is the first text in history to systematically furnish each of these four yogas with comprehensive, practical instructions.
  1. The Earliest Textual Appearance of Vajrolīmudrā:
  • Neither the Amṛtasiddhi nor the 12th-century Vivekamārtaṇḍa / Gorakṣaśataka knows the practice of vajrolī. The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» is the oldest surviving text to describe the physical absorption of sexual fluids and the subsidiary practices of sahajolī and amarolī (verses 150–178), marking a crucial historical development in early 13th-century yoga.

3. Stemmatic and Paleographical Evidence: Two Divergent Manuscript Branches

A pivotal argument for the early date of around 1200 CE, demonstrated by James Mallinson in the 2024 critical edition, rests on manuscript stemmatics:

  • The transmission of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» is split into two deeply bifurcated recensions: 1. The Southern Recension ( ): σ Preserved in ancient palm-leaf codices in Grantha script (repositories in Chennai, Mysore, Thanjavur, and Pondicherry). This branch preserves the more archaic, complete text of 193 verses, including explicit instructions for women practitioners of vajrolī (strī-vajrolī). 2. The Northern/Western Recension ($ u$): Preserved in paper manuscripts in Devanāgarī script (Jodhpur, Baroda, Vārāṇasī). In this branch, the text was abridged to 169–173 verses, and the explicit sexual-somatic verses were excised or softened.
  • In textual criticism, the geographic migration of a text from the Deccan/South to Rajasthan and Vārāṇasī, across different writing media (palm-leaf to paper) and scripts (Grantha to Devanāgarī), accompanied by deep textual divergence and conscious editorial bowdlerisation prior to 1363 CE, demands a developmental span of at least 100 to 150 years of scribal transmission.

4. Historical and Geographical Context: The Deccan and the Dattātreya Cult

  1. Provenance: Mallinson demonstrates that the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» was composed on the Deccan plateau (modern Maharashtra and neighbouring areas of Karnataka and Telangana).
  2. The Rise of Dattātreya Worship in the 12th–13th Centuries:
  • In first-millennium Purāṇic literature, Dattātreya is primarily an ancient sage (ṛṣi), the son of Atri and Anasūyā.
  • During the late 12th and 13th centuries, under the Seuna/Yādava dynasty of Devagiri in the Deccan, Dattātreya became the centre of an intense religious movement. He was adopted as a supreme divine manifestation by the Mahānubhāva-panth (founded by Cakradhara in the mid-13th century) and by the early Marathi Nāths (Jñāneśvar in his 1290 Jñāneśvarī lauds the lineage of Dattātreya and Gorakṣa).
  • This matches the concluding verse of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» (verse 193): harir eva svayaṃ sākṣād dattātreyo ’bhavat purā (“Hari [Viṣṇu] Himself directly became incarnate as Dattātreya”).
  1. Radical Egalitarianism (Verses 41–44): The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» famously declares that anyone, regardless of caste, sex, or religious identity, can succeed in yoga:

brāhmaṇaḥ śramaṇo vāpi bauddho vāpy ārhato ’thavā / kāpāliko vā cārvākaḥ śraddhayā sahitaḥ sudhīḥ // 41 //

“Whether a Brahmin, an ascetic (śramaṇa), a Buddhist, a Jain (ārhata), a Kāpālika, or even a materialist (Cārvāka)—if wise and endowed with faith, one attains all perfections in yoga.” This inclusive, non-sectarian milieu is hallmark of early 13th-century Deccan asceticism prior to the hardening of sectarian orthodoxy.

Conclusion

The convergence of philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence firmly dates the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra to circa 1200 CE (early 13th century).

This early date cements the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» as the foundational root text of the entire Haṭhayoga tradition, providing the direct textual and somatic archetype from which both the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) and the Haṭhapradīpikā (around 1400–1450 CE) drew their core teachings.

Excursus: Textual and Historical Evidence for the Dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) as an Absolute Terminus Ante Quem for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

The dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati («Śārṅgadharapaddhati») to 1363 CE is one of the most firmly established and methodologically robust chronological benchmarks in Sanskrit philology and the history of early Haṭhayoga. Because Śārṅgadhara borrowed verbatim into his anthology the definition of Padmāsana (verses 4376 & 4435 = «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verse 35), the techniques of channel purification and prāṇāyāma, and five cardinal mudrās (verses 4408–4417 = «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verses 131–145: Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandharabandha, Uḍḍīyānabandha, and Mūlabandha), the precise date of his compendium provides an incontrovertible upper limit (terminus ante quem) by which time the Dattātreyayogaśāstra must have already been in circulation.

The dating of 1363 CE is grounded in the convergence of three mutually independent bodies of historical and textual evidence (Aufrecht 1873; Peterson 1888; Keith 1928; Winternitz 1936; Sternbach 1974; Mallinson 2011, 2024; Birch 2013):

1. The Colophons and the Vikrama Era (1420 «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»)

In the scribal colophons of the earliest extant manuscripts (first systematically collated by Theodor Aufrecht in 1873 from Oxford and Berlin codices and corroborated by Peter Peterson in 1888 from the Government Collection in Pune), the date of composition is recorded as:

𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑣𝑎𝑡 1420 𝑣𝑎𝑟ṣ𝑒 ... śā𝑟ṅ𝑔𝑎𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒ṇ𝑎 𝑝𝑎𝑑𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑡𝑖ḥ 𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑘ṛ𝑡ā (“In the year 1420 of the [Vikrama] Samvat era, this paddhati was compiled by Śārṅgadhara”).

  • The Vikrama Era (Vikrama Saṃvat): Traditional northern Indian epoch commencing in 57 BCE.
  • Astronomical and calendar conversion: 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑚𝑜𝑛 𝐸𝑟𝑎 (𝐶𝐸) = 1420 −57 = 1363 𝐶𝐸

Depending on whether the regional lunar year began in Caitra (spring, March/April) or Kārttika (autumn, October/November, common in Rajasthan and Gujarat), the year spans 1363–1364 CE. Indologists universally cite 1363 CE as the standard reference date.

2. The Internal Genealogy of the Author (Verses 1.2–1.6)

The veracity of this colophon date is fully corroborated by the author’s own explicit autobiographical account in the opening section of the work (Kavi-vaṃśa-varṇana, Chapter 1, verses 1–12), which traces his lineage through three generations:

3. Historical Synchronism: King Hammīradeva of Raṇastambhapura (1282–1301 CE)

King Hammīra is a major, precisely documented figure of medieval Indian history: Hammīradeva Cāhamāna (Hammīr Dev), son of Jaitrasiṃha, the last great sovereign of the fortress of Raṇastambhapura (modern Ranthambore, Rajasthan): 1. Accession: Hammīradeva ascended the throne in 1282/1283 CE (1339 «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»), as recorded in Nayacandra Sūri’s historical epic Hammīra-mahākāvya (VIII.54) and Rājaśekhara Sūri’s Prabandhakośa (1349 CE). 2. The Siege of Ranthambore and Death (July 1301 CE): Hammīradeva refused to surrender rebel Mongol generals who had sought asylum with him, prompting Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi to mount a massive siege. Following the betrayal of generals Ratipāla and Raṇamalla and the performance of jauhar (mass self-immolation) by the Rajput women, Hammīra died fighting in the final sally on July 11, 1301 CE (3 Dhū al-Qa‘dah 700 AH). This event is documented to the exact day in contemporaneous Persian court chronicles by Amīr Khusrau (Khazā’in al-Futūḥ, 1311 CE) and Diyā’ al-Dīn Baranī (Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī). 3. Generational Chronology:

  • Generation I (Grandfather): Rāghavadeva was active as royal preceptor (rājaguru) during Hammīra’s floruit: around 1285–1301 CE.
  • Generation II (Father): Dāmodara was active in the subsequent generation: around 1310–1335 CE.
  • Generation III (Grandson/Author): Śārṅgadhara, born around 1315–1320 CE, compiled his 163-chapter anthology in full maturity by 1363 CE.
  • The 62-year span between Hammīra’s death (1301 CE) and the completion of the compendium (1363 CE) is a perfect historical match for two generational successions. 4. Court Archival Continuity: In Chapter 140 (Vīrarasa-nirvedaḥ, verse 3974), Śārṅgadhara quotes a stanza attributed directly to śrī-hammīrasya (“By King Hammīra”), uttered before his final heroic charge, proving that the family preserved authentic court and literary papers from the Cāhamāna dynasty.

4. Textual Boundaries (Terminus Post Quem and Ante Quem)

  • Terminus post quem: The anthology cites numerous late 13th-century poets, but contains zero references to 15th-century authors, Timurid invasions, or Sultanate developments after the mid-14th century.
  • Terminus ante quem: The Śārṅgadharapaddhati itself was heavily mined by 15th-century anthologists, most notably by Vallabhadeva in Kashmir for the Subhāṣitāvalī, which reproduces hundreds of «Śārṅgadharapaddhati» verses along with their author attributions. Early paper manuscripts of «Śārṅgadharapaddhati» survive from the 15th and 16th centuries (such as Royal Asiatic Society London MS Tod 32).

5. Critical Significance for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

Because the Dattātreyayogaśāstra lacks an explicit internal date, modern scholars rely on this external synchronism: 1. In chapters 157 and 158 of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE), Śārṅgadhara quotes «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verse 35 (Padmāsana) verbatim: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam // («Śārṅgadharapaddhati» verses 4376 & 4435); 2. He incorporates five of the ten mudrās of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» (verses 4408–4417 = «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verses 131–145); 3. He adopts the distinctive prāṇāyāma methodology of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra».

Conclusion: The date 1363 CE provides an absolute, historically verified terminus ante quem for the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra. This firmly establishes that the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» was composed in the 13th century (around 1200–1300 CE), well over a century before the Haṭhapradīpikā (around 1400–1450 CE), solidifying its position as the earliest surviving systematic root text of Haṭhayoga.

Editors’ commentary

  1. The internal alchemy of the sun and moon: In early Haṭhayoga physiology (first formulated in the Amṛtasiddhi and systematized here in the Dattātreyayogaśāstra), the body is governed by an inverted cosmic axis:
  • The Moon (candra) resides in the palate/head, constantly secreting the immortal nectar of life (bindu / amṛta).
  • The Sun (sūrya / agni) resides in the navel/belly, its upward-reaching flames devouring this falling nectar, which leads to physical decay, graying, wrinkling, and death. By physically standing on the head/shoulders (adhaḥśiraś cordhvapādaḥ), the spatial relation is literally inverted: the moon is now below and the sun is above. Gravity prevents the nectar from falling into the fire, allowing the practitioner to preserve bindu and achieve bodily rejuvenation.
  1. The ferocious blaze of the digestive fire: Because the downward drain of nectar is stopped and the solar fire in the abdomen is fanned, the metabolic furnace blazes intensely. Dattātreya specifically warns his disciple Sāṃkṛti that the yogin must consume substantial food (āhāro bahulaḥ). If insufficient fuel is ingested, the body enters auto-catabolism (agnir dāhaṃ karoti).
  2. From Mudrā to modern Āsana: In the Dattātreyayogaśāstra, Viparītakaraṇī is classified as a mudrā (or karaṇa) because its purpose is energetic: the seal/lock and reversal of fluids. In later centuries, this technique evolved directly into what modern hatha and postural yoga calls Viparīta Karaṇī Āsana and forms the conceptual root of inverted postures such as Śīrṣāsana (headstand) and Sarvāṅgāsana (shoulderstand).

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, number 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

But the picture is already clear: the earliest dated copies are 1496 (30109), 1626/27 (Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur, manuscript 2230), 1636/37 (29899), 1650/51 (30069), 1667/68 (Jodhpur 23709), 1745/46 (Jodhpur 02237).

Literal translation

The navel up, the palate down, the sun above, the moon below — [this] position, called “inverted”, is obtained by the guru’s words. [This] position, called “inverted”, destroys all diseases, in one who practises constantly it increases the fire in the belly. For the practitioner plentiful food is to be provided; if there is little food, the fire will burn the body in an instant. With the head down, with the feet up, let there be a moment on the first day, and let him practise a little more day after day. Wrinkles and grey hair are not seen after six months; he who practises constantly for [just] one yāma, he is a conqueror of time.

Literary translation

Viparītakaraṇī, the “inverted” position: the navel is raised and the palate lowered, the sun comes to be above and the moon below; this bodily position is learned from the teaching of a guru. It drives away all diseases and kindles the digestive fire in one who practises without fail. The practitioner needs plentiful food: on a meagre diet the fire will burn up the body in an instant. On the first day one holds the head down and the feet up for a moment only, adding a little each day. In six months neither wrinkles nor grey hair remain, and whoever practises for three hours every day conquers death.
