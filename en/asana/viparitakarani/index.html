Dating of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra (c. 1200 CE / 13th Century): Philological, Historical, and Stemmatic Evidence

The Dattātreyayogaśāstra (DYS) holds a singular place in the history of Indian thought as the earliest surviving Sanskrit treatise to systematically teach physical techniques under the name of Haṭhayoga and to integrate Patañjali’s eight-limbed system (aṣṭāṅgayoga) into an overarching hierarchy of four yogas.

In early 20th-century Indology, estimates of the text’s date fluctuated widely between the 13th and 15th centuries (J.N. Farquhar, P.K. Gode, H.D. Sharma, C. Bouy). However, the philological discoveries of the Haṭha Yoga Project (SOAS University of London / Oxford) and James Mallinson’s 2024 critical edition (Collection Indologie, n° 162, EFEO & IFP) have definitively anchored the text: the Dattātreyayogaśāstra was composed in about 1200 CE (late 12th to early 13th century, c. 1200–1250 CE).

Below is an exhaustive breakdown of the philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence supporting this dating.

1. The Upper Bound (Terminus Ante Quem: 1363 CE)

The upper chronological limit is firmly secured by external textual witnesses:

  1. The Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE): The anthological compendium of Śārṅgadhara is explicitly dated to 1420 VS (1363 CE) in its manuscript colophons. In chapters 157 and 158, Śārṅgadhara borrows directly from the DYS:
  • DYS verse 35 (Padmāsana) is quoted verbatim twice (ŚP vv. 4376 & 4435: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam //);
  • DYS verses 131–145 are borrowed almost word-for-word, including prāṇāyāma and five cardinal mudrās (Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandhara, Uḍḍīyāna, and Mūlabandha);
  • This proves that by 1363 CE, the DYS was already widely renowned and circulated as far north as Rajasthan.
  1. Svātmārāma’s Haṭhapradīpikā (c. 1400–1450 CE): Svātmārāma constructed large portions of the Haṭhapradīpikā directly from the DYS:
  • DYS verses 34–38 (Padmāsana) became HP 1.45–47;
  • DYS verses 146–149 (Viparītakaraṇī) became HP 3.79–82;
  • Verses on channel purification (nāḍīśodhana), signs of success, and dietary rules (mitāhāra) repeat DYS stanzas verbatim.
  1. The Śivasaṃhitā (14th–15th c.) and Yogabīja (14th c.): Both texts show direct familiarity with the formulations of the DYS, adapting its somatic practices into a Śaiva Vedāntic framework.

2. The Lower Bound (Terminus Post Quem: c. 1150–1200 CE)

The lower chronological limit is established by the DYS’s relationship with earlier yogic and tantric corpora:

  1. Reception and De-Buddhicisation of the Amṛtasiddhi (11th–12th c.):
  • The Amṛtasiddhi is the earliest surviving Sanskrit text to introduce the somatic physiology of bindu, the sun in the abdomen, the moon in the palate, and the three basic physical locks (Mahāmudrā, Mahābandha, Mahāvedha). As shown by Mallinson and Szántó (2021), the Amṛtasiddhi originated within a Buddhist Tantric (Mahāyāna/Vajrayāna) environment.
  • The DYS adopts this somatic system of bindu preservation and physical seals, but thoroughly adapts it to a Purāṇic Hindu and Vaiṣṇava framework, subordinating it to the worship of Viṣṇu/Hari and the sage Kapila. The assimilation and theological translation of a Buddhist tantric text into mainstream Brahmanical yoga required several decades, placing the DYS no earlier than the late 12th century.
  1. Systematisation of the Fourfold Yoga of the Amaraughaprabodha (12th c.):
  • The Amaraughaprabodha, attributed to Gorakṣanātha, first briefly enunciated the fourfold hierarchy of Mantrayoga, Layayoga, Haṭhayoga, and Rājayoga.
  • The DYS is the first text in history to systematically furnish each of these four yogas with comprehensive, practical instructions.
  1. The Earliest Textual Appearance of Vajrolīmudrā:
  • Neither the Amṛtasiddhi nor the 12th-century Vivekamārtaṇḍa / Gorakṣaśataka knows the practice of vajrolī. The DYS is the oldest surviving text to describe the physical absorption of sexual fluids and the subsidiary practices of sahajolī and amarolī (verses 150–178), marking a crucial historical development in early 13th-century yoga.

3. Stemmatic and Paleographical Evidence: Two Divergent Manuscript Branches

A pivotal argument for the early date of c. 1200 CE, demonstrated by James Mallinson in the 2024 critical edition, rests on manuscript stemmatics:

  • The transmission of the DYS is split into two deeply bifurcated recensions: 1. The Southern Recension ( ): σ Preserved in ancient palm-leaf codices in Grantha script (repositories in Chennai, Mysore, Thanjavur, and Pondicherry). This branch preserves the more archaic, complete text of 193 verses, including explicit instructions for women practitioners of vajrolī (strī-vajrolī). 2. The Northern/Western Recension ($ u$): Preserved in paper manuscripts in Devanāgarī script (Jodhpur, Baroda, Varanasi). In this branch, the text was abridged to 169–173 verses, and the explicit sexual-somatic verses were excised or softened.
  • In textual criticism, the geographic migration of a text from the Deccan/South to Rajasthan and Varanasi, across different writing media (palm-leaf to paper) and scripts (Grantha to Devanāgarī), accompanied by deep textual divergence and conscious editorial bowdlerisation prior to 1363 CE, demands a developmental span of at least 100 to 150 years of scribal transmission.

4. Historical and Geographical Context: The Deccan and the Dattātreya Cult

  1. Provenance: Mallinson demonstrates that the DYS was composed on the Deccan plateau (modern Maharashtra and neighbouring areas of Karnataka and Telangana).
  2. The Rise of Dattātreya Worship in the 12th–13th Centuries:
  • In first-millennium Purāṇic literature, Dattātreya is primarily an ancient sage (ṛṣi), the son of Atri and Anasūyā.
  • During the late 12th and 13th centuries, under the Seuna/Yādava dynasty of Devagiri in the Deccan, Dattātreya became the centre of an intense religious movement. He was adopted as a supreme divine manifestation by the Mahānubhāva-panth (founded by Cakradhara in the mid-13th century) and by the early Marathi Nāths (Jñāneśvar in his 1290 Jñāneśvarī lauds the lineage of Dattātreya and Gorakṣa).
  • This matches the concluding verse of the DYS (v. 193): harir eva svayaṃ sākṣād dattātreyo ’bhavat purā (“Hari [Viṣṇu] Himself directly became incarnate as Dattātreya”).
  1. Radical Egalitarianism (Verses 41–44): The DYS famously declares that anyone, regardless of caste, sex, or religious identity, can succeed in yoga:

brāhmaṇaḥ śramaṇo vāpi bauddho vāpy ārhato ’thavā / kāpāliko vā cārvākaḥ śraddhayā sahitaḥ sudhīḥ // 41 //

“Whether a Brahmin, an ascetic (śramaṇa), a Buddhist, a Jain (ārhata), a Kāpālika, or even a materialist (Cārvāka)—if wise and endowed with faith, one attains all perfections in yoga.” This inclusive, non-sectarian milieu is hallmark of early 13th-century Deccan asceticism prior to the hardening of sectarian orthodoxy.

Conclusion

The convergence of philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence firmly dates the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra to circa 1200 CE (early 13th century).

This early date cements the DYS as the foundational root text of the entire Haṭhayoga tradition, providing the direct textual and somatic archetype from which both the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) and the Haṭhapradīpikā (c. 1400–1450 CE) drew their core teachings.

Excursus: Textual and Historical Evidence for the Dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) as an Absolute Terminus Ante Quem for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

The dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (ŚP) to 1363 CE is one of the most firmly established and methodologically robust chronological benchmarks in Sanskrit philology and the history of early Haṭhayoga. Because Śārṅgadhara borrowed verbatim into his anthology the definition of Padmāsana (vv. 4376 & 4435 = DYS v. 35), the techniques of channel purification and prāṇāyāma, and five cardinal mudrās (vv. 4408–4417 = DYS vv. 131–145: Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandharabandha, Uḍḍīyānabandha, and Mūlabandha), the precise date of his compendium provides an incontrovertible upper limit (terminus ante quem) by which time the Dattātreyayogaśāstra must have already been in circulation.

The dating of 1363 CE is grounded in the convergence of three mutually independent bodies of historical and textual evidence (Aufrecht 1873; Peterson 1888; Keith 1928; Winternitz 1936; Sternbach 1974; Mallinson 2011, 2024; Birch 2013):

1. The Colophons and the Vikrama Era (1420 VS)

In the scribal colophons of the earliest extant manuscripts (first systematically collated by Theodor Aufrecht in 1873 from Oxford and Berlin codices and corroborated by Peter Peterson in 1888 from the Government Collection in Pune), the date of composition is recorded as:

𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑣𝑎𝑡 1420 𝑣𝑎𝑟ṣ𝑒 ... śā𝑟ṅ𝑔𝑎𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒ṇ𝑎 𝑝𝑎𝑑𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑡𝑖ḥ 𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑘ṛ𝑡ā (“In the year 1420 of the [Vikrama] Samvat era, this paddhati was compiled by Śārṅgadhara”).

  • The Vikrama Era (Vikrama Saṃvat): Traditional northern Indian epoch commencing in 57 BCE.
  • Astronomical and calendar conversion: 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑚𝑜𝑛 𝐸𝑟𝑎 (𝐶𝐸) = 1420 −57 = 1363 𝐶𝐸

Depending on whether the regional lunar year began in Caitra (spring, March/April) or Kārttika (autumn, October/November, common in Rajasthan and Gujarat), the year spans 1363–1364 CE. Indologists universally cite 1363 CE as the standard reference date.

2. The Internal Genealogy of the Author (Verses 1.2–1.6)

The veracity of this colophon date is fully corroborated by the author’s own explicit autobiographical account in the opening section of the work (Kavi-vaṃśa-varṇana, Chapter 1, vv. 1–12), which traces his lineage through three generations:

Verse 1.2: King Hammīra of the Cāhamāna Dynasty

पुरा शाकम्भरीदेशे श्रीमान्हम्मीरभूपतिः । चाहवाणान्वये जातः ख्यातः शौर्ये इवार्जुनः ॥ २ ॥

IAST: purā śākambharī-deśe śrīmān hammīra-bhūpatiḥ / cāhavāṇānvaye jātaḥ khyātaḥ śaurya ivārjunaḥ // 2 //

Literal Translation: “Formerly in the land of Śākambharī [eastern Rajasthan] there reigned the illustrious king Hammīra, born in the lineage of the Cāhavāṇas [Cāhamānas/Chauhans], renowned for his valour like Arjuna.”

Verse 1.3: The Grandfather Rāghavadeva (Royal Preceptor)

तस्याभवत्सभ्यजनेषु मुख्यः परोपकारव्यसनैकनिष्ठः । पुरंदरस्येव गुरुर्गरीयान्द्विजाग्रणी राघवदेवनामा ॥ ३ ॥

IAST: tasyābhavat sabhya-janeṣu mukhyaḥ paropakāra-vyasanaika-niṣṭhaḥ / puraṃdarasyeva gurur garīyān dvijāgraṇī rāghavadeva-nāmā // 3 //

Literal Translation: “Foremost among the members of his royal assembly was an eminent leader of the twice-born named Rāghavadeva, solely devoted to the welfare of others, who was as venerable a preceptor to the king as Bṛhaspati was to Puraṃdara [Indra].”

Verse 1.4: The Three Sons of Rāghavadeva

गोपालदामोदरदेवदाससंज्ञा बभूवुस्तनयास्तदीयाः । नेत्रावतारा इव चन्द्रमौलेरपाकृतध्वान्तगणाल्रयोऽपि ॥ ४ ॥

IAST: gopāla-dāmodara-devadāsa-saṃjñā babhūvus tanayās tadīyāḥ / netrāvatārā iva candramauler apākṛta-dhvānta-gaṇās trayo ’pi // 4 //

Literal Translation: “His sons were three, named Gopāla, Dāmodara, and Devadāsa, who, like the threefold eyes of the moon-crested Śiva, dispelled all darkness [of ignorance].”

Verse 1.5: The Father Dāmodara

तेषां मध्ये यस्तु दामोदरोऽभूदुत्पाद्य त्रीनात्मजान्वीतरागः । भागीरथ्यां शुद्धदेहं विधाय ज्ञानादात्मन्येव निष्ठां जगाम ॥ ५ ॥

IAST: teṣāṃ madhye yas tu dāmodaro ’bhūd utpādya trīn ātmajān vītarāgaḥ / bhāgīrathyāṃ śuddha-dehaṃ vidhāya jñānād ātmany eva niṣṭhāṃ jagāma // 5 //

Literal Translation: “Among them, Dāmodara, having begotten three sons and become dispassionate towards worldly affairs, purified his mortal frame in the sacred waters of the Bhāgīrathī [Ganges] and, through spiritual knowledge, attained final repose in the Supreme Self.”

Verse 1.6: Śārṅgadhara and His Brothers

ज्येष्ठः शार्ङ्गधरस्तेषां लघुर्लक्ष्मीधरस्ततः । कृष्णोऽनुजस्ततस्तेषां त्रय एतेऽमितेजसः ॥ ६ ॥

IAST: jyeṣṭhaḥ śārṅgadharas teṣāṃ laghur lakṣmīdharas tataḥ / kṛṣṇo ’nujas tatas teṣāṃ traya ete ’mitejasaḥ // 6 //

Literal Translation: “The eldest among them was Śārṅgadhara; younger than him was Lakṣmīdhara; and thereafter was their youngest brother Kṛṣṇa: these three were endowed with immeasurable lustre.”

3. Historical Synchronism: King Hammīradeva of Raṇastambhapura (1282–1301 CE)

King Hammīra is a major, precisely documented figure of medieval Indian history: Hammīradeva Cāhamāna (Hammīr Dev), son of Jaitrasiṃha, the last great sovereign of the fortress of Raṇastambhapura (modern Ranthambore, Rajasthan): 1. Accession: Hammīradeva ascended the throne in 1282/1283 CE (1339 VS), as recorded in Nayacandra Sūri’s historical epic Hammīra-mahākāvya (VIII.54) and Rājaśekhara Sūri’s Prabandhakośa (1349 CE). 2. The Siege of Ranthambore and Death (July 1301 CE): Hammīradeva refused to surrender rebel Mongol generals who had sought asylum with him, prompting Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi to mount a massive siege. Following the betrayal of generals Ratipāla and Raṇamalla and the performance of jauhar (mass self-immolation) by the Rajput women, Hammīra died fighting in the final sally on July 11, 1301 CE (3 Dhū al-Qa‘dah 700 AH). This event is documented to the exact day in contemporaneous Persian court chronicles by Amīr Khusrau (Khazā’in al-Futūḥ, 1311 CE) and Diyā’ al-Dīn Baranī (Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī). 3. Generational Chronology:

  • Generation I (Grandfather): Rāghavadeva was active as royal preceptor (rājaguru) during Hammīra’s floruit: c. 1285–1301 CE.
  • Generation II (Father): Dāmodara was active in the subsequent generation: c. 1310–1335 CE.
  • Generation III (Grandson/Author): Śārṅgadhara, born c. 1315–1320 CE, compiled his 163-chapter anthology in full maturity by 1363 CE.
  • The 62-year span between Hammīra’s death (1301 CE) and the completion of the compendium (1363 CE) is a perfect historical match for two generational successions. 4. Court Archival Continuity: In Chapter 140 (Vīrarasa-nirvedaḥ, v. 3974), Śārṅgadhara quotes a stanza attributed directly to śrī-hammīrasya (“By King Hammīra”), uttered before his final heroic charge, proving that the family preserved authentic court and literary papers from the Cāhamāna dynasty.

4. Textual Boundaries (Terminus Post Quem and Ante Quem)

  • Terminus post quem: The anthology cites numerous late 13th-century poets, but contains zero references to 15th-century authors, Timurid invasions, or Sultanate developments after the mid-14th century.
  • Terminus ante quem: The Śārṅgadharapaddhati itself was heavily mined by 15th-century anthologists, most notably by Vallabhadeva in Kashmir for the Subhāṣitāvalī, which reproduces hundreds of ŚP verses along with their author attributions. Early paper manuscripts of ŚP survive from the 15th and 16th centuries (such as Royal Asiatic Society London MS Tod 32).

5. Critical Significance for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

Because the Dattātreyayogaśāstra lacks an explicit internal date, modern scholars rely on this external synchronism: 1. In chapters 157 and 158 of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE), Śārṅgadhara quotes DYS verse 35 (Padmāsana) verbatim: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam // (ŚP vv. 4376 & 4435); 2. He incorporates five of the ten mudrās of the DYS (vv. 4408–4417 = DYS vv. 131–145); 3. He adopts the distinctive prāṇāyāma methodology of the DYS.

Conclusion: The date 1363 CE provides an absolute, historically verified terminus ante quem for the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra. This firmly establishes that the DYS was composed in the 13th century (c. 1200–1300 CE), well over a century before the Haṭhapradīpikā (c. 1400–1450 CE), solidifying its position as the earliest surviving systematic root text of Haṭhayoga.

Verse 146

The therapeutic virtue of the inverted practice and the dramatic rekindling of the gastric fire.

146

करणं विपरीताख्यं सर्व्याधिविनाशनम् । नित्यमभ्यासयुक्तस्य जठराग्निर्विवर्धते ॥ १४६ ॥

karaṇaṃ viparītākhyaṃ sarvavyādhivināśanam / nityam abhyāsayuktasya jaṭharāgnir vivardhate // 146 //

  • Metre: Anuṣṭubh
  • Edition translation (English, J. Mallinson 2024): The practice called the Inverted destroys all diseases. In one who is constantly devoted to its practice, the gastric fire increases.
  • Our rendering (Russian & English): English: The technique called Viparīta (“the Inverted”) destroys all disease; in one who practises daily, the digestive fire blazes up. Русский перевод: Действие, именуемое «Перевёрнутым» (Випарита), уничтожает все болезни; у того, кто ежедневно предан упражнению, огонь пищеварения (джатхарагни) мощно возрастает.

Verse 147

The absolute necessity of abundant nutrition when performing inversions.

147

आहारो बहुलस्तस्य संपाद्यः सांकृते ध्रुवम् । अल्पाहारो यदि भवेदग्निर्दाहं करोति वै ॥ १४७ ॥

āhāro bahulas tasya saṃpādyaḥ sāṃkṛte dhruvam / alpāhāro yadi bhaved agnir dāhaṃ karoti vai // 147 //

  • Metre: Anuṣṭubh
  • Edition translation (English): Copious food must certainly be provided for him, O Sāṃkṛti. If he eats little food, the fire will indeed burn [him] up.
  • Our rendering (Russian & English): English: Abundant food must certainly be supplied for him, O Sāṃkṛti! If his food is meager, the internal fire will burn up [his body]. Русский перевод: Обильная пища непременно должна быть предоставлена ему, о Самкрити! Если же пищи будет мало, огонь начнет сжигать [его тело].

Variant readings in the manuscripts

Literal translation

English: The practice named Inverted is the destroyer of all diseases. In one endowed with daily practice, the gastric fire increases. Copious food must certainly be procured for him, O Sāṃkṛti! After six months, wrinkles and greyness of hair are no longer seen. Whoever should constantly practise it for the measure of a yāma (three hours), he indeed is a knower of yoga.

Русский (буквальный): Действие, называемое «Перевёрнутым», есть уничтожитель всех болезней. У наделенного ежедневной практикой желудочный огонь разрастается. Обильная пища для него должна быть обеспечена, о Самкрити, непременно! Если будет мало пищи, огонь поистине производит сожжение. Головой вниз и стопами вверх пусть будет на мгновение в первый день. Начиная с мгновения, день ото дня пусть практикует чуть больше. Свыше шести месяцев морщина и седина не видятся. Literary translation

English: Viparītakaraṇī — The Inverted Technique. The physical procedure known as Viparīta (“the Inversion”) is the supreme cure for all bodily afflictions. In a practitioner who applies himself to it daily, the inner digestive fire (jaṭharāgni) blazes up with tremendous force. Therefore, O Sāṃkṛti, substantial and nourishing food must unfailingly be provided for him; if his sustenance is inadequate, the ravenous digestive fire will turn inward and consume his own flesh. Inverting the physical frame—placing the head down upon the ground and pointing the feet skyward—one should remain for merely a brief moment on the very first day. Then, day by day, one must steadily and incrementally extend the duration of the practice. When one perseveres for six months, wrinkles of the skin and graying of the hair completely vanish from the body. He who is able to sustain this inverted posture unbroken for a full yāma (three hours) each day has truly mastered the art of yoga.

Русский (литературный): Випарита-карани — перевёрнутое действие. Психофизическое упражнение, именуемое «Перевёрнутым» (Випарита), искореняет абсолютно все болезни. У того, кто регулярно посвящает себя ему, внутренний огонь пищеварения (джатхарагни) разгорается с колоссальной силой. Посему, о Самкрити, практикующий должен быть непременно обеспечен щедрой и обильной пищей; если же питания окажется недостаточно, разбушевавшийся внутренний огонь начнет сжигать само его тело. Перевернув тело — опустив голову вниз и устремив стопы кверху, — в самый первый день следует пробыть в этом положении всего лишь краткое мгновение. Затем день ото дня длительность удержания позы следует осторожно и постепенно увеличивать. По истечении шести месяцев непрерывных занятий у йогина без следа исчезают морщины и седина. А тот, кто способен удерживать это положение ежедневно на протяжении целой ямы (около трёх часов), по праву именуется истинным знатоком йоги.

Editors’ commentary

  1. The internal alchemy of the sun and moon: In early Haṭhayoga physiology (first formulated in the Amṛtasiddhi and systematized here in the Dattātreyayogaśāstra), the body is governed by an inverted cosmic axis:
  • The Moon (candra) resides in the palate/head, constantly secreting the immortal nectar of life (bindu / amṛta).
  • The Sun (sūrya / agni) resides in the navel/belly, its upward-reaching flames devouring this falling nectar, which leads to physical decay, graying, wrinkling, and death. By physically standing on the head/shoulders (adhaḥśiraś cordhvapādaḥ), the spatial relation is literally inverted: the moon is now below and the sun is above. Gravity prevents the nectar from falling into the fire, allowing the practitioner to preserve bindu and achieve bodily rejuvenation.
  1. The ferocious blaze of the digestive fire: Because the downward drain of nectar is stopped and the solar fire in the abdomen is fanned, the metabolic furnace blazes intensely. Dattātreya specifically warns his disciple Sāṃkṛti that the yogin must consume substantial food (āhāro bahulaḥ). If insufficient fuel is ingested, the body enters auto-catabolism (agnir dāhaṃ karoti).
  2. From Mudrā to modern Āsana: In the Dattātreyayogaśāstra, Viparītakaraṇī is classified as a mudrā (or karaṇa) because its purpose is energetic: the seal/lock and reversal of fluids. In later centuries, this technique evolved directly into what modern hatha and postural yoga calls Viparīta Karaṇī Āsana and forms the conceptual root of inverted postures such as Śīrṣāsana (headstand) and Sarvāṅgāsana (shoulderstand).
