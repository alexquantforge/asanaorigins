Dating of the Text: Sources, Evidence, and Citations

Methodological note. The passages in quotation marks below are verbatim from the publications named, and the page numbers refer to the printed pagination of those publications (not to PDF page numbers). Superscript footnote numbers in the originals are omitted from the quotations, and ellipses mark any omitted words. Each statement is attributed to the source that makes it; a source's own hypothesis is labelled as a hypothesis. Where the sources consulted here do not settle a question, that is stated openly instead of being resolved by conjecture. No judgement is passed on the accuracy of the source publication.

1. What the source publication (the uploaded PDF) actually says The dating reproduced in the introduction above comes from the AyurYog blog post by Christèle Barois (19 January 2017). The relevant sentence reads, verbatim:

"It comprises about 350 ślokas and was composed in the 10th century at the latest (an 11th century manuscript is preserved in Calcutta)."

— Christèle Barois, "Eight Yoga Postures in the Dharmaputrikā," AyurYog blog, 19 January 2017 [S1]

The same post adds, on the antiquity of the posture chapter relative to Hemacandra:

"This chapter is an ancient and unparalleled testimony of a systematic and detailed description of a closed group of yogic postures, preceding by at least two centuries the description of postures in the Yogaśāstra by Hemacandra (12th century)."

— Barois 2017 [S1]

Links for [S1]: the original URL printed in the PDF is `https://ayuryog.org/blog/eight-yoga-postures-dharmaputrikā`. As of the date of checking, that address returns a 404 error; the text is preserved in the Internet Archive at <https://web.archive.org/web/20221207075113/http://ayuryog.org/blog/eight-yoga-postures-dharmaputrik%C4%81> (capture of 7 December 2022), which matches the uploaded PDF word for word.

2. The manuscript evidence for the early transmission of the corpus The blog post does not name the manuscripts on which its dating rests. The published codicological evidence for the early manuscript transmission of the Śivadharma corpus is set out by Florinda De Simini (2016):

(a) The earliest dated manuscript of the corpus identified so far — which does NOT contain the Dharmaputrikā. The Asiatic Society of Calcutta manuscript G 4077 is dated by its colophon to Nepal era 156 = 1035–36 CE (verified as 6 July 1036 CE by Petech, as reported by De Simini). De Simini writes: "The earliest dated manuscript of the Śivadharma corpus identified so far is ASC G 4077" (page 243 note 28). She also notes that this manuscript lacks the Dharmaputrikā:

Main text: "The final colophon dates it to Nepal era 156 (1035–36 CE)" (page 251).

Footnote 44: "this manuscript also exhibits the unique feature of missing the Dharmaputrikā, which is replaced by a longer work titled Lalitavistara" (page 251 note 44).

— De Simini 2016 [S2]; the date is verified as 6 July 1036 CE by Petech 1984, 36, as cited by De Simini.

(b) An early dated manuscript that DOES contain the Dharmaputrikā (1069 CE). This is Kathmandu, National Archives, NAK 3–393 (Nepalese-German Manuscript Preservation Project, microfilm A 1082/3), whose colophon is placed immediately after the final heading of the Dharmaputrikā and is dated Nepal era 189 = 24 May 1069 CE:

"This colophon is placed immediately after the final heading of the Dharmaputrikā … and dates the completion of the manuscript to … Nepal era 189 (May 24, 1069 CE)."

— De Simini 2016, page 252 and note 49 [S2]

(c) A further early dated witness. Cambridge, University Library, manuscript Add. 1645, dated Nepal era 259 = 1139–40 CE:

"This is a palm leaf manuscript consisting of 247 folios and dated in the final colophon to Nepal era 259 (1139–40 CE), a circumstance that makes it one of the earliest dated manuscripts of the Śivadharma collection."

— De Simini 2016, page 243 [S2]

(The same author gives this manuscript's date as 1138–39 CE in the summary on page 270; both figures are hers.)

What this evidence establishes. NAK 3–393 contains the Dharmaputrikā and is dated 24 May 1069 CE. The text therefore existed by that date, which is a terminus ante quem (the latest possible date of composition) on the manuscript evidence quoted above.

What it does not establish. The blog's parenthetical "(an 11th century manuscript is preserved in Calcutta)" [S1] is reproduced here as the source publication's own wording. The blog does not identify which Calcutta manuscript it means, and the sources consulted for this document do not settle that question: De Simini's Appendix I expressly does not describe the Asiatic Society of Calcutta manuscripts and refers the reader to the catalogues instead — "For other manuscripts not falling into this category — like those at the Asiatic Society of Calcutta — the reader is referred to the pertinent catalogues" (De Simini 2016, page 273 [S2]). Accordingly, no conclusion is drawn here about that parenthetical statement. The only related fact reported by the sources is the one quoted in §2a: G 4077 (1036 CE) does not contain the Dharmaputrikā.

3. The author's own later academic article (2020)

In her 2020 academic article, Barois gives the manuscript-based dating and then argues for a considerably earlier composition date on the basis of textual parallels:

"The Dharmaputrikā is an early work on yoga that has been transmitted to us as part of the 'Śivadharma corpus,' a collection of eight texts preserved in several eleventh- and twelfth-century Nepalese palm-leaf manuscripts."

— Barois 2020, page 6 [S3]

She addresses the "1069 of the Common Era" date proposed in the New Catalogus Catalogorum, stating that it is the date of the copy, not of composition:

"The New Catalogus Catalogorum (NCC) volume IX (1977) also argued in favour of a late date: 'Dharmaputrikā - On yoga in 16 chs. of philosophical and tāntric nature; prob. composed in 1069 of the Common Era in the reign of Śaṅkara Deva. Nepal I. page 92. pref. page xlviii. Rep. Hpr.1895–1900, page 6.' It is the date of the copy of the manuscript (as for the Sanskrit text of the colophon which quotes Śaṅkaradeva, see Petech, 1984: 46)."

— Barois 2020, page 16, note 28 [S3]

In the same article she also records the one eleventh-century manuscript in which the Dharmaputrikā is absent:

"The Dharmaputrikā is replaced by a text called Lalitavistara in a single, multiple-text manuscript dated from the eleventh century (on the Lalitavistara, see De Simini and Mirnig, 2017)."

— Barois 2020, page 6, note 4 [S3]

And she proposes a much earlier terminus ad quem on the basis of parallels with the Śivadharmottara:

"Given the lack of context of the above parallel passages in the Śivadharmottara, I suggest that the Dharmaputrikā is the source, implying its terminus ad quem is the seventh century, which is the estimated date of composition of the Śivadharmottara. … This would make the Dharmaputrikā the earliest surviving practical manual on yoga."

— Barois 2020, pages 20–21 [S3]

Important: the "seventh century" date is Barois's hypothesis based on textual parallels, not a manuscript date. It should be cited as her suggestion, not as an established fact.

4. A secondary source repeating the "around 10th century" dating "… the tenth chapter on yogic medical treatment (cikitsā) of the Dharmaputrikā (around 10th century), a text of the Śivadharma corpus (Barois forthcoming)."

— Powell 2018, page 60 [S4]

Powell expressly refers the dating to Barois ("Barois forthcoming"), so this is a secondary citation rather than independent manuscript evidence.

आसनस्थेन पादौ द्वौ न्यस्येतां भूतले समं।जानुद्वये हस्ततलं स्थाप्य पीठासनं भवेत्॥१२॥

āсанастхена пāдау двау нйасйетāм̣ бхӯтале самам |джāнудвайе хастаталам̣ стхāпйа пӣт̣хāсанам̣ бхавет ||12||

Word-for-word Analysis

Verse 12

āsana-sthena
by [the yogin] seated in the posture
pādau dvau
the two feet
nyasyetām
should be placed (dual optative)
bhūtale
on the ground
samam
evenly, parallel
jānu-dvaye
on the two knees
hasta-talam

sthāpya

pīṭha-āsanam

Literal Translation

By [the yogin] seated in the posture, the two feet should be placed evenly on the ground; having placed the palms of the hands on the two knees, [this] becomes the throne posture.

Literary Translation

[The yogin] being seated, both feet should be placed parallel on the ground; after he has placed the palm of the hands on both knees, [this is] the posture [named] pīṭha. (12)

A simple upright seat: feet flat and parallel on the floor, palms resting on the knees — the posture of sitting on a "throne" (pīṭha).
