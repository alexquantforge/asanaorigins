Dating and Probable Place of Composition

The date of the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā is disputed; the dates of the manuscripts used for the printed edition do not in themselves establish the date of composition.

In the section “Provenience and Age” of his introduction, F. Otto Schrader associated the text with Kashmir. Among the arguments he adduces are the mention of yantra diagrams on birch bark in chapters 26 and 45; the image in chapter 39 of the sun emerging from an obstacle of snow; and the account of Muktāpīḍa in chapter 48. Schrader stressed that little can be said with certainty about the text’s age: he placed it in a group of Saṃhitās for which he proposed the eighth century CE as an upper limit. As a possible lower limit, he considered the text’s presentation of Buddhism as divided into three major schools; in Schrader’s view, this implies a date after the establishment of Mahāyāna, approximately after 300 CE. These are Schrader’s dating arguments, not an established date for the text. See the sections of his introduction on the manuscripts, provenance, and age, pages 94–99 6.

In a 2017 article, D. Wujastyk proposes a date after 1000 CE, and possibly South India, for the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā 5. Marion Rastelli’s 2018 article abstract likewise associates the text’s composition with South India, narrowing the hypothesis to the twelfth–thirteenth centuries 3. These later proposals differ substantially from Schrader’s earlier dating framework; without additional evidence, they cannot be collapsed into one precise date or presented as an agreed consensus. Accordingly, this article does not assign a date to the āsana section itself.

2.1. Lower limit: early authors do not quote the text (argument from silence)

“Chronology

The «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» can in no way be considered a very ancient work as ancient authors do not make any reference to the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā». For instance, Halāyudha (1050 of the Common Era) in his Brāhmaṇasarvasva and Lakṣmīdhara (1150 of the Common Era) in the Kalpataru do not mention «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» anywhere; nor do they quote any verse from the same whereas both the authors quote liberally from other sources. Were the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» prior to these authors, they would have certainly quoted from «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»; more so because (like the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā») both the authors advocate the doctrine of Jñānakarmasamuccaya (synthesis of Knowledge and Action) for the sake of salvation in support of which they quote from different works like the Yogayājñavalkya, Agnipurāṇa and so on The absence of such quotation of «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» in their works is an indication that «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is the later origin, that is after the 12th century of the Common Era”

Compiler's note: testing the argument.

  • Lakṣmīdhara. I checked the Mokṣakāṇḍa of the Kṛtyakalpataru in the electronic text by P. Olivelle (UT Austin, 2022; based on the edition by K. V. Rangaswami Aiyangar, Gaekwad’s Oriental Series CII, Baroda 1945; https://sites.utexas.edu/sanskrit/resources/dharmasastra/krtyakalpataru/). Results:
  • There are indeed no verses of the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» in it. The quotations marked “vasiṣṭhaḥ” are prose rules for hermits and ascetics, not verses of the yogic «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».
  • The Yogayājñavalkya is quoted precisely in defence of the combination of knowledge and action. Chapter 15 “atha jñānakarmopayogaḥ”, page 146 by the page markers of the e-text: “yogiyājñavalkyaḥ | … jñānakarmasamāyogāt param āpnoti pūruṣam | pṛthagbhāve na sidhyeta ubhe tasmāt samāśrayet ||” (“Yogiyājñavalkya: … From the union of knowledge and action one reaches the supreme Person; it is not achieved by either separately, therefore one should resort to both”). I have confirmed this part of the editors' claim.
  • A search of the Mokṣakāṇḍa found no quotations marked as from the Agnipurāṇa. The editors may have meant other parts of the Kalpataru, or Halāyudha; this has not been checked.
  • I did not examine the other volumes of the Kalpataru.
  • Halāyudha. I have not seen the text of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva (edited by D. Bhattacharyya, Sanskrit Sahitya Parishad Series 29, Calcutta 1960).
  • The date “1050 of the Common Era” conflicts with other sources. According to them, the author of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva was a minister of King Lakṣmaṇasena of Bengal, 12th century: the description of the 1960 edition on exoticindiaart.com (“the court of king Lakṣmaṇasena of Bengal in the 12th century”); Swami Harshananda on hindupedia.com (“CE 1160-1200”). The same source distinguishes the lexicographer Halāyudha (10th century) from the author of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva.
  • On these data Halāyudha lived after Lakṣmīdhara, not before him.
  • The argument itself is an argument from silence. According to the editors, both authors quote other works in support of jñānakarmasamuccaya; had the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» existed, they would have quoted it too. Birch 2018 (note 92) explicitly judges this argument to be inconclusive (see §4).

2.2. Upper limit: later authors quote the text

“That the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is not very recent becomes evident from the fact that authors like Nāgojībhaṭṭa (end of 17th century), Bhāvāgaṇeśa (1550-1600 of the Common Era) Vijñānabhikṣu (1550 of the Common Era) and so on, quote the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā», Mallīnātha (1450 of the Common Era) also, who preceded Vijñānabhikṣu quotes «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» in his commentaries on Raghuvaṃśa and Kumārasambhava.”

Wujastyk gives independent confirmation regarding Mallinātha (see §5.5). There Mallinātha is dated “flourished 1400” rather than 1450, and the passage is given as Raghuvaṃśa 13.52.

2.3. The calculation “about 1250” from the Haṭhapradīpikā

“The «Haṭhapradīpikā» also while describing the Āsanas qualifies them as recommended by sages like Vasiṣṭha and so on compare

"Vasiṣṭhādyaiśca munibhirmatsyendrādyaiśca yogibhiḥ

aṅgīkṛtānyāsanāni kathyante kānicinmayā" «Haṭhapradīpikā». I. 18

Thus, the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is a work prior to the «Haṭhapradīpikā» and if the «Haṭhapradīpikā» is assigned to about 1350 of the Common Era, the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» must be taken to have been ready at least before a hundred years. Thus, we can assign the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» to about 1250 of the Common Era”

  • «Haṭhapradīpikā» 1.18 names Vasiṣṭha among the sages who adopted āsanas. It does not refer explicitly to the work called the Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā; the conclusion “the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is older than the «Haṭhapradīpikā»” was drawn by the editors.

2.4. The editors' caveat: the text is multi-layered

“Yet we cannot definitely state that all the material that has come down to us in the extant «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» belonged to that period. Some of the material must belong to an earlier period and the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» must have attained the present form passing through various stages like the epic Jaya which attained the form of the Mahābhārata.”

Immediately after this the book has a paragraph on the lineages of teachers:

“The various traditions regarding the lineage of the teachers who transmitted that lore differ to a large extent. But all these (except the Mahābhārata, Śāntiparva) accounts have Vasiṣṭha and Śakti in common. The various accounts are given below.”

The lineage charts follow. In the online copy their words (Brahma, Vasistha, Sakti, Parasara, Vyasa, Suka, Gaudapada, Govinda, Sankara) were mixed into the text of the section. The printed text confirmed that the word “Sakti” is not part of the quotation about «Haṭhapradīpikā» 1.18: OCR had inserted it from the chart.

2.5. Manuscripts and early citations (section “Extent of the Text”)

“All the six manuscripts except one have eight chapters. The manuscript dated Samvat 1463 (that is 1406 of the Common Era), which is most authentic in this respect has eight chapters. Nrsimha Vajpeyi (1450 of the Common Era) quotes in his Nityacaradipa the following verse of Vasistha Yoga: […]”

(The verse itself is illegible in the OCR.)

“This verse happens to be the nineteenth verse of the eighth chapter of the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā». It can be inferred that upto about 1450 of the Common Era the work was recognised to comprise of at least eight chapters.”

Compiler's note. The manuscript dated 1406 by itself gives only a latest date for the existence of the text (not later than 1406).

2.6. Relation to the Yogayājñavalkya and the Haṭhapradīpikā

“None of the scholars, quoting the stanzas found to be common to both the works, ascribe them to Yogayajnavalkya but to Vasistha. Similarly Bhavaganesa and Nagojibhatta (on Yogasutra I. 33) while quoting on the process of the purification of Nadis without Kumbhaka, ascribe it clearly to «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» and not to «Yogayājñavalkya». The three types of Pranayama, namely Adhama, Madhyama and Uttama are also traced to Vasistha and not to «Yogayājñavalkya». From these it becomes clear that «Yogayājñavalkya» is a later work and that it seems to have borrowed from «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».”

“The verses 11-22, of chapter 5 of the «Yogayājñavalkya» which describe the purification of Nadis, occur almost verbatim in the second chapter of the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» (Vikrama era 58-70). Also the words (kecit vadanti) meaning 'some say' occurring prior to verse 10 of the Vth chapter of «Yogayājñavalkya» seem to indicate Vasistha. From this it can be inferred that «Yogayājñavalkya» has borrowed from «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».”

“But the «Haṭhapradīpikā» itself is a work later than the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» (the facts that lead us to this conclusion are given below). Hence, it can definitely be said that the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is not a borrower.”

2.7. A discrepancy within the edition: “prior to 1150”

Checked against the printed edition. Below is the text transcribed from the printed book (section “Sūta Saṃhitā – Jñānayogakāṇḍa”). It confirms the reading “prior to 1150 of the Common Era”, that is this is not an OCR error. The beginning and end of the paragraph were missing in the online copy and are restored here.

“Sūta Saṃhitā- Jñānayogakāṇḍa

The Sūta Saṃhitā has been published by the Ānandāśrama, Pune. Whilst tracing the pedigree of teachers through whom the work is handed down, the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» states that Śakti derived the lore from Vasiṣṭha. From this it can be inferred that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» borrowed from the dialogue between Śakti and Vasiṣṭha. The Sūta Saṃhitā (Muktikhaṇḍa, VII. 50-55) also refers to Cakradhara Mahānubhāva and to his disciple Subhaga. Cakradhara is assigned to 1250 of the Common Era. But as the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» (as shown below) is prior to 1150 of the Common Era and as there is a period of hundred years intervening between the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» and Cakradhara, the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» is certainly the borrower from the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā». We can also point out to influence of Vedānta upon the Sūta Saṃhitā. This also proves that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» is later than the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».”

This paragraph gives three arguments that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» is later than the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»:

3. In the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» lineage of teachers Śakti receives the teaching from Vasiṣṭha. From this the editors infer that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» borrows from the dialogue of Vasiṣṭha and Śakti.

4. The Sūta Saṃhitā mentions Cakradhara Mahānubhāva, whom the editors assign to 1250. The «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā», according to them, is older than 1150.

5. The influence of Vedānta is noticeable in the Sūta Saṃhitā. No concrete examples are given in this paragraph.

Compiler's note.

  • Within the sentence the arithmetic is consistent: 1250 − 100 = 1150. So “1150” is not a misprint but an intended figure.
  • The reference “as shown below” points to the section “Chronology”. But there a different conclusion is reached: “after the 12th century of the Common Era” and “we can assign the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» to about 1250 of the Common Era” (§§2.1, 2.3). The same book thus gives two incompatible dates for the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»: “prior to 1150” and “about 1250”. The book does not explain this discrepancy.
  • Why this happened cannot be established from the printed text of 2005. One could check whether these passages agree in the 1984 edition.
  • Argument (2) depends on the date. If the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» belongs to “about 1250”, it is contemporary with Cakradhara rather than a hundred years older. Then this argument for the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» borrowing from the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» loses its force. This is my observation, not the editors'.

The "Mānasollāsa" is dated by its author, Sureśvarācārya, and not by internal data of the text. Introduction. The Dating of the Mānasollāsa

2.1. The Starting Point: the Text Is Not Dated by Itself

No separate study of the dating of the "Mānasollāsa" exists. All existing datings are derivative: first the author, Sureśvarācārya, is dated, and then the date is transferred to the text. And the dating of Sureśvara depends on two things: which date for Śaṅkara is accepted, and whether the identity of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra is admitted.

This situation was recorded not in the scholarly literature but by the editor of the 19th century himself:

"Notwithstanding the attempts of several oriental scholars to fix the date of Śaṅkarācārya, there has been a certain amount of uncertainty regarding it, the date assigned to him varying from the sixth century before Christ to the eighth century of the Common Era. The date of Sureśvarācārya, closely connected as it is with that of his reputed teacher, has not been more satisfactorily settled."

— Preface to the 1895 edition, page 3 (keyed by the Ambuda portal)

And on the attribution as such — note this, it is a key fact:

"Nowhere in the hymn or the commentary is the author of either mentioned by name. The commentator refers in his Vārttika to the author of the hymn by mere general terms such as Kavi (author) and Guru (teacher). The style and the subject-matter, however, of the two works incline us to believe in their genuineness."

— there

That is: the author's name is never once stated in the text, and the attribution rests on style and subject-matter alone. This must be borne in mind when reading any dating.

2.5. The Dispute on Which Everything Depends: Sureśvara = Maṇḍana?

If they are one person, the text falls in the early 8th century. If they are different, it falls in the 8th–9th centuries.

Against the identification:

  • M. Hiriyanna, "Sureśvara and Maṇḍana-Miśra", JRAS 55/2 (April 1923), pages 259–263; continuation there, 56/1 (1924), pages 96–97. Arguments: doctrinal divergences, and the Śṛṅgeri tradition, in which Maṇḍana and Sureśvara are different persons.
  • S. Kuppuswami Sastri — introduction to the critical edition of the "Brahmasiddhi" (1937; reprinted 1984).
  • S. N. Dasgupta — A History of Indian Philosophy, volume 2 (1932).
  • S. S. Sharma, Advaita Tradition in Indian Philosophy (1997), pages 290–291 — sets out the arguments of Hiriyanna and Kuppuswami Sastri.

For the identification:

  • R. Balasubramanian: "there is no convincing evidence to prove that Maṇḍana … are different persons" (as reported in Wikipedia, article "Sureśvara").
  • The Śṛṅgeri Maṭha — officially, in the documents of the maṭha and in hagiographies.

What the divergences consist in (after Sharma and Wikipedia):

  • where avidyā (ignorance) is located: in Maṇḍana it is in the jīva (the individual being), in Sureśvara in Brahman. This reflects the two sub-schools of Advaita — Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa;
  • the role of meditation: for Maṇḍana, knowledge from the mahāvākya ("great sayings" of the Upaniṣads) alone is not enough, meditation is required; for Sureśvara knowledge liberates directly, and meditation is an aid;
  • the attitude to sphoṭa (the doctrine that the meaning of a word manifests in the sound as such): Maṇḍana accepts śabdādvaita, Sureśvara ignores it.

2.6. What the Text Itself Says

In the ninth ullāsa, in the commentary on verse 23½, the author refers to his own commentary on Maṇḍana's "Brahmasiddhi". Reading from the scan of the 2008 edition, page 119 (sources/scans/mss/2008-str119-celekom.png):

इति मण्डनसिद्धान्त एव सुरेश्वरस्यापि सम्मतः।तदनुषङ्गतः शाब्दापरोक्षवादोऽपि।विशदं चैतत् ब्रह्मसिद्धौ, तद्भूमिकायाञ्चास्मदीयायाम्॥२३१/२॥

"Thus, the doctrine of Maṇḍana is accepted by Sureśvara too; and, following from it, the doctrine of śabda-aparokṣa (immediate knowledge arising from the word). This is set out in detail in the 'Brahmasiddhi' and in our own bhūmikā to it."

Two circumstances must be kept in mind:

  1. The word अस्मदीया ("our") is the author's direct reference to his own commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi". This is internal evidence in favour of the identification, and it is usually quoted precisely in this dispute.
  2. It does not settle the question: the commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi" could also have been written by a pupil.

2.7. The Position of the Editors of 2008

The Hindi preface to the 2008 edition is a rare case of an editor setting out both the argument and the counter-argument.

«कुछ आधुनिकों ने इस स्तोत्र को अभिनवगुप्त के प्रत्यभिज्ञादर्शन का ग्रन्थ माना है।»

— "Some modern [scholars] have regarded this stotra as a text of Abhinavagupta's Pratyabhijñā-darśana" (page i). It was precisely to answer this that the commentary "Mānasollāsa-vardhinī" was commissioned.

«एतिहासिक दृष्टि से मण्डन आचार्य के पूर्ववर्ती थे।»

— "From the historical point of view Maṇḍana was a predecessor of the Ācārya [Śaṅkara]" (page ii).

The counter-argument against the identification, given in the same place: in the "Bṛhadāraṇyaka-vārttika" Maṇḍana is spoken of in the third person with the pejorative पण्डितम्मन्य ("one who fancies himself learned"), which no one writes of himself.

2.8. The Result on Dating

A formulation that can be defended:

The "Mānasollāsa" is dated by its author, Sureśvarācārya, and not by internal data of the text. Depending on the date of Śaṅkara accepted (788–820 or around 700) and on the outcome of the dispute over the identification of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra, the text is assigned either to the early 8th century (around 700–730) or to the 8th–9th centuries. There is no independent dating of the text on internal grounds in the literature.

A supporting boundary that does not depend on the dispute: Vācaspati Miśra, writing according to Dasgupta in 842, quotes Sureśvara — which gives an upper bound ("terminus ante quem") in the 9th century.

1.2. The Chronological Problem: Scholarly Arguments and Dating Debate

The historical dating of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī has been examined by leading Sanskritists and historians of yoga:

1. Arguments of Doctor M. L. Gharote and Doctor M. Venkata Reddy (Critical Edition, 2007):

  • Terminus post quem (Earliest possible date): Sundaradeva directly cites late seventeenth-century works:
  • «Haṭharatnāvalī» (Haṭharatnāvalī) by Śrīnivāsa (Śrīnivāsa), composed circa 1670–1695 CE. Sundaradeva adopts its specialized terminology, āsana lists, and quotes its verses;
  • «Kumbhakapaddhati» (Kumbhakapaddhati) by Raghuvīra (Raghuvīra), datable to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century;
  • «Yogacandrikā» (Yogacandrikā) and «Yogacintāmaṇi» (Yogacintāmaṇi) by Śivānanda Sarasvatī.
  • Terminus ante quem (Latest possible date): The oldest and only complete dated manuscript witness (manuscript number 4235, Scindia Oriental Institute, Ujjain) preserves a scribal colophon with the exact date of completion: Saṃvat 1846 (Vikrama Saṃvat), month of Vaiśākha, dark fortnight, 10th tithi, Tuesday, corresponding to April–May 1789 CE.
  • Gharote's Conclusion: The treatise was composed between 1700 and 1750 CE (first half of the eighteenth century).

2. Textological Arguments of Doctor Jason Birch (Oxford University / Haṭha Yoga Project):

  • Doctor Jason Birch points out that the Haṭhatattvakaumudī epitomizes the late medieval "encyclopedic turn" in yoga. This genre of comprehensive compendia, synthesizing hundreds of earlier sources (from Upaniṣads and Tantras to late medieval paddhatis), flourished among the scholarly Brahmin milieus of Vārāṇasī precisely in the first half of the eighteenth century.
  • Birch underscores that Sundaradeva's Sanskrit exhibits late classical syntax infused with the scientific medical lexicon of early modern India.

3. Iconographic and Structural Analysis of Professor Gudrun Bühnemann:

  • Professor Bühnemann examined the evolution of āsana classification schemes in Indian traditions. In Chapter 7 of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī, Sundaradeva retains the canonical core of 15 postures (inherited from Chapter 1 of Svātmārāma's Haṭhapradīpikā), yet reinterprets them within a therapeutic methodology and introduces distinctive technical nuances (for example, yonyāsana as an advanced modification of siddhāsana, specialized breath management during padmāsana). This marks a systematic transition before the proliferation of vast catalogs of 84 or more āsanas in late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century manuals.

4. Sociological Synthesis by Doctor Mark Singleton:

  • Mark Singleton emphasizes that Sundaradeva represents a notable synthesis of two social roles: the practicing yoga adept and the high-caste, university-educated vaidya-paṇḍita. Such integration was characteristic of cosmopolitan Vārāṇasī in the eighteenth century, where Haṭha yoga techniques were absorbed into the orthodox curriculum of health, longevity, and therapeutics.

Placing the feet upon the opposite thighs with soles turned upwards, the yogin cups his hands in his lap, fixes his gaze upon the tip of the nose (nāsāgradṛṣṭi), curls the tip of the tongue against the palate behind the front teeth (nabhomudrā), and firmly locks the chin against the jugular notch of the chest (jālandharabandha). PART I. THE HISTORICAL AND PHILOLOGICAL DATING (1363 CE)

The dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati to 1363 CE is universally recognized as one of the most solid chronological anchors in medieval Indology. It rests upon the convergence of three independent lines of evidence: 1. Direct manuscript colophons recording the exact year in the Vikrama Saṃvat calendar; 2. The compiler’s autobiographical genealogy spanning three generations in verses 1.2–1.6; 3. External epigraphical and historiographical synchronisms with the documented reign of King Hammīradeva Cāhamāna of Raṇastambhapura (r. 1282–1301 CE).

1. Manuscript Colophons and the Vikrama Saṃvat Era

In the colophons of the earliest preserved manuscripts of the anthology—identified by Theodor Aufrecht in 1873 and Peter Peterson in 1888—the exact formulaic date of completion is preserved:

𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑣𝑎𝑡 1420 𝑣𝑎𝑟ṣ𝑒 ... śā𝑟ṅ𝑔𝑎𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒ṇ𝑎 𝑝𝑎𝑑𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑡𝑖ḥ 𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑘ṛ𝑡ā

(“In the year 1420 of the Saṃvat era, this paddhati was composed by Śārṅgadhara”).

Calendar Conversion:

  • The Vikrama Saṃvat Era: An epoch standardly counted from 57 BCE.
  • Direct Mathematical Conversion:

𝑌𝑒𝑎𝑟 𝐶𝐸= 1420 −57 = 1363 𝐶𝐸

  • Seasonal Adjustment: Depending on whether the local North/West Indian calendar followed the Caitrādi reckoning (new year beginning in March/April) or the Kārttikādi reckoning (beginning in October/November, customary in Rajasthan and Gujarat), the year 1420 corresponds to 1363–1364 CE. Modern scholarly consensus standardly cites 1363 CE.

3. Historical and Epigraphical Synchronism: King Hammīradeva (r. 1282–1301 CE)

The monarch named in verse 1.2 is the famed historical ruler Hammīradeva Cāhamāna of the fortress-kingdom of Raṇastambhapura (Ranthambhore): Documented Chronology:

  1. Accession (1282/1283 CE = Vikrama era 1339): Confirmed in the Hammīramahākāvya of Nayacandra Sūri (VIII.54) and the Prabandhakośa of Rājaśekhara Sūri (compiled in Delhi in 1349 CE).
  2. Epigraphical Proof: The Balvan stone inscription of Vikrama era 1345 (1288 CE) records Hammīra’s military expeditions (digvijaya) and religious gifts.
  3. Siege and Fall of Ranthambhore (11 July 1301 CE): Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi besieged Ranthambhore; after the heroic defense, the jauhar (mass self-immolation) of the Rajput women, Hammīra died fighting on the battlefield on 3 Dhū al-Qa’dah 700 AH (11 July 1301 CE). This catastrophe is recorded by contemporary Persian eyewitnesses: Amīr Khusrau (Khazā’in al-Futūḥ, 1311 CE) and Ḍiyā’ al-Dīn Baranī (Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī).

Generational Calculation:

  • Generation 1 (Grandfather): Rāghavadeva served as royal preceptor during Hammīra’s mature reign (around 1285–1300 CE).
  • Generation 2 (Father): Dāmodara flourished in the subsequent period (around 1310–1335 CE).
  • Generation 3 (Grandson/Compiler): Śārṅgadhara, born around 1315–1320 CE, completed his vast compendium in 163 chapters at full scholarly maturity in 1363 CE.
  • Time Span: Between Hammīra’s death (1301 CE) and the completion of the anthology (1363 CE), exactly 62 years elapsed—the precise demographic span for two full generations from grandfather to grandson.

Personal Verse of King Hammīra Preserved:

In Chapter 140 (Vīrarasanirvedaḥ, verse 3974), Śārṅgadhara quotes an authentic stanza explicitly attributed śrī-hammīrasya (“By King Hammīra”), proving that the compiler’s family held the direct literary archives of the Cāhamāna royal court.

4. Philological Boundaries (Terminus Post Quem and Terminus Ante Quem)

  • Lower Boundary (Terminus post quem): The anthology incorporates late 13th-century poets, yet contains no trace of Timurid or 15th-century authors, establishing that it was compiled before 1400 CE.
  • Upper Boundary (Terminus ante quem): The Kashmiri florilegium Subhāṣitāvalī of Vallabhadeva (15th century) quotes over 300 verses directly from the Śārṅgadharapaddhati.

  • Modern Consensus: Accepted without qualification by A. B. Keith (1928), M. Winternitz (1936), S. K. De (1947), Ludwik Sternbach (1974), James Mallinson (2011, 2024), and Jason Birch (2013).

Dating of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra (around 1200 CE / 13th Century): Philological, Historical, and Stemmatic Evidence

The Dattātreyayogaśāstra («Dattātreyayogaśāstra») holds a singular place in the history of Indian thought as the earliest surviving Sanskrit treatise to systematically teach physical techniques under the name of Haṭhayoga and to integrate Patañjali’s eight-limbed system (aṣṭāṅgayoga) into an overarching hierarchy of four yogas.

In early 20th-century Indology, estimates of the text’s date fluctuated widely between the 13th and 15th centuries (J.N. Farquhar, P.K. Gode, H.D. Sharma, C. Bouy). However, the philological discoveries of the Haṭha Yoga Project (SOAS University of London / Oxford) and James Mallinson’s 2024 critical edition (Collection Indologie, number 162, École française d’Extrême-Orient & IFP) have definitively anchored the text: the Dattātreyayogaśāstra was composed in about 1200 CE (late 12th to early 13th century, around 1200–1250 CE).

Below is an exhaustive breakdown of the philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence supporting this dating.

1. The Upper Bound (Terminus Ante Quem: 1363 CE)

The upper chronological limit is firmly secured by external textual witnesses:

  • «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verse 35 (Padmāsana) is quoted verbatim twice («Śārṅgadharapaddhati» verses 4376 & 4435: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam //);

2. The Lower Bound (Terminus Post Quem: around 1150–1200 CE)

The lower chronological limit is established by the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra»’s relationship with earlier yogic and tantric corpora:

  1. Reception and De-Buddhicisation of the Amṛtasiddhi (11th–12th century):
  • The Amṛtasiddhi is the earliest surviving Sanskrit text to introduce the somatic physiology of bindu, the sun in the abdomen, the moon in the palate, and the three basic physical locks (Mahāmudrā, Mahābandha, Mahāvedha). As shown by Mallinson and Szántó (2021), the Amṛtasiddhi originated within a Buddhist Tantric (Mahāyāna/Vajrayāna) environment.
  • The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» adopts this somatic system of bindu preservation and physical seals, but thoroughly adapts it to a Purāṇic Hindu and Vaiṣṇava framework, subordinating it to the worship of Viṣṇu/Hari and the sage Kapila. The assimilation and theological translation of a Buddhist tantric text into mainstream Brahmanical yoga required several decades, placing the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» no earlier than the late 12th century.
  1. Systematisation of the Fourfold Yoga of the Amaraughaprabodha (12th century):
  • The Amaraughaprabodha, attributed to Gorakṣanātha, first briefly enunciated the fourfold hierarchy of Mantrayoga, Layayoga, Haṭhayoga, and Rājayoga.
  • The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» is the first text in history to systematically furnish each of these four yogas with comprehensive, practical instructions.
  1. The Earliest Textual Appearance of Vajrolīmudrā:
  • Neither the Amṛtasiddhi nor the 12th-century Vivekamārtaṇḍa / Gorakṣaśataka knows the practice of vajrolī. The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» is the oldest surviving text to describe the physical absorption of sexual fluids and the subsidiary practices of sahajolī and amarolī (verses 150–178), marking a crucial historical development in early 13th-century yoga.

3. Stemmatic and Paleographical Evidence: Two Divergent Manuscript Branches

A pivotal argument for the early date of around 1200 CE, demonstrated by James Mallinson in the 2024 critical edition, rests on manuscript stemmatics:

  • The transmission of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» is split into two deeply bifurcated recensions: 1. The Southern Recension ( ): σ Preserved in ancient palm-leaf codices in Grantha script (repositories in Chennai, Mysore, Thanjavur, and Pondicherry). This branch preserves the more archaic, complete text of 193 verses, including explicit instructions for women practitioners of vajrolī (strī-vajrolī). 2. The Northern/Western Recension ($ u$): Preserved in paper manuscripts in Devanāgarī script (Jodhpur, Baroda, Varanasi). In this branch, the text was abridged to 169–173 verses, and the explicit sexual-somatic verses were excised or softened.
  • In textual criticism, the geographic migration of a text from the Deccan/South to Rajasthan and Varanasi, across different writing media (palm-leaf to paper) and scripts (Grantha to Devanāgarī), accompanied by deep textual divergence and conscious editorial bowdlerisation prior to 1363 CE, demands a developmental span of at least 100 to 150 years of scribal transmission.

4. Historical and Geographical Context: The Deccan and the Dattātreya Cult

  1. Provenance: Mallinson demonstrates that the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» was composed on the Deccan plateau (modern Maharashtra and neighbouring areas of Karnataka and Telangana).
  2. The Rise of Dattātreya Worship in the 12th–13th Centuries:
  • In first-millennium Purāṇic literature, Dattātreya is primarily an ancient sage (ṛṣi), the son of Atri and Anasūyā.
  • During the late 12th and 13th centuries, under the Seuna/Yādava dynasty of Devagiri in the Deccan, Dattātreya became the centre of an intense religious movement. He was adopted as a supreme divine manifestation by the Mahānubhāva-panth (founded by Cakradhara in the mid-13th century) and by the early Marathi Nāths (Jñāneśvar in his 1290 Jñāneśvarī lauds the lineage of Dattātreya and Gorakṣa).
  • This matches the concluding verse of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» (verse 193): harir eva svayaṃ sākṣād dattātreyo ’bhavat purā (“Hari [Viṣṇu] Himself directly became incarnate as Dattātreya”).
  1. Radical Egalitarianism (Verses 41–44): The «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» famously declares that anyone, regardless of caste, sex, or religious identity, can succeed in yoga:

brāhmaṇaḥ śramaṇo vāpi bauddho vāpy ārhato ’thavā / kāpāliko vā cārvākaḥ śraddhayā sahitaḥ sudhīḥ // 41 //

“Whether a Brahmin, an ascetic (śramaṇa), a Buddhist, a Jain (ārhata), a Kāpālika, or even a materialist (Cārvāka)—if wise and endowed with faith, one attains all perfections in yoga.” This inclusive, non-sectarian milieu is hallmark of early 13th-century Deccan asceticism prior to the hardening of sectarian orthodoxy.

Conclusion

The convergence of philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence firmly dates the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra to circa 1200 CE (early 13th century).

This early date cements the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» as the foundational root text of the entire Haṭhayoga tradition, providing the direct textual and somatic archetype from which both the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) and the Haṭhapradīpikā (around 1400–1450 CE) drew their core teachings.

Excursus: Textual and Historical Evidence for the Dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) as an Absolute Terminus Ante Quem for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

The dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati («Śārṅgadharapaddhati») to 1363 CE is one of the most firmly established and methodologically robust chronological benchmarks in Sanskrit philology and the history of early Haṭhayoga. Because Śārṅgadhara borrowed verbatim into his anthology the definition of Padmāsana (verses 4376 & 4435 = «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verse 35), the techniques of channel purification and prāṇāyāma, and five cardinal mudrās (verses 4408–4417 = «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verses 131–145: Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandharabandha, Uḍḍīyānabandha, and Mūlabandha), the precise date of his compendium provides an incontrovertible upper limit (terminus ante quem) by which time the Dattātreyayogaśāstra must have already been in circulation.

The dating of 1363 CE is grounded in the convergence of three mutually independent bodies of historical and textual evidence (Aufrecht 1873; Peterson 1888; Keith 1928; Winternitz 1936; Sternbach 1974; Mallinson 2011, 2024; Birch 2013):

1. The Colophons and the Vikrama Era (1420 «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»)

In the scribal colophons of the earliest extant manuscripts (first systematically collated by Theodor Aufrecht in 1873 from Oxford and Berlin codices and corroborated by Peter Peterson in 1888 from the Government Collection in Pune), the date of composition is recorded as:

𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑣𝑎𝑡 1420 𝑣𝑎𝑟ṣ𝑒 ... śā𝑟ṅ𝑔𝑎𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒ṇ𝑎 𝑝𝑎𝑑𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑡𝑖ḥ 𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑘ṛ𝑡ā (“In the year 1420 of the [Vikrama] Samvat era, this paddhati was compiled by Śārṅgadhara”).

  • The Vikrama Era (Vikrama Saṃvat): Traditional northern Indian epoch commencing in 57 BCE.
  • Astronomical and calendar conversion: 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑚𝑜𝑛 𝐸𝑟𝑎 (𝐶𝐸) = 1420 −57 = 1363 𝐶𝐸

Depending on whether the regional lunar year began in Caitra (spring, March/April) or Kārttika (autumn, October/November, common in Rajasthan and Gujarat), the year spans 1363–1364 CE. Indologists universally cite 1363 CE as the standard reference date.

2. The Internal Genealogy of the Author (Verses 1.2–1.6)

The veracity of this colophon date is fully corroborated by the author’s own explicit autobiographical account in the opening section of the work (Kavi-vaṃśa-varṇana, Chapter 1, verses 1–12), which traces his lineage through three generations:

3. Historical Synchronism: King Hammīradeva of Raṇastambhapura (1282–1301 CE)

King Hammīra is a major, precisely documented figure of medieval Indian history: Hammīradeva Cāhamāna (Hammīr Dev), son of Jaitrasiṃha, the last great sovereign of the fortress of Raṇastambhapura (modern Ranthambore, Rajasthan): 1. Accession: Hammīradeva ascended the throne in 1282/1283 CE (1339 «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»), as recorded in Nayacandra Sūri’s historical epic Hammīra-mahākāvya (VIII.54) and Rājaśekhara Sūri’s Prabandhakośa (1349 CE). 2. The Siege of Ranthambore and Death (July 1301 CE): Hammīradeva refused to surrender rebel Mongol generals who had sought asylum with him, prompting Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi to mount a massive siege. Following the betrayal of generals Ratipāla and Raṇamalla and the performance of jauhar (mass self-immolation) by the Rajput women, Hammīra died fighting in the final sally on July 11, 1301 CE (3 Dhū al-Qa‘dah 700 AH). This event is documented to the exact day in contemporaneous Persian court chronicles by Amīr Khusrau (Khazā’in al-Futūḥ, 1311 CE) and Diyā’ al-Dīn Baranī (Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī). 3. Generational Chronology:

  • Generation I (Grandfather): Rāghavadeva was active as royal preceptor (rājaguru) during Hammīra’s floruit: around 1285–1301 CE.
  • Generation II (Father): Dāmodara was active in the subsequent generation: around 1310–1335 CE.
  • Generation III (Grandson/Author): Śārṅgadhara, born around 1315–1320 CE, compiled his 163-chapter anthology in full maturity by 1363 CE.
  • The 62-year span between Hammīra’s death (1301 CE) and the completion of the compendium (1363 CE) is a perfect historical match for two generational successions. 4. Court Archival Continuity: In Chapter 140 (Vīrarasa-nirvedaḥ, verse 3974), Śārṅgadhara quotes a stanza attributed directly to śrī-hammīrasya (“By King Hammīra”), uttered before his final heroic charge, proving that the family preserved authentic court and literary papers from the Cāhamāna dynasty.

4. Textual Boundaries (Terminus Post Quem and Ante Quem)

  • Terminus post quem: The anthology cites numerous late 13th-century poets, but contains zero references to 15th-century authors, Timurid invasions, or Sultanate developments after the mid-14th century.
  • Terminus ante quem: The Śārṅgadharapaddhati itself was heavily mined by 15th-century anthologists, most notably by Vallabhadeva in Kashmir for the Subhāṣitāvalī, which reproduces hundreds of «Śārṅgadharapaddhati» verses along with their author attributions. Early paper manuscripts of «Śārṅgadharapaddhati» survive from the 15th and 16th centuries (such as Royal Asiatic Society London MS Tod 32).

5. Critical Significance for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

Because the Dattātreyayogaśāstra lacks an explicit internal date, modern scholars rely on this external synchronism: 1. In chapters 157 and 158 of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE), Śārṅgadhara quotes «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verse 35 (Padmāsana) verbatim: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam // («Śārṅgadharapaddhati» verses 4376 & 4435); 2. He incorporates five of the ten mudrās of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» (verses 4408–4417 = «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» verses 131–145); 3. He adopts the distinctive prāṇāyāma methodology of the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra».

Conclusion: The date 1363 CE provides an absolute, historically verified terminus ante quem for the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra. This firmly establishes that the «Dattātreyayogaśāstra» was composed in the 13th century (around 1200–1300 CE), well over a century before the Haṭhapradīpikā (around 1400–1450 CE), solidifying its position as the earliest surviving systematic root text of Haṭhayoga.

Literary translation

English: Padmāsana, the Lotus Pose. Of the 8,400,000 postures taught in the universe, hear now the supreme one, revealed by Ādinātha. One places both feet sole-upward upon the thighs with care, and likewise rests both hands palm-upward between the thighs. Directing the gaze unswervingly to the tip of the nose, one presses the tongue up against the soft palate at the root of the uvula and locks the chin firmly down upon the chest. Then, drawing the breath inward smoothly to one’s full capacity, one gently inflates the abdomen, and thereafter exhales the breath with equal gentleness and control. This is Padmāsana, the sovereign remedy that destroys every bodily affliction—unattainable by the negligent, but mastered on earth by the truly resolute and wise.

Русский (литературный): Падмасана — поза лотоса. Из всех восьми миллионов четырехсот тысяч асан внимай наивысшей, открытой первозданным Владыкой Адинатхой. Практикующий с должным старанием укладывает обе стопы подошвами вверх на противоположные бёдра, а ладони разворачивает вверх и опускает между бёдер. Зафиксировав неподвижный взгляд на кончике носа, он кончиком языка упирается вверх, поднимая корень нёбного язычка, и плотно прижимает подбородок к грудине. Editors’ commentary

  1. The sole posture of the eightfold scheme: The Dattātreyayogaśāstra is the earliest known Sanskrit text to integrate the eight limbs (aṣṭāṅga) traditionally traced to Yājñavalkya with the bodily praxis of Haṭhayoga. Remarkable here is that although the text acknowledges the existence of 8,400,000 postures (caturaśītilakṣāṇi), it describes only Padmāsana. For Dattātreya, āsana does not denote gymnastic variety, but an unshakeable, locked seated posture capable of supporting the prolonged practice of internal breath control (prāṇāyāma) and channel purification (nāḍīśodhana).
  2. Integrated bandhas and dr̥ṣṭi: The description in verses 35–37 is not merely an external bodily shape; it integrates three key Haṭha techniques directly into the posture:
  • Nāsāgradṛṣṭi — fixation of the visual focus on the tip of the nose to still mental fluctuations.
  • Jālandharabandha (described descriptively as cibukaṃ vakṣaḥsaṃsthāpya — pressing the chin to the chest) to seal the upper throat channel.
  • Tongue lock (rājadantamūlaṃ jihvayā uttabhya), an early archaic precursor to the internal sealing of the cranial cavity, preventing the escape of prāṇa and stabilizing the nectar of the palate.
  1. The textual source of Haṭhapradīpikā 1.45–47: When Svātmārāma composed the Haṭhapradīpikā in the 15th century, he incorporated two descriptions of Padmāsana: the first («Haṭhapradīpikā» 1.44) was taken from the Vivekamārtaṇḍa (verse 8), describing the “bound” posture (baddhapadmāsana with hands crossed behind the back); the second («Haṭhapradīpikā» 1.45–47) was lifted verbatim from Dattātreyayogaśāstra verses 35–38. Svātmārāma recognized Dattātreya’s formulation as the definitive technical standard for pairing Padmāsana with prāṇāyāma.

This manuscript (Sampūrṇānanda Library, number 30109) is known in the scholarly literature as well — it is referred to, for instance, in an article on the Amaraughaprabodha (Journal of Indian Philosophy, 2019).

In the other manuscripts the dates survive in the colophons — they are transcribed in the editors’ draft files. A systematic check of the edition’s sources (the archive of the project’s LaTeX files of 20 May 2026) yielded five more dated copies. The dates stand in scribal notes at the end of the text and are written in the figures of the “saṃvat” era (that is the Vikrama era; subtracting 57 gives the year CE):

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, number 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

All five notes are the editors’ remarks in the comments to the draft files of the edition (they are not in the published digital reader); the era is not stated in the colophons themselves, and the final dates, checked against the manuscripts, will appear in the introduction to the printed book. But the picture is already clear: the earliest dated copies are 1496 (30109), 1626/27 (Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur, manuscript 2230), 1636/37 (29899), 1650/51 (30069), 1667/68 (Jodhpur 23709), 1745/46 (Jodhpur 02237).

Literal translation

Having placed the right foot upon the left thigh and the left in the same way upon the right, firmly holding [the toes] with both hands [passed] behind the back, placing the big toes [of the feet] at the heart and [pressing] the chin, one should gaze at the tip of the nose: this is called Padmāsana, which destroys disease in those who keep the vows.

Carefully placing the feet turned upwards upon the thighs and the hands turned upwards in the middle of the thighs, then fixing both eyes on the tip of the nose, raising the root of the uvula with the tongue, placing the chin on the chest, one should slowly [draw] the breath.

This Padmāsana is declared to destroy all diseases; hard to attain for anyone, it is attained on earth by the wise.

Joining the hands like a casket, firmly taking up Padmāsana, laying the chin tightly on the chest and [holding] meditation in the mind, again and again raising the apāna breath upwards [and] releasing the filled prāṇa breath, a man through the power of Śakti attains unequalled knowledge.

A yogi abiding in Padmāsana [and] filling with breath the openings of the channels — whoever thus stills the breath is liberated; there is no doubt here.

Literary translation

Padmāsana, the “lotus pose”. The right foot is laid on the left thigh, the left on the right; the hands are passed behind the back and firmly seize the big toes; the chin is pressed to the chest and the gaze rests on the tip of the nose. This pose shatters the ailments of those who keep the vows.

[The second description adds the locks:] the feet are turned up and laid on the thighs, the palms turned up in the middle of the thighs; the gaze rests on the tip of the nose; the tongue raises the root of the uvula and the chin is pressed to the chest while the breath is drawn slowly and evenly. This Padmāsana, destroyer of all diseases, is not given to everyone: on earth only the wise attain it.

Joining the palms like a casket, seated firmly in the lotus, the chin pressed to the chest and the mind held in concentration, [the yogi] again and again raises the apāna breath and releases the filled prāṇa breath — and by the power of [the goddess] Śakti gains unequalled knowledge. And whoever, seated in the lotus, fills the openings of the channels with air and stills the breath, is liberated — of this there is no doubt.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.45–46 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The syntax of this verse as we have presented it is incomplete: at its end pavanaṃ śanaiḥ, ‘the breath gradually’, is left hanging. In the source text, the Dattātreyayogaśāstra, the following verse completes the syntax with pūrayed, “one should inhale”. Either Svātmārāma chose to leave the verse hanging (the following verse in the Dattātreyayogaśāstra adds nothing about the form of the posture, which is the topic here) or the verse that completes the syntax fell out, perhaps because of a scribal error that happened early in the transmission. In the Dattātreyayogaśāstra verses 36 and 37 both end with pavanaṃ śanaiḥ, the repetition of which may have caused an eyeskip. The manuscript readings with vakṣa sthāpayet (Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur, manuscript Jodhpur 2230, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur and Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 29899, Vārāṇasī) or something similar (Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī) do not offer a solution to the incomplete syntax and do not indicate that Svātmārāma rewrote Dattātreyayogaśāstra 36 so that he could omit Dattātreyayogaśāstra 37. (The readings osthāpyot (Jodhpur 02235, Jodhpur and “pi-1” (the project’s working code; shelfmark not disclosed)) and otthāpya (Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30051, Vārāṇasī) are surprising and are perhaps Middle Indic forms in which Sanskrit ava- becomes o-.) In the absence of evidence that Svātmārāma included Dattātreyayogaśāstra 37 or wrote a coherent version of Haṭhapradīpikā 1.46, we have made sense of pavanaṃ śanaiḥ by adding “[draw in]” in our translation. Brahmānanda’s comment on the statement, ‘having raised the root of the uvula with the tongue’ (rājadantamūlaṃ ca jihvayā uttabhya) in 1.46 is worth noting. In the context of Haṭhayoga, one would assume this statement to be referring to a type of khecarīmudrā, in which the tongue lifts the root of the uvula, here called the ‘royal tooth’ (rājadanta, on the meaning of which see Mallinson 2007: 209 note 258). However, Brahmānanda understands it differently (synonyms omitted for clarity): rājadantānāṃ daṃṣṭrāṇāṃ savyadakṣiṇabhāge sthitānāṃ mūle ubhe mūlasthāne jihvayā uttambhya ūrdhvaṃ stambhayitvā/ gurumukhād avagantavyo’yaṃ jihvābandhaḥ/ Pushing against both roots of the front teeth on the left and right with the tongue […] — this fixation of the tongue has to be understood from the mouth of the teacher. Brahmānanda appears to have had in mind a probably older rule for meditation postures, according to which the tongue rests near the front teeth. One example of this is in Svacchandatantra (4.365 and the following page), which teaches a meditation pose called divyaṃ karaṇam, in which the tongue is to rest at the tip of the teeth (dantāgre jihvām ādāya). Other Tantric texts have this or similar rules, in which the tongue is supposed to rest either on the teeth or the palate, early examples being the Mṛgendrāgama, yogapāda 19 (dantāgre jihvām ādāya) and Mataṅgapārameśvaratantra, yogapāda 2.27 (tālumadhyagatenaiva jihvāgreṇa). Placing the tongue where it does not disturb the meditation seems quite appropriate for a ‘normal’ meditative practice (The rule of placing the tongue at the palate is also found in Īśānaśivagurudevapaddhati 18.120: tāluke jihvāṃ saṃyojya kiñcidvivṛtavaktro dantair dantān asaṃspṛśan ṛjukāyaḥ. For similar references in tantric and other works see Mallinson 2007: 17–24.). When the context is haṭhayogic physiology, placing the tongue at the uvula, which is the source of ‘nectar’, is more appropriate. Confusingly, yogic terminology includes many names for the uvula, and among these especially the term rājadanta may give rise to confusion, since, as we have seen, the tongue might also in some yoga systems be placed at the front teeth. Furthermore, the haṭhayogic khecarīmudrā has been described in manifold ways. Usually the tongue is said to be inserted into the cavity above the palate but in some cases it is placed at the uvula. Thus the tenth-century Mokṣopāya (5.55.14c) says that the tongue rests at the ‘source of the palate’ (tālumūlatalālagnajihvā) and the commentary, the Saṃsārataraṇi, on the parallel passage in Laghuyogavāsiṣṭha V.6.155, which reads tālumūlāntarālagnajihvā, explains that this means that the tongue is to be placed in the middle of the two regions of the palate, and that this is the nabhomudrā, alias ‘khecarī’ (tālumūlāntarālagnajihvamūlaḥ tālumūlayoḥ kākudamūladeśayoḥ āntare lagnam ālagnam jihvāmūlam yasyety anena nabhomudrā darśitā / yā hi khecarı̄ty ucyate). A little later in the Mokṣopāya (5.78.24ab) it is made clear that one should reach the uvula, ‘at the root of the palate’ (tālumūlagatāṃ yatnāj jihvayākramya ghaṇṭikām). In view of this background we must conclude that the author of the Jyotsnā was probably not aware of the yogic meaning of rājadanta and has tried his best to make sense of the passage, echoing the idea of the two roots of the palate (although his text is not talking about the palate), but then referring to the instruction of the teacher for practical details, probably noticing that his literal interpretation is somewhat opaque. In addition to his commentary on 1.46 (translated above), Brahmānanda’s comments on rājadantasthajihvāyām at 3.22 indicate that he thought the rājadanta refers to the front teeth (kutaḥ? yato dantānāṃ rājāno rājadantā rājadanteṣu tiṣṭhatīti rājadantasthāḥ, rājadantasthā cāsau jihvā ca tasyāṃ rājadantasthajihvāyāṃ bandhaḥ, taduparibhāgasya sambandhaḥ śastaḥ).

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.47 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

In this context, the word durlabham is somewhat ambiguous as to whether the posture is hard to perform or hard to acquire (the more usual meaning). In commenting on durlabham in Jyotsnā 2.74, Brahmānanda glosses it as duṣprāpam, which means ‘difficult to attain’ and ‘inaccessible.’ The comment added to this verse by Svātmārāma indicates that he prefers the second version of padmāsana, which derives from the Dattātreyayogaśāstra, rather than the one he has borrowed from the Vivekamārtaṇḍa.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.48 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The text at end of the second verse quarter is uncertain. Later witnesses, including Brahmānanda, have dhyāyaṃś ca but none of the early ones has this reading. We are taking dhyānaṃ with saṃnidhāya, but this renders tat problematic because it has no clear referent. In the source text, the Vivekamārtaṇḍa, tat appears to refer to the mokṣadvāra broken by kuṇḍalinī, which is mentioned in the previous verse. The two participles proccālayan and muñcan imply that the two things are happening at the same time, which is surprising but perhaps possible.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.49 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

It is not unusual to read forms from pṝ with the locative as seen in this verse (compare pūrayen mukhe in Amaraugha 21d) but it is not clear what is meant here. Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati 1.66 identifies the ten dvāras at which the ten principle channels terminate, and adds that all other channels terminate at the pores of the skin. Perhaps the primary nāḍīdvāras are intended here, and the yogi is to fill himself up with air through each of them. Alternatively, the meaning could be that by filling the dvāras with air, the yogi is blocking them, thus enabling the stilling of the breath as described in some secondary readings in the second half of the verse. This appears to be how this verse is understood at Haṭhatattvakaumudī 17.30 (compare Yogatattvopaniṣad 141–142). The Jyotsnā (1.49) has nāḍīdvāreṇa instead of nāḍīdvāreṣu, which Brahmānanda understands to mean “by way of the opening of the central channel (suṣumnāmārgeṇa)”. This yields the idea of filling up the central channel (as opposed to other channels), which is described in the Yogabīja (94–95). In order to make sense of this verse we have understood mārutaṃ to be read with the preceding line, as the object of pūrayan. Such enjambement is rare in haṭha texts (other than the Dattātreyayogaśāstra) and is absent from the verses which we have identified as authorial. We have not identified a source text for this verse but the enjambement and its unusual teaching suggest that there may be one. The unusual enjambement accounts for many of the variants in the second line, with Jodhpur 02235, Jodhpur reading mārutaṃ mārayed yas tu, invoking an alchemical metaphor for stilling the breath as found in the Amṛtasiddhi (see Mallinson 2021). The reading dhārayet found in some later witnesses may have resulted from confusion over the meaning of mṛ in this context.

Dating of the Matsyendrasaṃhitā

The manuscript is undated. Sensharma describes his palaeographic estimate as follows (introduction, page 1): “The ms bears no date but on paleographic examination the date for writing the ms appears to be about 1900 Vikram era corresponding to 1858 of the Common Era”

The conversion given in this sentence is inconsistent: 1900 of the Vikram era corresponds approximately to 1843–44 CE, while 1858 CE corresponds approximately to 1914–15 of that era. The sentence does not establish which number the editor intended. On the Vikram calendar.

The date of the manuscript must be distinguished from the date of composition and from the lifetime attributed to Matsyendranātha. On the range of scholarly opinions about the latter, Sensharma writes (introduction, page 9): “The upper limit of his time is fifth century of the Common Era and lowest limit is sometime in the thirteenth century of the Common Era”

On page 26, he offers his own conclusion: “Matsyendranātha therefore probably flourished in 5th or 6th century of the Common Era and lived for 400 years or so in the physical body and taught Yoga to Gorakṣanātha in the 10th century of the Common Era”

This is the editor’s interpretation, not an established historical conclusion. jamaṇā paganī pānī ḍābā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ ane ḍābā paganī pānī jamaṇā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ rākhīne ḍokane saheja nīcī namāvī besavuṁ, ne pachī banne hāthone pīṭhanī pachavāḍī pheravīne jamaṇā hāthathī jamaṇā pagano aṅgūṭho ne ḍābā hāthathī ḍābā pagano aṅgūṭho pakaḍavā ne dṛṣṭine nāsikānā agrabhāga upara ṭharāvavī te padmāsana kahevāya che.

джаман̣ā паганӣ пāнӣ д̣āбā сāтхал̣анā мӯл̣амāм̣ ане д̣āбā паганӣ пāнӣ джаман̣ā сāтхал̣анā мӯл̣амāм̣ рāкхӣне д̣окане сахеджа нӣчӣ намāвӣ бесавум̣, не пачхӣ банне хāтхоне пӣт̣ханӣ пачхавāд̣ӣ пхеравӣне джаман̣ā хāтхатхӣ джаман̣ā пагано ан̇гӯт̣хо не д̣āбā хāтхатхӣ д̣āбā пагано ан̇гӯт̣хо пакад̣авā не др̣шт̣ине нāсикāнā аграбхāга упара т̣харāвавӣ те падмāсана кахевāйа чхе.

Word-by-word analysis

jamaṇā paganī pānī — the right heel; ḍābā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ — at the root of the left thigh; ane — and; ḍābā paganī pānī — the left heel; jamaṇā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ — at the root of the right thigh; rākhīne — having placed; ḍokane saheja nīcī namāvī besavuṁ — sit with the neck inclined slightly downward; ne pachī — and then; banne hāthone pīṭhanī pachavāḍī pheravīne — bring both hands behind the back; jamaṇā hāthathī jamaṇā pagano aṅgūṭho — the right big toe with the right hand; ne ḍābā hāthathī ḍābā pagano aṅgūṭho — and the left big toe with the left hand; pakaḍavā — grasp; dṛṣṭine nāsikānā agrabhāga upara ṭharāvavī — fix the gaze on the tip of the nose; te padmāsana kahevāya che — this is called Padmāsana.

Literal translation (our interlinear)

The heel of the right leg at the root of the left thigh, and the heel of the left leg at the root of the right thigh having placed, the neck slightly downward inclining, sit, and then both hands behind the back turning, with the right hand the big toe of the right leg, and with the left hand the big toe of the left leg to grasp, and the gaze on the tip of the nose to fix — this is called Padmāsana.

Literary translation (ours)

“Place the heel of your right foot at the base of your left thigh, and the heel of your left foot at the base of your right thigh, and slightly tilt your neck, and sit down. Then, placing both hands behind your back, you should take hold of the big toe of your right foot with your right hand, and the big toe of your left foot with your left hand, and fix your gaze on the tip of your nose - this is called Padmāsana.”

Word-by-word analysis (ours)

ḍābā paganī pānī — the heel of the left leg; jamaṇā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṃ — at the base of the right thigh; ne — and; jamaṇā paganī pānī — the heel of the right leg; ḍābā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṃ — at the base of the left thigh; rākhavāthī — from placing; paṇa — too; e — this; āsana — posture; thāya che — comes about.

Literal translation (our interlinear)

By placing the heel of the left leg at the base of the right thigh, and the heel of the right leg at the base of the left thigh, this posture is also produced.

Additional passage — English (ours)

The posture may also be made by placing the left heel at the base of the right thigh and the right heel at the base of the left thigh.

Traditional effect

પિંડીની નસો દબાવાથી ઊંઘ આવતી નથી; શરીર સીધું રહેવાથી શ્વાસ સીધો ચાલે છે; ધ્યાન શ્વાસના મૂળમાં જાય છે; વૃત્તિ સ્થિર થાય છે; શરીરમાં લોહીનું ફરવું બરાબર થવાથી તંદુરસ્તી સારી રહે છે; ને આળસ થતી નથી.

piṃḍīnī naso dabāvāthī ūṃgha āvatī nathī; śarīra sīdhuṃ rahevāthī śvāsa sīdho cāle che; dhyāna śvāsanā mūḷamāṃ jāya che; vṛtti sthira thāya che; śarīramāṃ lohīnuṃ pharavuṃ barābara thavāthī taṃdurastī sārī rahe che; ne āḷasa thatī nathī.

пим̣д̣ӣнӣ насо дабāвāтхӣ ӯм̣гха āватӣ натхӣ; ш́арӣра сӣдхум̣ рахевāтхӣ ш́вāса сӣдхо чāле чхе; дхйāна ш́вāсанā мӯл̣амāм̣ джāйа чхе; вр̣тти стхира тхāйа чхе; ш́арӣрамāм̣ лохӣнум̣ пхаравум̣ барāбара тхавāтхӣ там̣дурастӣ сāрӣ рахе чхе; не āл̣аса тхатӣ натхӣ.

Word-by-word analysis (ours)

piṃḍīnī naso dabāvāthī — from the pressing of the nerves of the calves; ūṃgha āvatī nathī — sleep does not come; śarīra sīdhuṃ rahevāthī — from the body staying straight; śvāsa sīdho cāle che — the breath goes evenly; dhyāna śvāsanā mūḷamāṃ jāya che — attention goes to the source of the breath; vṛtti sthira thāya che — the activity of the mind becomes steady; śarīramāṃ lohīnuṃ pharavuṃ barābara thavāthī — from the blood in the body circulating properly; taṃdurastī sārī rahe che — health stays good; ne — and; āḷasa thatī nathī — lethargy does not arise.

Literal translation (our interlinear)

From the pressing of the nerves of the calves sleep does not come; because the body stays straight, the breath goes evenly; attention goes to the source of the breath; the activity of the mind becomes steady; because the blood in the body moves properly, health stays good; and lethargy does not arise.

Traditional effect — English (ours)

Pressure on the nerves or vessels of the calves prevents sleep; an upright body makes breathing regular; attention goes to the source of the breath; mental activity becomes steady; circulation and health improve; and lethargy does not arise.
