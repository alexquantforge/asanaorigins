Dating and Probable Place of Composition

The date of the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā is disputed; the dates of the manuscripts used for the printed edition do not in themselves establish the date of composition.

In the section “Provenience and Age” of his introduction, F. Otto Schrader associated the text with Kashmir. Among the arguments he adduces are the mention of yantra diagrams on birch bark in chapters 26 and 45; the image in chapter 39 of the sun emerging from an obstacle of snow; and the account of Muktāpīḍa in chapter 48. Schrader stressed that little can be said with certainty about the text’s age: he placed it in a group of Saṃhitās for which he proposed the eighth century CE as an upper limit. As a possible lower limit, he considered the text’s presentation of Buddhism as divided into three major schools; in Schrader’s view, this implies a date after the establishment of Mahāyāna, approximately after 300 CE. These are Schrader’s dating arguments, not an established date for the text. See the sections of his introduction on the manuscripts, provenance, and age, pp. 94–99 6.

In a 2017 article, D. Wujastyk proposes a date after 1000 CE, and possibly South India, for the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā 5. Marion Rastelli’s 2018 article abstract likewise associates the text’s composition with South India, narrowing the hypothesis to the twelfth–thirteenth centuries 3. These later proposals differ substantially from Schrader’s earlier dating framework; without additional evidence, they cannot be collapsed into one precise date or presented as an agreed consensus. Accordingly, this article does not assign a date to the āsana section itself.

2.1. Lower limit: early authors do not quote the text (argument from silence)

“Chronology

The VS can in no way be considered a very ancient work as ancient authors do not make any reference to the VS. For instance, Halāyudha (1050 A.D.) in his Brāhmaṇasarvasva and Lakṣmīdhara (1150 A.D.) in the Kalpataru do not mention VS anywhere; nor do they quote any verse from the same whereas both the authors quote liberally from other sources. Were the VS prior to these authors, they would have certainly quoted from VS; more so because (like the VS) both the authors advocate the doctrine of Jñānakarmasamuccaya (synthesis of Knowledge and Action) for the sake of salvation in support of which they quote from different works like the Yogayājñavalkya, Agnipurāṇa etc. The absence of such quotation of VS in their works is an indication that VS is the later origin, i.e. after the 12th century A.D.”

Compiler's note: testing the argument.

  • Lakṣmīdhara. I checked the Mokṣakāṇḍa of the Kṛtyakalpataru in the electronic text by P. Olivelle (UT Austin, 2022; based on the edition by K. V. Rangaswami Aiyangar, GOS CII, Baroda 1945; https://sites.utexas.edu/sanskrit/resources/dharmasastra/krtyakalpataru/). Results:
  • There are indeed no verses of the VS in it. The quotations marked “vasiṣṭhaḥ” are prose rules for hermits and ascetics, not verses of the yogic VS.
  • The Yogayājñavalkya is quoted precisely in defence of the combination of knowledge and action. Chapter 15 “atha jñānakarmopayogaḥ”, p. 146 by the page markers of the e-text: “yogiyājñavalkyaḥ | … jñānakarmasamāyogāt param āpnoti pūruṣam | pṛthagbhāve na sidhyeta ubhe tasmāt samāśrayet ||” (“Yogiyājñavalkya: … From the union of knowledge and action one reaches the supreme Person; it is not achieved by either separately, therefore one should resort to both”). I have confirmed this part of the editors' claim.
  • A search of the Mokṣakāṇḍa found no quotations marked as from the Agnipurāṇa. The editors may have meant other parts of the Kalpataru, or Halāyudha; this has not been checked.
  • I did not examine the other volumes of the Kalpataru.
  • Halāyudha. I have not seen the text of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva (ed. D. Bhattacharyya, Sanskrit Sahitya Parishad Series 29, Calcutta 1960).
  • The date “1050 A.D.” conflicts with other sources. According to them, the author of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva was a minister of King Lakṣmaṇasena of Bengal, 12th century: the description of the 1960 edition on exoticindiaart.com (“the court of king Lakṣmaṇasena of Bengal in the 12th century”); Swami Harshananda on hindupedia.com (“CE 1160-1200”). The same source distinguishes the lexicographer Halāyudha (10th c.) from the author of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva.
  • On these data Halāyudha lived after Lakṣmīdhara, not before him.
  • The argument itself is an argument from silence. According to the editors, both authors quote other works in support of jñānakarmasamuccaya; had the VS existed, they would have quoted it too. Birch 2018 (n. 92) explicitly judges this argument to be inconclusive (see §4).

2.2. Upper limit: later authors quote the text

“That the VS is not very recent becomes evident from the fact that authors like Nāgojībhaṭṭa (end of 17th century), Bhāvāgaṇeśa (1550-1600 A.D.) Vijñānabhikṣu (1550 A.D.) etc., quote the VS, Mallīnātha (1450 A.D.) also, who preceded Vijñānabhikṣu quotes VS in his commentaries on Raghuvaṃśa and Kumārasambhava.”

Wujastyk gives independent confirmation regarding Mallinātha (see §5.5). There Mallinātha is dated “fl. 1400” rather than 1450, and the passage is given as Raghuvaṃśa 13.52.

2.4. The editors' caveat: the text is multi-layered

“Yet we cannot definitely state that all the material that has come down to us in the extant VS belonged to that period. Some of the material must belong to an earlier period and the VS must have attained the present form passing through various stages like the epic Jaya which attained the form of the Mahābhārata.”

Immediately after this the book has a paragraph on the lineages of teachers:

“The various traditions regarding the lineage of the teachers who transmitted that lore differ to a large extent. But all these (except the Mahābhārata, Śāntiparva) accounts have Vasiṣṭha and Śakti in common. The various accounts are given below.”

The lineage charts follow. In the online copy their words (Brahma, Vasistha, Sakti, Parasara, Vyasa, Suka, Gaudapada, Govinda, Sankara) were mixed into the text of the section. The printed text confirmed that the word “Sakti” is not part of the quotation about HP 1.18: OCR had inserted it from the chart.

2.6. Relation to the Yogayājñavalkya and the Haṭhapradīpikā

“None of the scholars, quoting the stanzas found to be common to both the works, ascribe them to Yogayajnavalkya but to Vasistha. Similarly Bhavaganesa and Nagojibhatta (on Yogasutra I. 33) while quoting on the process of the purification of Nadis without Kumbhaka, ascribe it clearly to VS and not to YY. The three types of Pranayama, viz. Adhama, Madhyama and Uttama are also traced to Vasistha and not to YY. From these it becomes clear that YY is a later work and that it seems to have borrowed from VS.”

“The verses 11-22, of chapter 5 of the YY which describe the purification of Nadis, occur almost verbatim in the second chapter of the VS (V.S. 58-70). Also the words (kecit vadanti) meaning 'some say' occurring prior to verse 10 of the Vth chapter of YY seem to indicate Vasistha. From this it can be inferred that YY has borrowed from VS.”

“But the HP itself is a work later than the VS (the facts that lead us to this conclusion are given below). Hence, it can definitely be said that the VS is not a borrower.”

2.7. A discrepancy within the edition: “prior to 1150”

Checked against the printed edition. Below is the text transcribed from the printed book (section “Sūta Saṃhitā – Jñānayogakāṇḍa”). It confirms the reading “prior to 1150 A.D.”, i.e. this is not an OCR error. The beginning and end of the paragraph were missing in the online copy and are restored here.

“Sūta Saṃhitā- Jñānayogakāṇḍa

The Sūta Saṃhitā has been published by the Ānandāśrama, Pune. Whilst tracing the pedigree of teachers through whom the work is handed down, the JSS states that Śakti derived the lore from Vasiṣṭha. From this it can be inferred that the JSS borrowed from the dialogue between Śakti and Vasiṣṭha. The Sūta Saṃhitā (Muktikhaṇḍa, VII. 50-55) also refers to Cakradhara Mahānubhāva and to his disciple Subhaga. Cakradhara is assigned to 1250 A.D. But as the VS (as shown below) is prior to 1150 A.D. and as there is a period of hundred years intervening between the VS and Cakradhara, the JSS is certainly the borrower from the VS. We can also point out to influence of Vedānta upon the Sūta Saṃhitā. This also proves that the JSS is later than the VS.”

This paragraph gives three arguments that the JSS is later than the VS:

3. In the JSS lineage of teachers Śakti receives the teaching from Vasiṣṭha. From this the editors infer that the JSS borrows from the dialogue of Vasiṣṭha and Śakti.

4. The Sūta Saṃhitā mentions Cakradhara Mahānubhāva, whom the editors assign to 1250. The VS, according to them, is older than 1150.

5. The influence of Vedānta is noticeable in the Sūta Saṃhitā. No concrete examples are given in this paragraph.

Compiler's note.

  • Within the sentence the arithmetic is consistent: 1250 − 100 = 1150. So “1150” is not a misprint but an intended figure.
  • The reference “as shown below” points to the section “Chronology”. But there a different conclusion is reached: “after the 12th century A.D.” and “we can assign the VS to about 1250 A.D.” (§§2.1, 2.3). The same book thus gives two incompatible dates for the VS: “prior to 1150” and “about 1250”. The book does not explain this discrepancy.
  • Why this happened cannot be established from the printed text of 2005. One could check whether these passages agree in the 1984 edition.
  • Argument (2) depends on the date. If the VS belongs to “about 1250”, it is contemporary with Cakradhara rather than a hundred years older. Then this argument for the JSS borrowing from the VS loses its force. This is my observation, not the editors'.

Introduction. The Dating of the Mānasollāsa

2.1. The Starting Point: the Text Is Not Dated by Itself

No separate study of the dating of the "Mānasollāsa" exists. All existing datings are derivative: first the author, Sureśvarācārya, is dated, and then the date is transferred to the text. And the dating of Sureśvara depends on two things: which date for Śaṅkara is accepted, and whether the identity of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra is admitted.

This situation was recorded not in the scholarly literature but by the editor of the 19th century himself:

"Notwithstanding the attempts of several oriental scholars to fix the date of Śaṅkarācārya, there has been a certain amount of uncertainty regarding it, the date assigned to him varying from the sixth century before Christ to the eighth century A. D. The date of Sureśvarācārya, closely connected as it is with that of his reputed teacher, has not been more satisfactorily settled."

— Preface to the 1895 edition, p. 3 (keyed by the Ambuda portal)

And on the attribution as such — note this, it is a key fact:

"Nowhere in the hymn or the commentary is the author of either mentioned by name. The commentator refers in his Vārttika to the author of the hymn by mere general terms such as Kavi (author) and Guru (teacher). The style and the subject-matter, however, of the two works incline us to believe in their genuineness."

— ibid.

That is: the author's name is never once stated in the text, and the attribution rests on style and subject-matter alone. This must be borne in mind when reading any dating.

2.5. The Dispute on Which Everything Depends: Sureśvara = Maṇḍana?

If they are one person, the text falls in the early 8th century. If they are different, it falls in the 8th–9th centuries.

Against the identification:

  • M. Hiriyanna, "Sureśvara and Maṇḍana-Miśra", JRAS 55/2 (April 1923), pp. 259–263; continuation ibid., 56/1 (1924), pp. 96–97. Arguments: doctrinal divergences, and the Śṛṅgeri tradition, in which Maṇḍana and Sureśvara are different persons.
  • S. Kuppuswami Sastri — introduction to the critical edition of the "Brahmasiddhi" (1937; reprinted 1984).
  • S. N. Dasgupta — A History of Indian Philosophy, vol. 2 (1932).
  • S. S. Sharma, Advaita Tradition in Indian Philosophy (1997), pp. 290–291 — sets out the arguments of Hiriyanna and Kuppuswami Sastri.

For the identification:

  • R. Balasubramanian: "there is no convincing evidence to prove that Maṇḍana … are different persons" (as reported in Wikipedia, article "Sureśvara").
  • The Śṛṅgeri Maṭha — officially, in the documents of the maṭha and in hagiographies.

What the divergences consist in (after Sharma and Wikipedia):

  • where avidyā (ignorance) is located: in Maṇḍana it is in the jīva (the individual being), in Sureśvara in Brahman. This reflects the two sub-schools of Advaita — Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa;
  • the role of meditation: for Maṇḍana, knowledge from the mahāvākya ("great sayings" of the Upaniṣads) alone is not enough, meditation is required; for Sureśvara knowledge liberates directly, and meditation is an aid;
  • the attitude to sphoṭa (the doctrine that the meaning of a word manifests in the sound as such): Maṇḍana accepts śabdādvaita, Sureśvara ignores it.

2.6. What the Text Itself Says

In the ninth ullāsa, in the commentary on verse 23½, the author refers to his own commentary on Maṇḍana's "Brahmasiddhi". Reading from the scan of the 2008 edition, p. 119 (sources/scans/mss/2008-str119-celekom.png):

इति मण्डनसिद्धान्त एव सुरेश्वरस्यापि सम्मतः।तदनुषङ्गतः शाब्दापरोक्षवादोऽपि।विशदं चैतत् ब्रह्मसिद्धौ, तद्भूमिकायाञ्चास्मदीयायाम्॥२३१/२॥

"Thus, the doctrine of Maṇḍana is accepted by Sureśvara too; and, following from it, the doctrine of śabda-aparokṣa (immediate knowledge arising from the word). This is set out in detail in the 'Brahmasiddhi' and in our own bhūmikā to it."

Two circumstances must be kept in mind:

  1. The word अस्मदीया ("our") is the author's direct reference to his own commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi". This is internal evidence in favour of the identification, and it is usually quoted precisely in this dispute.
  2. It does not settle the question: the commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi" could also have been written by a pupil.

2.7. The Position of the Editors of 2008

The Hindi preface to the 2008 edition is a rare case of an editor setting out both the argument and the counter-argument.

2.8. The Result on Dating

A formulation that can be defended:

The "Mānasollāsa" is dated by its author, Sureśvarācārya, and not by internal data of the text. Depending on the date of Śaṅkara accepted (788–820 or c. 700) and on the outcome of the dispute over the identification of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra, the text is assigned either to the early 8th century (c. 700–730) or to the 8th–9th centuries. There is no independent dating of the text on internal grounds in the literature.

A supporting boundary that does not depend on the dispute: Vācaspati Miśra, writing according to Dasgupta in 842, quotes Sureśvara — which gives an upper bound ("terminus ante quem") in the 9th century.

  • Dr. Jason Birch points out that the Haṭhatattvakaumudī epitomizes the late medieval "encyclopedic turn" in yoga. This genre of comprehensive compendia, synthesizing hundreds of earlier sources (from Upaniṣads and Tantras to late medieval paddhatis), flourished among the scholarly Brahmin milieus of Vārāṇasī precisely in the first half of the eighteenth century.
  • Birch underscores that Sundaradeva's Sanskrit exhibits late classical syntax infused with the scientific medical lexicon of early modern India.

  • Prof. Bühnemann examined the evolution of āsana classification schemes in Indian traditions. 4. Sociological Synthesis by Dr. Mark Singleton:

  • Mark Singleton emphasizes that Sundaradeva represents a notable synthesis of two social roles: the practicing yoga adept and the high-caste, university-educated vaidya-paṇḍita. Such integration was characteristic of cosmopolitan Vārāṇasī in the eighteenth century, where Haṭha yoga techniques were absorbed into the orthodox curriculum of health, longevity, and therapeutics.

Literary Translation (Verses 22–28)

Placing the feet upon the opposite thighs with soles turned upwards, the yogin cups his hands in his lap, fixes his gaze upon the tip of the nose (nāsāgradṛṣṭi), curls the tip of the tongue against the palate behind the front teeth (nabhomudrā), and firmly locks the chin against the jugular notch of the chest (jālandharabandha). This is Padmāsana («the Lotus Pose»), the sovereign destroyer of all physical ailments. Almost unachievable for the common run of mankind, it is mastered in this world only by the truly wise. Properly seated in Padmāsana with hands cupped together, chin pressed to the chest, the practitioner withdraws his mind from external objects and meditates single-mindedly upon the Supreme Lord Hari. Again and again, he gently draws the descending apāna breath upward (mūlabandha) while directing the ascending prāṇa downward, equalizing them in the navel fire and forcing the vital energy into the central channel (suṣumnā).

PART I. THE HISTORICAL AND PHILOLOGICAL DATING (1363 CE)

The dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati to 1363 CE is universally recognized as one of the most solid chronological anchors in medieval Indology. It rests upon the convergence of three independent lines of evidence: 1. Direct manuscript colophons recording the exact year in the Vikrama Saṃvat calendar; 2. The compiler’s autobiographical genealogy spanning three generations in verses 1.2–1.6; 3. External epigraphical and historiographical synchronisms with the documented reign of King Hammīradeva Cāhamāna of Raṇastambhapura (r. 1282–1301 CE).

1. Manuscript Colophons and the Vikrama Saṃvat Era

3. Historical and Epigraphical Synchronism: King Hammīradeva (r. 1282–1301 CE)

The monarch named in verse 1.2 is the famed historical ruler Hammīradeva Cāhamāna of the fortress-kingdom of Raṇastambhapura (Ranthambhore): Documented Chronology:

  1. Accession (1282/1283 CE = VS 1339): Confirmed in the Hammīramahākāvya of Nayacandra Sūri (VIII.54) and the Prabandhakośa of Rājaśekhara Sūri (compiled in Delhi in 1349 CE).
  2. Epigraphical Proof: The Balvan stone inscription of VS 1345 (1288 CE) records Hammīra’s military expeditions (digvijaya) and religious gifts.
  3. Siege and Fall of Ranthambhore (11 July 1301 CE): Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi besieged Ranthambhore; after the heroic defense, the jauhar (mass self-immolation) of the Rajput women, Hammīra died fighting on the battlefield on 3 Dhū al-Qa’dah 700 AH (11 July 1301 CE). This catastrophe is recorded by contemporary Persian eyewitnesses: Amīr Khusrau (Khazā’in al-Futūḥ, 1311 CE) and Ḍiyā’ al-Dīn Baranī (Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī).

Generational Calculation:

  • Generation 1 (Grandfather): Rāghavadeva served as royal preceptor during Hammīra’s mature reign (c. 1285–1300 CE).
  • Generation 2 (Father): Dāmodara flourished in the subsequent period (c. 1310–1335 CE).
  • Generation 3 (Grandson/Compiler): Śārṅgadhara, born c. 1315–1320 CE, completed his vast compendium in 163 chapters at full scholarly maturity in 1363 CE.
  • Time Span: Between Hammīra’s death (1301 CE) and the completion of the anthology (1363 CE), exactly 62 years elapsed—the precise demographic span for two full generations from grandfather to grandson.

Personal Verse of King Hammīra Preserved:

In Chapter 140 (Vīrarasanirvedaḥ, verse 3974), Śārṅgadhara quotes an authentic stanza explicitly attributed śrī-hammīrasya (“By King Hammīra”), proving that the compiler’s family held the direct literary archives of the Cāhamāna royal court.

4. Philological Boundaries (Terminus Post Quem and Terminus Ante Quem)

  • Lower Boundary (Terminus post quem): The anthology incorporates late 13th-century poets, yet contains no trace of Timurid or 15th-century authors, establishing that it was compiled before 1400 CE.
  • Upper Boundary (Terminus ante quem): The Kashmiri florilegium Subhāṣitāvalī of Vallabhadeva (15th c.) quotes over 300 verses directly from the Śārṅgadharapaddhati.

5. Historiographical Consensus

  • 1873: Theodor Aufrecht (ZDMG, Vol. 27, pp. 1–120) analyzed Oxford and Berlin manuscripts and established the 1363 CE date.
  • 1888: Peter Peterson published the editio princeps (Bombay Sanskrit Series, No. 37), verifying the date from Pune government manuscripts.
  • Modern Consensus: Accepted without qualification by A. B. Keith (1928), M. Winternitz (1936), S. K. De (1947), Ludwik Sternbach (1974), James Mallinson (2011, 2024), and Jason Birch (2013).

Dating of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra (c. 1200 CE / 13th Century): Philological, Historical, and Stemmatic Evidence

The Dattātreyayogaśāstra (DYS) holds a singular place in the history of Indian thought as the earliest surviving Sanskrit treatise to systematically teach physical techniques under the name of Haṭhayoga and to integrate Patañjali’s eight-limbed system (aṣṭāṅgayoga) into an overarching hierarchy of four yogas.

In early 20th-century Indology, estimates of the text’s date fluctuated widely between the 13th and 15th centuries (J.N. Farquhar, P.K. Gode, H.D. Sharma, C. Bouy). However, the philological discoveries of the Haṭha Yoga Project (SOAS University of London / Oxford) and James Mallinson’s 2024 critical edition (Collection Indologie, n° 162, EFEO & IFP) have definitively anchored the text: the Dattātreyayogaśāstra was composed in about 1200 CE (late 12th to early 13th century, c. 1200–1250 CE).

Below is an exhaustive breakdown of the philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence supporting this dating.

1. The Upper Bound (Terminus Ante Quem: 1363 CE)

The upper chronological limit is firmly secured by external textual witnesses:

  1. The Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE): The anthological compendium of Śārṅgadhara is explicitly dated to 1420 VS (1363 CE) in its manuscript colophons. In chapters 157 and 158, Śārṅgadhara borrows directly from the DYS:
  • DYS verse 35 (Padmāsana) is quoted verbatim twice (ŚP vv. 4376 & 4435: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam //);
  • DYS verses 131–145 are borrowed almost word-for-word, including prāṇāyāma and five cardinal mudrās (Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandhara, Uḍḍīyāna, and Mūlabandha);
  • This proves that by 1363 CE, the DYS was already widely renowned and circulated as far north as Rajasthan.
  1. Svātmārāma’s Haṭhapradīpikā (c. 1400–1450 CE): Svātmārāma constructed large portions of the Haṭhapradīpikā directly from the DYS:

2. The Lower Bound (Terminus Post Quem: c. 1150–1200 CE)

The lower chronological limit is established by the DYS’s relationship with earlier yogic and tantric corpora:

  1. Reception and De-Buddhicisation of the Amṛtasiddhi (11th–12th c.):
  • The Amṛtasiddhi is the earliest surviving Sanskrit text to introduce the somatic physiology of bindu, the sun in the abdomen, the moon in the palate, and the three basic physical locks (Mahāmudrā, Mahābandha, Mahāvedha). As shown by Mallinson and Szántó (2021), the Amṛtasiddhi originated within a Buddhist Tantric (Mahāyāna/Vajrayāna) environment.
  • The DYS adopts this somatic system of bindu preservation and physical seals, but thoroughly adapts it to a Purāṇic Hindu and Vaiṣṇava framework, subordinating it to the worship of Viṣṇu/Hari and the sage Kapila. The assimilation and theological translation of a Buddhist tantric text into mainstream Brahmanical yoga required several decades, placing the DYS no earlier than the late 12th century.
  1. Systematisation of the Fourfold Yoga of the Amaraughaprabodha (12th c.):
  • The Amaraughaprabodha, attributed to Gorakṣanātha, first briefly enunciated the fourfold hierarchy of Mantrayoga, Layayoga, Haṭhayoga, and Rājayoga.
  • The DYS is the first text in history to systematically furnish each of these four yogas with comprehensive, practical instructions.
  1. The Earliest Textual Appearance of Vajrolīmudrā:
  • Neither the Amṛtasiddhi nor the 12th-century Vivekamārtaṇḍa / Gorakṣaśataka knows the practice of vajrolī. The DYS is the oldest surviving text to describe the physical absorption of sexual fluids and the subsidiary practices of sahajolī and amarolī (verses 150–178), marking a crucial historical development in early 13th-century yoga.

3. Stemmatic and Paleographical Evidence: Two Divergent Manuscript Branches

A pivotal argument for the early date of c. 1200 CE, demonstrated by James Mallinson in the 2024 critical edition, rests on manuscript stemmatics:

  • The transmission of the DYS is split into two deeply bifurcated recensions: 1. The Southern Recension ( ): σ Preserved in ancient palm-leaf codices in Grantha script (repositories in Chennai, Mysore, Thanjavur, and Pondicherry). This branch preserves the more archaic, complete text of 193 verses, including explicit instructions for women practitioners of vajrolī (strī-vajrolī). 2. The Northern/Western Recension ($ u$): Preserved in paper manuscripts in Devanāgarī script (Jodhpur, Baroda, Varanasi). In this branch, the text was abridged to 169–173 verses, and the explicit sexual-somatic verses were excised or softened.
  • In textual criticism, the geographic migration of a text from the Deccan/South to Rajasthan and Varanasi, across different writing media (palm-leaf to paper) and scripts (Grantha to Devanāgarī), accompanied by deep textual divergence and conscious editorial bowdlerisation prior to 1363 CE, demands a developmental span of at least 100 to 150 years of scribal transmission.

4. Historical and Geographical Context: The Deccan and the Dattātreya Cult

  1. Provenance: Mallinson demonstrates that the DYS was composed on the Deccan plateau (modern Maharashtra and neighbouring areas of Karnataka and Telangana).
  2. The Rise of Dattātreya Worship in the 12th–13th Centuries:
  • In first-millennium Purāṇic literature, Dattātreya is primarily an ancient sage (ṛṣi), the son of Atri and Anasūyā.
  • During the late 12th and 13th centuries, under the Seuna/Yādava dynasty of Devagiri in the Deccan, Dattātreya became the centre of an intense religious movement. He was adopted as a supreme divine manifestation by the Mahānubhāva-panth (founded by Cakradhara in the mid-13th century) and by the early Marathi Nāths (Jñāneśvar in his 1290 Jñāneśvarī lauds the lineage of Dattātreya and Gorakṣa).
  • This matches the concluding verse of the DYS (v. 193): harir eva svayaṃ sākṣād dattātreyo ’bhavat purā (“Hari [Viṣṇu] Himself directly became incarnate as Dattātreya”).
  1. Radical Egalitarianism (Verses 41–44): The DYS famously declares that anyone, regardless of caste, sex, or religious identity, can succeed in yoga:

brāhmaṇaḥ śramaṇo vāpi bauddho vāpy ārhato ’thavā / kāpāliko vā cārvākaḥ śraddhayā sahitaḥ sudhīḥ // 41 //

“Whether a Brahmin, an ascetic (śramaṇa), a Buddhist, a Jain (ārhata), a Kāpālika, or even a materialist (Cārvāka)—if wise and endowed with faith, one attains all perfections in yoga.” This inclusive, non-sectarian milieu is hallmark of early 13th-century Deccan asceticism prior to the hardening of sectarian orthodoxy.

Conclusion

The convergence of philological, stemmatic, and historical evidence firmly dates the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra to circa 1200 CE (early 13th century).

This early date cements the DYS as the foundational root text of the entire Haṭhayoga tradition, providing the direct textual and somatic archetype from which both the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) and the Haṭhapradīpikā (c. 1400–1450 CE) drew their core teachings.

Excursus: Textual and Historical Evidence for the Dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE) as an Absolute Terminus Ante Quem for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

The dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (ŚP) to 1363 CE is one of the most firmly established and methodologically robust chronological benchmarks in Sanskrit philology and the history of early Haṭhayoga. Because Śārṅgadhara borrowed verbatim into his anthology the definition of Padmāsana (vv. 4376 & 4435 = DYS v. 35), the techniques of channel purification and prāṇāyāma, and five cardinal mudrās (vv. 4408–4417 = DYS vv. 131–145: Mahāmudrā, Nabhomudrā, Jālandharabandha, Uḍḍīyānabandha, and Mūlabandha), the precise date of his compendium provides an incontrovertible upper limit (terminus ante quem) by which time the Dattātreyayogaśāstra must have already been in circulation.

The dating of 1363 CE is grounded in the convergence of three mutually independent bodies of historical and textual evidence (Aufrecht 1873; Peterson 1888; Keith 1928; Winternitz 1936; Sternbach 1974; Mallinson 2011, 2024; Birch 2013):

1. The Colophons and the Vikrama Era (1420 VS)

In the scribal colophons of the earliest extant manuscripts (first systematically collated by Theodor Aufrecht in 1873 from Oxford and Berlin codices and corroborated by Peter Peterson in 1888 from the Government Collection in Pune), the date of composition is recorded as:

𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑣𝑎𝑡 1420 𝑣𝑎𝑟ṣ𝑒 ... śā𝑟ṅ𝑔𝑎𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒ṇ𝑎 𝑝𝑎𝑑𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑡𝑖ḥ 𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑘ṛ𝑡ā (“In the year 1420 of the [Vikrama] Samvat era, this paddhati was compiled by Śārṅgadhara”).

  • The Vikrama Era (Vikrama Saṃvat): Traditional northern Indian epoch commencing in 57 BCE.
  • Astronomical and calendar conversion: 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑚𝑜𝑛 𝐸𝑟𝑎 (𝐶𝐸) = 1420 −57 = 1363 𝐶𝐸

Depending on whether the regional lunar year began in Caitra (spring, March/April) or Kārttika (autumn, October/November, common in Rajasthan and Gujarat), the year spans 1363–1364 CE. Indologists universally cite 1363 CE as the standard reference date.

2. The Internal Genealogy of the Author (Verses 1.2–1.6)

The veracity of this colophon date is fully corroborated by the author’s own explicit autobiographical account in the opening section of the work (Kavi-vaṃśa-varṇana, Chapter 1, vv. 1–12), which traces his lineage through three generations:

3. Historical Synchronism: King Hammīradeva of Raṇastambhapura (1282–1301 CE)

King Hammīra is a major, precisely documented figure of medieval Indian history: Hammīradeva Cāhamāna (Hammīr Dev), son of Jaitrasiṃha, the last great sovereign of the fortress of Raṇastambhapura (modern Ranthambore, Rajasthan): 1. Accession: Hammīradeva ascended the throne in 1282/1283 CE (1339 VS), as recorded in Nayacandra Sūri’s historical epic Hammīra-mahākāvya (VIII.54) and Rājaśekhara Sūri’s Prabandhakośa (1349 CE). 2. The Siege of Ranthambore and Death (July 1301 CE): Hammīradeva refused to surrender rebel Mongol generals who had sought asylum with him, prompting Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi to mount a massive siege. Following the betrayal of generals Ratipāla and Raṇamalla and the performance of jauhar (mass self-immolation) by the Rajput women, Hammīra died fighting in the final sally on July 11, 1301 CE (3 Dhū al-Qa‘dah 700 AH). This event is documented to the exact day in contemporaneous Persian court chronicles by Amīr Khusrau (Khazā’in al-Futūḥ, 1311 CE) and Diyā’ al-Dīn Baranī (Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī). 3. Generational Chronology:

  • Generation I (Grandfather): Rāghavadeva was active as royal preceptor (rājaguru) during Hammīra’s floruit: c. 1285–1301 CE.
  • Generation II (Father): Dāmodara was active in the subsequent generation: c. 1310–1335 CE.
  • Generation III (Grandson/Author): Śārṅgadhara, born c. 1315–1320 CE, compiled his 163-chapter anthology in full maturity by 1363 CE.
  • The 62-year span between Hammīra’s death (1301 CE) and the completion of the compendium (1363 CE) is a perfect historical match for two generational successions. 4. Court Archival Continuity: In Chapter 140 (Vīrarasa-nirvedaḥ, v. 3974), Śārṅgadhara quotes a stanza attributed directly to śrī-hammīrasya (“By King Hammīra”), uttered before his final heroic charge, proving that the family preserved authentic court and literary papers from the Cāhamāna dynasty.

4. Textual Boundaries (Terminus Post Quem and Ante Quem)

  • Terminus post quem: The anthology cites numerous late 13th-century poets, but contains zero references to 15th-century authors, Timurid invasions, or Sultanate developments after the mid-14th century.
  • Terminus ante quem: The Śārṅgadharapaddhati itself was heavily mined by 15th-century anthologists, most notably by Vallabhadeva in Kashmir for the Subhāṣitāvalī, which reproduces hundreds of ŚP verses along with their author attributions. Early paper manuscripts of ŚP survive from the 15th and 16th centuries (such as Royal Asiatic Society London MS Tod 32).

5. Critical Significance for the Dattātreyayogaśāstra

Because the Dattātreyayogaśāstra lacks an explicit internal date, modern scholars rely on this external synchronism: 1. In chapters 157 and 158 of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati (1363 CE), Śārṅgadhara quotes DYS verse 35 (Padmāsana) verbatim: uttānau caraṇau kṛtvā hy urusaṃsthau prayatnataḥ / ūrumadhye tathottānau pāṇī padmāsanaṃ tv idam // (ŚP vv. 4376 & 4435); 2. He incorporates five of the ten mudrās of the DYS (vv. 4408–4417 = DYS vv. 131–145); 3. He adopts the distinctive prāṇāyāma methodology of the DYS.

Conclusion: The date 1363 CE provides an absolute, historically verified terminus ante quem for the composition of the Dattātreyayogaśāstra. This firmly establishes that the DYS was composed in the 13th century (c. 1200–1300 CE), well over a century before the Haṭhapradīpikā (c. 1400–1450 CE), solidifying its position as the earliest surviving systematic root text of Haṭhayoga.

Literal translation

English: Hear the best among the eighty-four lakh postures; which posture was proclaimed by Ādinātha, that is told here. Having placed the feet turned upwards upon the thighs with effort, likewise having placed the hands turned upwards in the middle of the thighs, then [the yogin] should fix both eyes on the tip of the nose; and having propped up the root of the uvula with the tongue, having placed the chin firmly on the chest, [drawing in] the wind slowly according to capacity, he should fill the belly slowly; and afterwards, according to capacity, he should exhale the wind slowly. This is declared to be Padmāsana, destroying all diseases; hard to attain by just anyone, it is attained on earth by the wise.

Русский (буквальный): Среди восьмидесяти четырех лакхов асан внимай наилучшей: та асана, что была возвещена Адинатхой, излагается здесь. Расположив обе стопы вывернутыми вверх на бёдрах со старанием, равным образом расположив обе кисти рук вывернутыми вверх в середине бёдер, затем пусть устремит оба глаза на кончик носа; и языком подперев корень язычка, прижав подбородок к груди, медленно втянув дыхание в меру сил, пусть наполнит живот медленно; а затем, соразмерно силе, пусть выдохнет дыхание медленно. Literary translation

English: Padmāsana, the Lotus Pose. Of the 8,400,000 postures taught in the universe, hear now the supreme one, revealed by Ādinātha. One places both feet sole-upward upon the thighs with care, and likewise rests both hands palm-upward between the thighs. Directing the gaze unswervingly to the tip of the nose, one presses the tongue up against the soft palate at the root of the uvula and locks the chin firmly down upon the chest. Then, drawing the breath inward smoothly to one’s full capacity, one gently inflates the abdomen, and thereafter exhales the breath with equal gentleness and control. This is Padmāsana, the sovereign remedy that destroys every bodily affliction—unattainable by the negligent, but mastered on earth by the truly resolute and wise.

Русский (литературный): Падмасана — поза лотоса. Из всех восьми миллионов четырехсот тысяч асан внимай наивысшей, открытой первозданным Владыкой Адинатхой. Практикующий с должным старанием укладывает обе стопы подошвами вверх на противоположные бёдра, а ладони разворачивает вверх и опускает между бёдер. Зафиксировав неподвижный взгляд на кончике носа, он кончиком языка упирается вверх, поднимая корень нёбного язычка, и плотно прижимает подбородок к грудине. Затем, соразмеряя вдох со своими силами, он плавно и медленно наполняет воздухом брюшную полость, после чего столь же мягко и размеренно выдыхает. Сия поза именуется Падмасаной — сокрушительницей всех недугов; обычному человеку овладеть ею крайне нелегко, но мудрый и терпеливый непременно достигает в ней совершенства.

Editors’ commentary

  1. The sole posture of the eightfold scheme: The Dattātreyayogaśāstra is the earliest known Sanskrit text to integrate the eight limbs (aṣṭāṅga) traditionally traced to Yājñavalkya with the bodily praxis of Haṭhayoga. Remarkable here is that although the text acknowledges the existence of 8,400,000 postures (caturaśītilakṣāṇi), it describes only Padmāsana. For Dattātreya, āsana does not denote gymnastic variety, but an unshakeable, locked seated posture capable of supporting the prolonged practice of internal breath control (prāṇāyāma) and channel purification (nāḍīśodhana).
  2. Integrated bandhas and dr̥ṣṭi: The description in verses 35–37 is not merely an external bodily shape; it integrates three key Haṭha techniques directly into the posture:
  • Nāsāgradṛṣṭi — fixation of the visual focus on the tip of the nose to still mental fluctuations.
  • Jālandharabandha (described descriptively as cibukaṃ vakṣaḥsaṃsthāpya — pressing the chin to the chest) to seal the upper throat channel.
  • Tongue lock (rājadantamūlaṃ jihvayā uttabhya), an early archaic precursor to the internal sealing of the cranial cavity, preventing the escape of prāṇa and stabilizing the nectar of the palate.
  1. The textual source of Haṭhapradīpikā 1.45–47: When Svātmārāma composed the Haṭhapradīpikā in the 15th century, he incorporated two descriptions of Padmāsana: the first (HP 1.44) was taken from the Vivekamārtaṇḍa (verse 8), describing the “bound” posture (baddhapadmāsana with hands crossed behind the back); the second (HP 1.45–47) was lifted verbatim from Dattātreyayogaśāstra verses 35–38. Svātmārāma recognized Dattātreya’s formulation as the definitive technical standard for pairing Padmāsana with prāṇāyāma.

Dates of the manuscripts: what is known

Manuscripts with a firm date. One manuscript is explicitly dated in the edition, Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 30109, Vārāṇasī: in the comments on verses 1.24 and 1.56 the editors call it the “oldest dated manuscript”. The year is not printed in the reader; the project’s public materials give it as 1496 CE — only about a century from the supposed time of Svātmārāma’s authorship (the early 15th century). This manuscript (Sampūrṇānanda Library, no. 30109) is known in the scholarly literature as well — it is referred to, for instance, in an article on the Amaraughaprabodha (Journal of Indian Philosophy, 2019).

In the other manuscripts the dates survive in the colophons — they are transcribed in the editors’ draft files. A systematic check of the edition’s sources (the archive of the project’s LaTeX files of 20 May 2026) yielded five more dated copies. The dates stand in scribal notes at the end of the text and are written in the figures of the “saṃvat” era (i.e. the Vikrama era; subtracting 57 gives the year CE):

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash (MSPP), no. 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

All five notes are the editors’ remarks in the comments to the draft files of the edition (they are not in the published digital reader); the era is not stated in the colophons themselves, and the final dates, checked against the manuscripts, will appear in the introduction to the printed book. But the picture is already clear: the earliest dated copies are 1496 (30109), 1626/27 (MSPP 2230), 1636/37 (29899), 1650/51 (30069), 1667/68 (Jodhpur 23709), 1745/46 (Jodhpur 02237).

GOML R17256/SR8080, Chennai (in the apparatus — “epsilon-1”): “śrī śivāya namaḥ … gaṇapati svahasta likhitaṃ” — “written by the hand of Gaṇapati himself”, no date. Lalchand M-2089, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “pi-2”): only an ownership note, “pustakam idaṃ jājīrāmavyāsasya”. Lalchand M-6494, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “delta-3”): “…iti śrīmadātmārāmaviracitāyāṃ … upadeśaḥ … śubham astu sarvajagatām”. NGMPP B 62-20, Nepal (in the apparatus — “alpha-1”): the brief “śrīsahajasadguruātmārāmārpaṇa saṃpūrṇam”. NGMPP E-1528-1 / E-1527-7(4) (in the apparatus — “zeta-2”) has no colophon at all: the text ends exactly on leaf 346, and another work begins on the next leaf.

Literal translation

Having placed the right foot upon the left thigh and the left in the same way upon the right, firmly holding [the toes] with both hands [passed] behind the back, placing the big toes [of the feet] at the heart and [pressing] the chin, one should gaze at the tip of the nose: this is called Padmāsana, which destroys disease in those who keep the vows.

Carefully placing the feet turned upwards upon the thighs and the hands turned upwards in the middle of the thighs, then fixing both eyes on the tip of the nose, raising the root of the uvula with the tongue, placing the chin on the chest, one should slowly [draw] the breath.

This Padmāsana is declared to destroy all diseases; hard to attain for anyone, it is attained on earth by the wise.

Joining the hands like a casket, firmly taking up Padmāsana, laying the chin tightly on the chest and [holding] meditation in the mind, again and again raising the apāna breath upwards [and] releasing the filled prāṇa breath, a man through the power of Śakti attains unequalled knowledge.

A yogi abiding in Padmāsana [and] filling with breath the openings of the channels — whoever thus stills the breath is liberated; there is no doubt here.

Literary translation

Padmāsana, the “lotus pose”. The right foot is laid on the left thigh, the left on the right; the hands are passed behind the back and firmly seize the big toes; the chin is pressed to the chest and the gaze rests on the tip of the nose. This pose shatters the ailments of those who keep the vows.

[The second description adds the locks:] the feet are turned up and laid on the thighs, the palms turned up in the middle of the thighs; the gaze rests on the tip of the nose; the tongue raises the root of the uvula and the chin is pressed to the chest while the breath is drawn slowly and evenly. This Padmāsana, destroyer of all diseases, is not given to everyone: on earth only the wise attain it.

Joining the palms like a casket, seated firmly in the lotus, the chin pressed to the chest and the mind held in concentration, [the yogi] again and again raises the apāna breath and releases the filled prāṇa breath — and by the power of [the goddess] Śakti gains unequalled knowledge. And whoever, seated in the lotus, fills the openings of the channels with air and stills the breath, is liberated — of this there is no doubt.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.45–46 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The syntax of this verse as we have presented it is incomplete: at its end pavanaṃ śanaiḥ, ‘the breath gradually’, is left hanging. In the source text, the Dattātreyayogaśāstra, the following verse completes the syntax with pūrayed, “one should inhale”. Either Svātmārāma chose to leave the verse hanging (the following verse in the Dattātreyayogaśāstra adds nothing about the form of the posture, which is the topic here) or the verse that completes the syntax fell out, perhaps because of a scribal error that happened early in the transmission. In the Dattātreyayogaśāstra verses 36 and 37 both end with pavanaṃ śanaiḥ, the repetition of which may have caused an eyeskip. The manuscript readings with vakṣa sthāpayet (MSPP Jodhpur 2230, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur and Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 29899, Vārāṇasī) or something similar (Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 30109, Vārāṇasī) do not offer a solution to the incomplete syntax and do not indicate that Svātmārāma rewrote Dattātreyayogaśāstra 36 so that he could omit Dattātreyayogaśāstra 37. (The readings osthāpyot (Jodhpur 02235, Jodhpur and “pi-1” (the project’s working code; shelfmark not disclosed)) and otthāpya (Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 30051, Vārāṇasī) are surprising and are perhaps Middle Indic forms in which Sanskrit ava- becomes o-.) In the absence of evidence that Svātmārāma included Dattātreyayogaśāstra 37 or wrote a coherent version of Haṭhapradīpikā 1.46, we have made sense of pavanaṃ śanaiḥ by adding “[draw in]” in our translation. Brahmānanda’s comment on the statement, ‘having raised the root of the uvula with the tongue’ (rājadantamūlaṃ ca jihvayā uttabhya) in 1.46 is worth noting. In the context of Haṭhayoga, one would assume this statement to be referring to a type of khecarīmudrā, in which the tongue lifts the root of the uvula, here called the ‘royal tooth’ (rājadanta, on the meaning of which see Mallinson 2007: 209 n. 258). However, Brahmānanda understands it differently (synonyms omitted for clarity): rājadantānāṃ daṃṣṭrāṇāṃ savyadakṣiṇabhāge sthitānāṃ mūle ubhe mūlasthāne jihvayā uttambhya ūrdhvaṃ stambhayitvā/ gurumukhād avagantavyo’yaṃ jihvābandhaḥ/ Pushing against both roots of the front teeth on the left and right with the tongue […] — this fixation of the tongue has to be understood from the mouth of the teacher. Brahmānanda appears to have had in mind a probably older rule for meditation postures, according to which the tongue rests near the front teeth. One example of this is in Svacchandatantra (4.365f.), which teaches a meditation pose called divyaṃ karaṇam, in which the tongue is to rest at the tip of the teeth (dantāgre jihvām ādāya). Other Tantric texts have this or similar rules, in which the tongue is supposed to rest either on the teeth or the palate, early examples being the Mṛgendrāgama, yogapāda 19 (dantāgre jihvām ādāya) and Mataṅgapārameśvaratantra, yogapāda 2.27 (tālumadhyagatenaiva jihvāgreṇa). Placing the tongue where it does not disturb the meditation seems quite appropriate for a ‘normal’ meditative practice (The rule of placing the tongue at the palate is also found in Īśānaśivagurudevapaddhati 18.120: tāluke jihvāṃ saṃyojya kiñcidvivṛtavaktro dantair dantān asaṃspṛśan ṛjukāyaḥ. For similar references in tantric and other works see Mallinson 2007: 17–24.). When the context is haṭhayogic physiology, placing the tongue at the uvula, which is the source of ‘nectar’, is more appropriate. Confusingly, yogic terminology includes many names for the uvula, and among these especially the term rājadanta may give rise to confusion, since, as we have seen, the tongue might also in some yoga systems be placed at the front teeth. Furthermore, the haṭhayogic khecarīmudrā has been described in manifold ways. Usually the tongue is said to be inserted into the cavity above the palate but in some cases it is placed at the uvula. Thus the tenth-century Mokṣopāya (5.55.14c) says that the tongue rests at the ‘source of the palate’ (tālumūlatalālagnajihvā) and the commentary, the Saṃsārataraṇi, on the parallel passage in Laghuyogavāsiṣṭha V.6.155, which reads tālumūlāntarālagnajihvā, explains that this means that the tongue is to be placed in the middle of the two regions of the palate, and that this is the nabhomudrā, alias ‘khecarī’ (tālumūlāntarālagnajihvamūlaḥ tālumūlayoḥ kākudamūladeśayoḥ āntare lagnam ālagnam jihvāmūlam yasyety anena nabhomudrā darśitā / yā hi khecarı̄ty ucyate). A little later in the Mokṣopāya (5.78.24ab) it is made clear that one should reach the uvula, ‘at the root of the palate’ (tālumūlagatāṃ yatnāj jihvayākramya ghaṇṭikām). In view of this background we must conclude that the author of the Jyotsnā was probably not aware of the yogic meaning of rājadanta and has tried his best to make sense of the passage, echoing the idea of the two roots of the palate (although his text is not talking about the palate), but then referring to the instruction of the teacher for practical details, probably noticing that his literal interpretation is somewhat opaque. In addition to his commentary on 1.46 (translated above), Brahmānanda’s comments on rājadantasthajihvāyām at 3.22 indicate that he thought the rājadanta refers to the front teeth (kutaḥ? yato dantānāṃ rājāno rājadantā rājadanteṣu tiṣṭhatīti rājadantasthāḥ, rājadantasthā cāsau jihvā ca tasyāṃ rājadantasthajihvāyāṃ bandhaḥ, taduparibhāgasya sambandhaḥ śastaḥ).

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.47 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

In this context, the word durlabham is somewhat ambiguous as to whether the posture is hard to perform or hard to acquire (the more usual meaning). In commenting on durlabham in Jyotsnā 2.74, Brahmānanda glosses it as duṣprāpam, which means ‘difficult to attain’ and ‘inaccessible.’ The comment added to this verse by Svātmārāma indicates that he prefers the second version of padmāsana, which derives from the Dattātreyayogaśāstra, rather than the one he has borrowed from the Vivekamārtaṇḍa.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.48 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The text at end of the second verse quarter is uncertain. Later witnesses, including Brahmānanda, have dhyāyaṃś ca but none of the early ones has this reading. We are taking dhyānaṃ with saṃnidhāya, but this renders tat problematic because it has no clear referent. In the source text, the Vivekamārtaṇḍa, tat appears to refer to the mokṣadvāra broken by kuṇḍalinī, which is mentioned in the previous verse. The two participles proccālayan and muñcan imply that the two things are happening at the same time, which is surprising but perhaps possible.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.49 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

It is not unusual to read forms from pṝ with the locative as seen in this verse (cf. pūrayen mukhe in Amaraugha 21d) but it is not clear what is meant here. Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati 1.66 identifies the ten dvāras at which the ten principle channels terminate, and adds that all other channels terminate at the pores of the skin. Perhaps the primary nāḍīdvāras are intended here, and the yogi is to fill himself up with air through each of them. Alternatively, the meaning could be that by filling the dvāras with air, the yogi is blocking them, thus enabling the stilling of the breath as described in some secondary readings in the second half of the verse. This appears to be how this verse is understood at Haṭhatattvakaumudī 17.30 (cf. Yogatattvopaniṣad 141–142). The Jyotsnā (1.49) has nāḍīdvāreṇa instead of nāḍīdvāreṣu, which Brahmānanda understands to mean “by way of the opening of the central channel (suṣumnāmārgeṇa)”. This yields the idea of filling up the central channel (as opposed to other channels), which is described in the Yogabīja (94–95). In order to make sense of this verse we have understood mārutaṃ to be read with the preceding line, as the object of pūrayan. Such enjambement is rare in haṭha texts (other than the Dattātreyayogaśāstra) and is absent from the verses which we have identified as authorial. We have not identified a source text for this verse but the enjambement and its unusual teaching suggest that there may be one. The unusual enjambement accounts for many of the variants in the second line, with Jodhpur 02235, Jodhpur reading mārutaṃ mārayed yas tu, invoking an alchemical metaphor for stilling the breath as found in the Amṛtasiddhi (see Mallinson 2021). The reading dhārayet found in some later witnesses may have resulted from confusion over the meaning of mṛ in this context.
