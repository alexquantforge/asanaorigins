Dating of the text

The "Mānasollāsa" is dated by its author, Sureśvarācārya, and not by internal data of the text. Depending on the date of Śaṅkara accepted (788–820 or around 700) and on the outcome of the dispute over the identification of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra, the text is assigned either to the early 8th century (around 700–730) or to the 8th–9th centuries. There is no independent dating of the text on internal grounds in the literature.

Introduction. The Dating of the Mānasollāsa

2.1. The Starting Point: the Text Is Not Dated by Itself

No separate study of the dating of the "Mānasollāsa" exists. All existing datings are derivative: first the author, Sureśvarācārya, is dated, and then the date is transferred to the text. And the dating of Sureśvara depends on two things: which date for Śaṅkara is accepted, and whether the identity of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra is admitted.

This situation was recorded not in the scholarly literature but by the editor of the 19th century himself:

"Notwithstanding the attempts of several oriental scholars to fix the date of Śaṅkarācārya, there has been a certain amount of uncertainty regarding it, the date assigned to him varying from the sixth century before Christ to the eighth century of the Common Era. The date of Sureśvarācārya, closely connected as it is with that of his reputed teacher, has not been more satisfactorily settled."

— Preface to the 1895 edition, page 3 (keyed by the Ambuda portal)

And on the attribution as such — note this, it is a key fact:

"Nowhere in the hymn or the commentary is the author of either mentioned by name. The commentator refers in his Vārttika to the author of the hymn by mere general terms such as Kavi (author) and Guru (teacher). The style and the subject-matter, however, of the two works incline us to believe in their genuineness."

— there

That is: the author's name is never once stated in the text, and the attribution rests on style and subject-matter alone. This must be borne in mind when reading any dating.

2.3. The Camp of "Śaṅkara 788–820 ⇒ Sureśvara around 800"

S. N. Dasgupta, A History of Indian Philosophy, volume 2 (1932), the chapter on the Śaṅkara school. Dates: Sureśvara — 800 CE, Sarvajñātman — 900 CE. Arguments:

"Hence there seems to be no reason why the traditionally accepted view that Śaṅkara was born in Saṃvat 844, or of the Common Era 788, or Kali age 3889, should be given up."

"Taking the approximate date of Śaṅkara's death to be about of the Common Era 820 and taking into consideration that Sureśvara, the teacher of Sarvajñātman, occupied his high pontifical position for a long time, the supposition that Sarvajñātman lived in of the Common Era 900 may not be very far wrong."

"…Vācaspati, who probably wrote his earlier work the Nyāya-sūcī-nibandha in of the Common Era 842, also wrote his commentary on Maṇḍana's Brahma-siddhi when Sureśvara was occupying the pontifical position."

And his own caveat:

"The exact date of neither Sureśvara nor Sarvajñātma can be definitely determined."

(All four from Dasgupta, A History of Indian Philosophy, volume 2; from the text at wisdomlib, "A History of Indian Philosophy", volume 2.)

2.5. The Dispute on Which Everything Depends: Sureśvara = Maṇḍana?

If they are one person, the text falls in the early 8th century. If they are different, it falls in the 8th–9th centuries.

Against the identification:

  • M. Hiriyanna, "Sureśvara and Maṇḍana-Miśra", JRAS 55/2 (April 1923), pages 259–263; continuation there, 56/1 (1924), pages 96–97. Arguments: doctrinal divergences, and the Śṛṅgeri tradition, in which Maṇḍana and Sureśvara are different persons.
  • S. Kuppuswami Sastri — introduction to the critical edition of the "Brahmasiddhi" (1937; reprinted 1984).
  • S. N. Dasgupta — A History of Indian Philosophy, volume 2 (1932).
  • S. S. Sharma, Advaita Tradition in Indian Philosophy (1997), pages 290–291 — sets out the arguments of Hiriyanna and Kuppuswami Sastri.

For the identification:

  • R. Balasubramanian: "there is no convincing evidence to prove that Maṇḍana … are different persons" (as reported in Wikipedia, article "Sureśvara").
  • The Śṛṅgeri Maṭha — officially, in the documents of the maṭha and in hagiographies.

What the divergences consist in (after Sharma and Wikipedia):

  • where avidyā (ignorance) is located: in Maṇḍana it is in the jīva (the individual being), in Sureśvara in Brahman. This reflects the two sub-schools of Advaita — Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa;
  • the role of meditation: for Maṇḍana, knowledge from the mahāvākya ("great sayings" of the Upaniṣads) alone is not enough, meditation is required; for Sureśvara knowledge liberates directly, and meditation is an aid;
  • the attitude to sphoṭa (the doctrine that the meaning of a word manifests in the sound as such): Maṇḍana accepts śabdādvaita, Sureśvara ignores it.

2.6. What the Text Itself Says

In the ninth ullāsa, in the commentary on verse 23½, the author refers to his own commentary on Maṇḍana's "Brahmasiddhi". Reading from the scan of the 2008 edition, page 119 (sources/scans/mss/2008-str119-celekom.png):

इति मण्डनसिद्धान्त एव सुरेश्वरस्यापि सम्मतः।तदनुषङ्गतः शाब्दापरोक्षवादोऽपि।विशदं चैतत् ब्रह्मसिद्धौ, तद्भूमिकायाञ्चास्मदीयायाम्॥२३१/२॥

"Thus, the doctrine of Maṇḍana is accepted by Sureśvara too; and, following from it, the doctrine of śabda-aparokṣa (immediate knowledge arising from the word). This is set out in detail in the 'Brahmasiddhi' and in our own bhūmikā to it."

Two circumstances must be kept in mind:

  1. The word अस्मदीया ("our") is the author's direct reference to his own commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi". This is internal evidence in favour of the identification, and it is usually quoted precisely in this dispute.
  2. It does not settle the question: the commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi" could also have been written by a pupil.

2.7. The Position of the Editors of 2008

The Hindi preface to the 2008 edition is a rare case of an editor setting out both the argument and the counter-argument.

«कुछ आधुनिकों ने इस स्तोत्र को अभिनवगुप्त के प्रत्यभिज्ञादर्शन का ग्रन्थ माना है।»

— "Some modern [scholars] have regarded this stotra as a text of Abhinavagupta's Pratyabhijñā-darśana" (page i). It was precisely to answer this that the commentary "Mānasollāsa-vardhinī" was commissioned.

«एतिहासिक दृष्टि से मण्डन आचार्य के पूर्ववर्ती थे।»

— "From the historical point of view Maṇḍana was a predecessor of the Ācārya [Śaṅkara]" (page ii).

The counter-argument against the identification, given in the same place: in the "Bṛhadāraṇyaka-vārttika" Maṇḍana is spoken of in the third person with the pejorative पण्डितम्मन्य ("one who fancies himself learned"), which no one writes of himself.

1.2. The Chronological Problem: Scholarly Arguments and Dating Debate

The historical dating of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī has been examined by leading Sanskritists and historians of yoga:

1. Arguments of Doctor M. L. Gharote and Doctor M. Venkata Reddy (Critical Edition, 2007):

  • Terminus post quem (Earliest possible date): Sundaradeva directly cites late seventeenth-century works:
  • «Haṭharatnāvalī» (Haṭharatnāvalī) by Śrīnivāsa (Śrīnivāsa), composed circa 1670–1695 CE. Sundaradeva adopts its specialized terminology, āsana lists, and quotes its verses;
  • «Kumbhakapaddhati» (Kumbhakapaddhati) by Raghuvīra (Raghuvīra), datable to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century;
  • «Yogacandrikā» (Yogacandrikā) and «Yogacintāmaṇi» (Yogacintāmaṇi) by Śivānanda Sarasvatī.
  • Terminus ante quem (Latest possible date): The oldest and only complete dated manuscript witness (manuscript number 4235, Scindia Oriental Institute, Ujjain) preserves a scribal colophon with the exact date of completion: Saṃvat 1846 (Vikrama Saṃvat), month of Vaiśākha, dark fortnight, 10th tithi, Tuesday, corresponding to April–May 1789 CE.
  • Gharote's Conclusion: The treatise was composed between 1700 and 1750 CE (first half of the eighteenth century).

2. Textological Arguments of Doctor Jason Birch (Oxford University / Haṭha Yoga Project):

  • Doctor Jason Birch points out that the Haṭhatattvakaumudī epitomizes the late medieval "encyclopedic turn" in yoga. This genre of comprehensive compendia, synthesizing hundreds of earlier sources (from Upaniṣads and Tantras to late medieval paddhatis), flourished among the scholarly Brahmin milieus of Vārāṇasī precisely in the first half of the eighteenth century.
  • Birch underscores that Sundaradeva's Sanskrit exhibits late classical syntax infused with the scientific medical lexicon of early modern India.

3. Iconographic and Structural Analysis of Professor Gudrun Bühnemann:

  • Professor Bühnemann examined the evolution of āsana classification schemes in Indian traditions. In Chapter 7 of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī, Sundaradeva retains the canonical core of 15 postures (inherited from Chapter 1 of Svātmārāma's Haṭhapradīpikā), yet reinterprets them within a therapeutic methodology and introduces distinctive technical nuances (for example, yonyāsana as an advanced modification of siddhāsana, specialized breath management during padmāsana). This marks a systematic transition before the proliferation of vast catalogs of 84 or more āsanas in late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century manuals.

4. Sociological Synthesis by Doctor Mark Singleton:

  • Mark Singleton emphasizes that Sundaradeva represents a notable synthesis of two social roles: the practicing yoga adept and the high-caste, university-educated vaidya-paṇḍita. Such integration was characteristic of cosmopolitan Vārāṇasī in the eighteenth century, where Haṭha yoga techniques were absorbed into the orthodox curriculum of health, longevity, and therapeutics.

Literary Translation (Verses 33–40)

Firmly lodging the heel of one foot against the perineal center (yonisthāna), the practitioner rests the other foot directly above the pubic bone. Fixing his unwavering gaze upon the space between the eyebrows (śāmbhavīmudrā), the steadfast yogin rests in contemplative stillness. The ancient masters declare this pristine, sacred posture to be Siddhāsana («the Accomplished Pose»). The same discipline is known as Yonyāsana: pressing the left heel forcefully against the perineum and resting the right heel above the generative organ, the yogin locks his chin to his chest in jālandharabandha and centers his unblinking gaze upon the tip of the nose. What achievement in the three worlds remains beyond the reach of one who has mastered Yonyāsana? Maintaining conservation of vital energy, practicing moderate diet, free from worldly entanglement, and whole-heartedly dedicated to yoga, he becomes an accomplished siddha in the span of just one year — on this point let there be no doubt! Just as clinical medical treatment never fails in a patient who strictly adheres to the physician's prescribed diet, even so the science of yoga invariably attains fruition in one who observes moderate eating. Why should the yogin exhaust himself with a dizzying maze of inverted headstands (śīrṣāsana)? Devoted entirely to Yonyāsana and mastering the vital breath, he is instantly purified of all sins and karmic afflictions. Among all the postures taught in yoga, Siddhāsana reigns sovereign: just as no river on earth equals the sacred Gaṅgā, so no āsana in existence surpasses Siddhāsana.

PART I. THE HISTORICAL AND PHILOLOGICAL DATING (1363 CE)

The dating of the Śārṅgadharapaddhati to 1363 CE is universally recognized as one of the most solid chronological anchors in medieval Indology. It rests upon the convergence of three independent lines of evidence: 1. Direct manuscript colophons recording the exact year in the Vikrama Saṃvat calendar; 2. The compiler’s autobiographical genealogy spanning three generations in verses 1.2–1.6; 3. External epigraphical and historiographical synchronisms with the documented reign of King Hammīradeva Cāhamāna of Raṇastambhapura (r. 1282–1301 CE).

1. Manuscript Colophons and the Vikrama Saṃvat Era

In the colophons of the earliest preserved manuscripts of the anthology—identified by Theodor Aufrecht in 1873 and Peter Peterson in 1888—the exact formulaic date of completion is preserved:

𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑣𝑎𝑡 1420 𝑣𝑎𝑟ṣ𝑒 ... śā𝑟ṅ𝑔𝑎𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑒ṇ𝑎 𝑝𝑎𝑑𝑑ℎ𝑎𝑡𝑖ḥ 𝑠𝑎ṃ𝑘ṛ𝑡ā

(“In the year 1420 of the Saṃvat era, this paddhati was composed by Śārṅgadhara”).

Calendar Conversion:

  • The Vikrama Saṃvat Era: An epoch standardly counted from 57 BCE.
  • Direct Mathematical Conversion:

𝑌𝑒𝑎𝑟 𝐶𝐸= 1420 −57 = 1363 𝐶𝐸

  • Seasonal Adjustment: Depending on whether the local North/West Indian calendar followed the Caitrādi reckoning (new year beginning in March/April) or the Kārttikādi reckoning (beginning in October/November, customary in Rajasthan and Gujarat), the year 1420 corresponds to 1363–1364 CE. Modern scholarly consensus standardly cites 1363 CE.

3. Historical and Epigraphical Synchronism: King Hammīradeva (r. 1282–1301 CE)

The monarch named in verse 1.2 is the famed historical ruler Hammīradeva Cāhamāna of the fortress-kingdom of Raṇastambhapura (Ranthambhore): Documented Chronology:

  1. Accession (1282/1283 CE = Vikrama era 1339): Confirmed in the Hammīramahākāvya of Nayacandra Sūri (VIII.54) and the Prabandhakośa of Rājaśekhara Sūri (compiled in Delhi in 1349 CE).
  2. Epigraphical Proof: The Balvan stone inscription of Vikrama era 1345 (1288 CE) records Hammīra’s military expeditions (digvijaya) and religious gifts.
  3. Siege and Fall of Ranthambhore (11 July 1301 CE): Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi besieged Ranthambhore; after the heroic defense, the jauhar (mass self-immolation) of the Rajput women, Hammīra died fighting on the battlefield on 3 Dhū al-Qa’dah 700 AH (11 July 1301 CE). This catastrophe is recorded by contemporary Persian eyewitnesses: Amīr Khusrau (Khazā’in al-Futūḥ, 1311 CE) and Ḍiyā’ al-Dīn Baranī (Tārīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī).

Generational Calculation:

  • Generation 1 (Grandfather): Rāghavadeva served as royal preceptor during Hammīra’s mature reign (around 1285–1300 CE).
  • Generation 2 (Father): Dāmodara flourished in the subsequent period (around 1310–1335 CE).
  • Generation 3 (Grandson/Compiler): Śārṅgadhara, born around 1315–1320 CE, completed his vast compendium in 163 chapters at full scholarly maturity in 1363 CE.
  • Time Span: Between Hammīra’s death (1301 CE) and the completion of the anthology (1363 CE), exactly 62 years elapsed—the precise demographic span for two full generations from grandfather to grandson.

Personal Verse of King Hammīra Preserved:

In Chapter 140 (Vīrarasanirvedaḥ, verse 3974), Śārṅgadhara quotes an authentic stanza explicitly attributed śrī-hammīrasya (“By King Hammīra”), proving that the compiler’s family held the direct literary archives of the Cāhamāna royal court.

4. Philological Boundaries (Terminus Post Quem and Terminus Ante Quem)

  • Lower Boundary (Terminus post quem): The anthology incorporates late 13th-century poets, yet contains no trace of Timurid or 15th-century authors, establishing that it was compiled before 1400 CE.
  • Upper Boundary (Terminus ante quem): The Kashmiri florilegium Subhāṣitāvalī of Vallabhadeva (15th century) quotes over 300 verses directly from the Śārṅgadharapaddhati.

5. Historiographical Consensus

  • 1873: Theodor Aufrecht (ZDMG, volume 27, pages 1–120) analyzed Oxford and Berlin manuscripts and established the 1363 CE date.
  • 1888: Peter Peterson published the editio princeps (Bombay Sanskrit Series, number 37), verifying the date from Pune government manuscripts.
  • Modern Consensus: Accepted without qualification by A. B. Keith (1928), M. Winternitz (1936), S. K. De (1947), Ludwik Sternbach (1974), James Mallinson (2011, 2024), and Jason Birch (2013).

Dates of the manuscripts: what is known

Manuscripts with a firm date. One manuscript is explicitly dated in the edition, Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī: in the comments on verses 1.24 and 1.56 the editors call it the “oldest dated manuscript”. The year is not printed in the reader; the project’s public materials give it as 1496 CE — only about a century from the supposed time of Svātmārāma’s authorship (the early 15th century). This manuscript (Sampūrṇānanda Library, number 30109) is known in the scholarly literature as well — it is referred to, for instance, in an article on the Amaraughaprabodha (Journal of Indian Philosophy, 2019).

In the other manuscripts the dates survive in the colophons — they are transcribed in the editors’ draft files. A systematic check of the edition’s sources (the archive of the project’s LaTeX files of 20 May 2026) yielded five more dated copies. The dates stand in scribal notes at the end of the text and are written in the figures of the “saṃvat” era (that is the Vikrama era; subtracting 57 gives the year CE):

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, number 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

All five notes are the editors’ remarks in the comments to the draft files of the edition (they are not in the published digital reader); the era is not stated in the colophons themselves, and the final dates, checked against the manuscripts, will appear in the introduction to the printed book. But the picture is already clear: the earliest dated copies are 1496 (30109), 1626/27 (Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur, manuscript 2230), 1636/37 (29899), 1650/51 (30069), 1667/68 (Jodhpur 23709), 1745/46 (Jodhpur 02237).

Literal translation

Having placed the heel (the base of the foot) in the perineum and firmly set one foot upon the penis, [the yogi], with the mind fixed on one, holding the body even, motionless as a post, with the senses restrained, gazing with an unwavering look into the space between the eyebrows — [abides in] what is called Siddhāsana, which breaks open the shutters of the door of liberation.

Having placed the left ankle above [the penis] and the other ankle on top — this will be Siddhāsana.

This [pose] some call Siddhāsana, others know [as] Vajrāsana, some say Muktāsana, and others call it Guptāsana.

When the prāṇa breath is carefully stopped in kevala-kumbhaka, the “beyond-mind” state (unmanī) arises of itself, without effort.

In just the same way, when one alone is firmly and constantly held — Siddhāsana — the three locks arise of themselves, without effort.

Literary translation

Siddhāsana, the “adept’s pose”. The heel is pressed to the perineum and the other foot set firmly upon the penis; the body is held straight and motionless as a post, the senses are restrained, and the gaze is fixed between the eyebrows. This pose breaks the bolts of the gates of liberation.

The second description is simpler: the left ankle is laid over the penis and the second ankle above it. Some call this pose Siddhāsana, others Vajrāsana, some say “Muktāsana”, and others “Guptāsana”.

As moderation in food is first among the yamas, and non-violence first among the niyamas, so the adepts hold Siddhāsana to be the chief of all āsanas. Of the eighty-four postures one should always choose Siddhāsana — as of the seventy-two thousand channels [one chooses] Suṣumṇā.

Whoever meditates on the self, eats in moderation and practises Siddhāsana for twelve years without fail, attains niṣpatti, the fullness of perfection. Why [bother with] the many tiring postures when Siddhāsana is there?

Let the prāṇa breath be carefully held in kevala-kumbhaka — and “beyond-mind” (unmanī) will come of itself, without any effort. And in just the same way, let one firmly and constantly sit in Siddhāsana, and the three locks engage by themselves.

There is no posture equal to Siddhāsana, no breath-retention equal to kevala, no mudrā equal to khecarī, and no dissolution of mind like [the contemplation of] nāda.

In the second verse quarter, the adopted reading ekahṛdayo is supported by two manuscripts of the Haṭhapradīpikā (Jodhpur 02232, Jodhpur and one more manuscript whose shelfmark is not given in the edition) and is close to the Nepalese-German Manuscript Preservation Project, microfilm B 62-20, Nepal (microfilm) reading ekahṛdaye. It is also attested by the six-chapter Vivekamārtaṇḍa and the Yogacintāmaṇi, which attributes this verse to an unkown work called the Pavanayogasaṅgraha. In this case, ekahṛdayaḥ appears to describe the yogi as having his mind focused on one thing. There are many variations of this verse quarter in the Haṭhapradīpikā manuscripts, as well as in the manuscripts of the sources and testimonia. Most of the collated witnesses have athaikam eva hṛdaye dhṛtvā, which is close to the adopted reading. Other readings allude here to the practice of the Jālandhara lock, in which the chin is placed on the chest. This is most clearly seen in the Jyotsnā’s version, hṛdaye kṛtvā hanuṃ susthiram (‘having put the jaw firmly on the chest’). The other well-attested reading, athaikaṃ eva niyataṃ, was an attempt to fix the problem of hṛdaye by replacing it with niyatam, which must be read with meḍhre pādam athaikaṃ (‘having fixed one foot on the penis’), but niyatam is redundant here because of vinyaset in the first verse quarter.

Most witnesses have suṣumṇām as the object of the verb abhyaset, which is odd. Others have tried to make sense of this by changing suṣumnām iva nāḍiṣu to nāḍīnāṃ malaśodhanam, which occurs in the Jyotsnā (1.39), Kumbhakapaddhati (120), and Haṭhayoga (quoted in the Yogacintāmaṇi, folio 79, recto). Dating of the Matsyendrasaṃhitā

The manuscript is undated. Sensharma describes his palaeographic estimate as follows (introduction, page 1): “The ms bears no date but on paleographic examination the date for writing the ms appears to be about 1900 Vikram era corresponding to 1858 of the Common Era”

The conversion given in this sentence is inconsistent: 1900 of the Vikram era corresponds approximately to 1843–44 CE, while 1858 CE corresponds approximately to 1914–15 of that era. The sentence does not establish which number the editor intended. On the Vikram calendar.

The date of the manuscript must be distinguished from the date of composition and from the lifetime attributed to Matsyendranātha. On the range of scholarly opinions about the latter, Sensharma writes (introduction, page 9): “The upper limit of his time is fifth century of the Common Era and lowest limit is sometime in the thirteenth century of the Common Era”

On page 26, he offers his own conclusion: “Matsyendranātha therefore probably flourished in 5th or 6th century of the Common Era and lived for 400 years or so in the physical body and taught Yoga to Gorakṣanātha in the 10th century of the Common Era”

This is the editor’s interpretation, not an established historical conclusion. The description of Csaba Kiss’s critical edition dates the composition tentatively to the thirteenth century and refers to five nineteenth-century witnesses: “This volume presents the first critical edition, along with an English translation, of the core chapters of a Sanskrit work of perhaps the thirteenth century surviving in five nineteenth-century witnesses” Kiss’s The Yoga of the Matsyendrasaṃhitā: A Critical Edition and Annotated Translation of Chapters 1–13 and 55 (IFP / École française d’Extrême-Orient, 2021) includes chapter 3. An accessible description of the book characterises the earlier edition as “partial and extremely unreliable”; its readings should therefore be approached with caution.
