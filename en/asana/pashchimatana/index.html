Dating of the text

The "Mānasollāsa" is dated by its author, Sureśvarācārya, and not by internal data of the text. Depending on the date of Śaṅkara accepted (788–820 or c. 700) and on the outcome of the dispute over the identification of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra, the text is assigned either to the early 8th century (c. 700–730) or to the 8th–9th centuries. There is no independent dating of the text on internal grounds in the literature.

Introduction. The Dating of the Mānasollāsa

2.1. The Starting Point: the Text Is Not Dated by Itself

No separate study of the dating of the "Mānasollāsa" exists. All existing datings are derivative: first the author, Sureśvarācārya, is dated, and then the date is transferred to the text. And the dating of Sureśvara depends on two things: which date for Śaṅkara is accepted, and whether the identity of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra is admitted.

This situation was recorded not in the scholarly literature but by the editor of the 19th century himself:

"Notwithstanding the attempts of several oriental scholars to fix the date of Śaṅkarācārya, there has been a certain amount of uncertainty regarding it, the date assigned to him varying from the sixth century before Christ to the eighth century A. D. The date of Sureśvarācārya, closely connected as it is with that of his reputed teacher, has not been more satisfactorily settled."

— Preface to the 1895 edition, p. 3 (keyed by the Ambuda portal)

And on the attribution as such — note this, it is a key fact:

"Nowhere in the hymn or the commentary is the author of either mentioned by name. The commentator refers in his Vārttika to the author of the hymn by mere general terms such as Kavi (author) and Guru (teacher). The style and the subject-matter, however, of the two works incline us to believe in their genuineness."

— ibid.

That is: the author's name is never once stated in the text, and the attribution rests on style and subject-matter alone. This must be borne in mind when reading any dating.

2.3. The Camp of "Śaṅkara 788–820 ⇒ Sureśvara c. 800"

S. N. Dasgupta, A History of Indian Philosophy, vol. 2 (1932), the chapter on the Śaṅkara school. Dates: Sureśvara — 800 CE, Sarvajñātman — 900 CE. Arguments:

"Hence there seems to be no reason why the traditionally accepted view that Śaṅkara was born in Saṃvat 844, or A.D. 788, or Kali age 3889, should be given up."

"Taking the approximate date of Śaṅkara's death to be about A.D. 820 and taking into consideration that Sureśvara, the teacher of Sarvajñātman, occupied his high pontifical position for a long time, the supposition that Sarvajñātman lived in A.D. 900 may not be very far wrong."

"…Vācaspati, who probably wrote his earlier work the Nyāya-sūcī-nibandha in A.D. 842, also wrote his commentary on Maṇḍana's Brahma-siddhi when Sureśvara was occupying the pontifical position."

And his own caveat:

"The exact date of neither Sureśvara nor Sarvajñātma can be definitely determined."

(All four from Dasgupta, A History of Indian Philosophy, vol. 2; from the text at wisdomlib, "A History of Indian Philosophy", vol. 2.)

The same date is repeated by:

  • Swami Harshananda — "Sureśvara (A. D. 800)" (Hindupedia, article "Mānasollāsa");
  • Hindu Blog, article "Mānasollāsa of Sureśvara" (2024) — "written by Sureśvara around 800 CE";
  • P. S. Roodurmum, Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa Schools of Advaita Vedānta (2002), pp. 29–30 — "flourished c. 800–900 CE" (as reported in Wikipedia, article "Sureśvara").

2.5. The Dispute on Which Everything Depends: Sureśvara = Maṇḍana?

If they are one person, the text falls in the early 8th century. If they are different, it falls in the 8th–9th centuries.

Against the identification:

  • M. Hiriyanna, "Sureśvara and Maṇḍana-Miśra", JRAS 55/2 (April 1923), pp. 259–263; continuation ibid., 56/1 (1924), pp. 96–97. Arguments: doctrinal divergences, and the Śṛṅgeri tradition, in which Maṇḍana and Sureśvara are different persons.
  • S. Kuppuswami Sastri — introduction to the critical edition of the "Brahmasiddhi" (1937; reprinted 1984).
  • S. N. Dasgupta — A History of Indian Philosophy, vol. 2 (1932).
  • S. S. Sharma, Advaita Tradition in Indian Philosophy (1997), pp. 290–291 — sets out the arguments of Hiriyanna and Kuppuswami Sastri.

For the identification:

  • R. Balasubramanian: "there is no convincing evidence to prove that Maṇḍana … are different persons" (as reported in Wikipedia, article "Sureśvara").
  • The Śṛṅgeri Maṭha — officially, in the documents of the maṭha and in hagiographies.

What the divergences consist in (after Sharma and Wikipedia):

  • where avidyā (ignorance) is located: in Maṇḍana it is in the jīva (the individual being), in Sureśvara in Brahman. This reflects the two sub-schools of Advaita — Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa;
  • the role of meditation: for Maṇḍana, knowledge from the mahāvākya ("great sayings" of the Upaniṣads) alone is not enough, meditation is required; for Sureśvara knowledge liberates directly, and meditation is an aid;
  • the attitude to sphoṭa (the doctrine that the meaning of a word manifests in the sound as such): Maṇḍana accepts śabdādvaita, Sureśvara ignores it.

2.6. What the Text Itself Says

In the ninth ullāsa, in the commentary on verse 23½, the author refers to his own commentary on Maṇḍana's "Brahmasiddhi". Reading from the scan of the 2008 edition, p. 119 (sources/scans/mss/2008-str119-celekom.png):

इति मण्डनसिद्धान्त एव सुरेश्वरस्यापि सम्मतः।तदनुषङ्गतः शाब्दापरोक्षवादोऽपि।विशदं चैतत् ब्रह्मसिद्धौ, तद्भूमिकायाञ्चास्मदीयायाम्॥२३१/२॥

"Thus, the doctrine of Maṇḍana is accepted by Sureśvara too; and, following from it, the doctrine of śabda-aparokṣa (immediate knowledge arising from the word). This is set out in detail in the 'Brahmasiddhi' and in our own bhūmikā to it."

Two circumstances must be kept in mind:

  1. The word अस्मदीया ("our") is the author's direct reference to his own commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi". This is internal evidence in favour of the identification, and it is usually quoted precisely in this dispute.
  2. It does not settle the question: the commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi" could also have been written by a pupil.

2.7. The Position of the Editors of 2008

The Hindi preface to the 2008 edition is a rare case of an editor setting out both the argument and the counter-argument.

«कुछ आधुनिकों ने इस स्तोत्र को अभिनवगुप्त के प्रत्यभिज्ञादर्शन का ग्रन्थ माना है।»

— "Some modern [scholars] have regarded this stotra as a text of Abhinavagupta's Pratyabhijñā-darśana" (p. i). It was precisely to answer this that the commentary "Mānasollāsa-vardhinī" was commissioned.

«एतिहासिक दृष्टि से मण्डन आचार्य के पूर्ववर्ती थे।»

— "From the historical point of view Maṇḍana was a predecessor of the Ācārya [Śaṅkara]" (p. ii).

The counter-argument against the identification, given in the same place: in the "Bṛhadāraṇyaka-vārttika" Maṇḍana is spoken of in the third person with the pejorative पण्डितम्मन्य ("one who fancies himself learned"), which no one writes of himself.

2.8. The Result on Dating

A formulation that can be defended:

The "Mānasollāsa" is dated by its author, Sureśvarācārya, and not by internal data of the text. Depending on the date of Śaṅkara accepted (788–820 or c. 700) and on the outcome of the dispute over the identification of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra, the text is assigned either to the early 8th century (c. 700–730) or to the 8th–9th centuries. There is no independent dating of the text on internal grounds in the literature.

A supporting boundary that does not depend on the dispute: Vācaspati Miśra, writing according to Dasgupta in 842, quotes Sureśvara — which gives an upper bound ("terminus ante quem") in the 9th century.

Literary translation

24-26. Svastika, Gomukha, Padma, Haṃsa — these are the Brahmic postures (Āsanas).

Nṛsiṃha, Garuḍa, Kūrma, Nāga — these are the Vaiṣṇava postures.

Vīra, Mayūra, Vajra, Siddha — these are the Raudra postures.

Yoni is the Śākta posture.

Paścima-tanaka is the Śaiva posture.

The posture for the Nirālambana-Yoga is nirālambana (lit. propless, involving no specific position of hands, feet or other members of the body); meditation should be on the Nirālambana, and the Nirālambana is Sadāśiva, the Unconditioned, the Paramātman.

— English translation, 1978 edition, p. 170

1.2. The Chronological Problem: Scholarly Arguments and Dating Debate

The historical dating of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī has been examined by leading Sanskritists and historians of yoga:

1. Arguments of Dr. M. L. Gharote and Dr. M. Venkata Reddy (Critical Edition, 2007):

  • Terminus post quem (Earliest possible date): Sundaradeva directly cites late seventeenth-century works:
  • «Haṭharatnāvalī» (Haṭharatnāvalī) by Śrīnivāsa (Śrīnivāsa), composed circa 1670–1695 CE. Sundaradeva adopts its specialized terminology, āsana lists, and quotes its verses;
  • «Kumbhakapaddhati» (Kumbhakapaddhati) by Raghuvīra (Raghuvīra), datable to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century;
  • «Yogacandrikā» (Yogacandrikā) and «Yogacintāmaṇi» (Yogacintāmaṇi) by Śivānanda Sarasvatī.
  • Terminus ante quem (Latest possible date): The oldest and only complete dated manuscript witness (MS. No. 4235, Scindia Oriental Institute, Ujjain) preserves a scribal colophon with the exact date of completion: Saṃvat 1846 (Vikrama Saṃvat), month of Vaiśākha, dark fortnight, 10th tithi, Tuesday, corresponding to April–May 1789 CE.
  • Gharote's Conclusion: The treatise was composed between 1700 and 1750 CE (first half of the eighteenth century).

2. Textological Arguments of Dr. Jason Birch (Oxford University / Haṭha Yoga Project):

  • Dr. Jason Birch points out that the Haṭhatattvakaumudī epitomizes the late medieval "encyclopedic turn" in yoga. This genre of comprehensive compendia, synthesizing hundreds of earlier sources (from Upaniṣads and Tantras to late medieval paddhatis), flourished among the scholarly Brahmin milieus of Vārāṇasī precisely in the first half of the eighteenth century.
  • Birch underscores that Sundaradeva's Sanskrit exhibits late classical syntax infused with the scientific medical lexicon of early modern India.

3. Iconographic and Structural Analysis of Prof. Gudrun Bühnemann:

  • Prof. Bühnemann examined the evolution of āsana classification schemes in Indian traditions. In Chapter 7 of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī, Sundaradeva retains the canonical core of 15 postures (inherited from Chapter 1 of Svātmārāma's Haṭhapradīpikā), yet reinterprets them within a therapeutic methodology and introduces distinctive technical nuances (e.g., yonyāsana as an advanced modification of siddhāsana, specialized breath management during padmāsana). This marks a systematic transition before the proliferation of vast catalogs of 84 or more āsanas in late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century manuals.

4. Sociological Synthesis by Dr. Mark Singleton:

  • Mark Singleton emphasizes that Sundaradeva represents a notable synthesis of two social roles: the practicing yoga adept and the high-caste, university-educated vaidya-paṇḍita. Such integration was characteristic of cosmopolitan Vārāṇasī in the eighteenth century, where Haṭha yoga techniques were absorbed into the orthodox curriculum of health, longevity, and therapeutics.

Literal Translation (Verses 41–43)

Having extended the two legs on the ground straight like a staff, having grasped the two toes with both hands, with head resting upon the knees, balanced, let him remain in that posture possessing the splendor of ten million suns. Thus Paścimatāna is the posture causing the entrance of the movement of the prāṇa breath [into the central channel], kindling the gastric fire, when the head is leveled with the knees upon the ground. Thus Paścimatāna is supreme among postures, kindling the dimmed digestive fire and destroying vāta.

Literary Translation (Verses 41–43)

Stretching both legs out upon the earth, straight and unbending like a wooden staff, the yogin grasps his toes with both hands and rests his forehead against his knees. Resting balanced in this posture, he radiates with the blazing solar brilliance of ten million suns! This is Paścimatāna («the Posterior Stretch Pose»); it forces the current of the vital breath straight into the central suṣumnā channel, dramatically intensifies the digestive fire of the belly, and levels the spine into perfect harmony. Paścimatāna is celebrated as the foremost among all physical postures: it reignites the dying embers of the gastric fire and thoroughly eradicates every affliction born of aggravated vāta.

Textological Commentary and Critical Apparatus

  • Footnote 1 to Verse 41: In «a», the grand solar metaphor varteta yasyāṃ ravikoṭitejaḥ ("radiating with the luster of ten million suns"); «b» replaces this with an empty phrase: varteta hyeṣā bhavatīti śaṃsaḥ.
  • Footnotes to Verse 42: In «b», the line is harmonized with Haṭhapradīpikā 1.29; «a» preserves Sundaradeva's genuine original phrasing.
  • Footnote 4 to Verse 43: In «a», dara-mandasya ("slightly sluggish / dimmed fire"); «b» misreads this as jara-mandasya ("sluggish from old age") due to scribal confusion between Devanāgarī da and ja.

Dates of the manuscripts: what is known

Manuscripts with a firm date. One manuscript is explicitly dated in the edition, Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 30109, Vārāṇasī: in the comments on verses 1.24 and 1.56 the editors call it the “oldest dated manuscript”. The year is not printed in the reader; the project’s public materials give it as 1496 CE — only about a century from the supposed time of Svātmārāma’s authorship (the early 15th century). This manuscript (Sampūrṇānanda Library, no. 30109) is known in the scholarly literature as well — it is referred to, for instance, in an article on the Amaraughaprabodha (Journal of Indian Philosophy, 2019).

In the other manuscripts the dates survive in the colophons — they are transcribed in the editors’ draft files. A systematic check of the edition’s sources (the archive of the project’s LaTeX files of 20 May 2026) yielded five more dated copies. The dates stand in scribal notes at the end of the text and are written in the figures of the “saṃvat” era (i.e. the Vikrama era; subtracting 57 gives the year CE):

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash (MSPP), no. 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, ms. no. 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

All five notes are the editors’ remarks in the comments to the draft files of the edition (they are not in the published digital reader); the era is not stated in the colophons themselves, and the final dates, checked against the manuscripts, will appear in the introduction to the printed book. But the picture is already clear: the earliest dated copies are 1496 (30109), 1626/27 (MSPP 2230), 1636/37 (29899), 1650/51 (30069), 1667/68 (Jodhpur 23709), 1745/46 (Jodhpur 02237).

Several witnesses have colophons that survive without a date. GOML R17256/SR8080, Chennai (in the apparatus — “epsilon-1”): “śrī śivāya namaḥ … gaṇapati svahasta likhitaṃ” — “written by the hand of Gaṇapati himself”, no date. Lalchand M-2089, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “pi-2”): only an ownership note, “pustakam idaṃ jājīrāmavyāsasya”. Lalchand M-6494, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “delta-3”): “…iti śrīmadātmārāmaviracitāyāṃ … upadeśaḥ … śubham astu sarvajagatām”. NGMPP B 62-20, Nepal (in the apparatus — “alpha-1”): the brief “śrīsahajasadguruātmārāmārpaṇa saṃpūrṇam”. NGMPP E-1528-1 / E-1527-7(4) (in the apparatus — “zeta-2”) has no colophon at all: the text ends exactly on leaf 346, and another work begins on the next leaf.

The Berlin manuscripts are described in detail in the printed VOHD catalogue. Our two Berlin manuscripts received no dating in the catalogue “Indian Manuscripts” (Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland). Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, or. fol. 2166 (= “eta-omega”): VOHD II, 8 (no. 3417) — a paper manuscript, 53 leaves, Devanagari script, six lines to a page; explicitly marked “Undatiert” (“undated”), and the title stands in the colophon in the form “Hathapradipikā”. Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, or.

11056 (= “delta-2”): VOHD II, 16 (no. 5442) — a paper manuscript in Śāradā script, leaves 1–48 and 1–[22], ten lines; it contains an incomplete “Hathayogapradīpikā” (whose first verse is “śrīādināthāya namo ’stu…”) and the “Rudrayāmala-Vijñānabhairava”; no date is given in the description.

The same catalogue describes two more Berlin “Hathayogapradīpikās” — neither was collated for the edition, but it is curious that they too are undated: Ms.or.oct. 659 (VOHD II, 8, no. 3416) — a paper manuscript, 43 leaves, Devanagari, “Undatiert”; and Hs or. 13133 (VOHD II, 15, no. 5136.2) — palm leaves, Grantha script, badly damaged, in a collection together with the “Haṭharatnāvalī”; here the “Hathayogapradīpikā” is incomplete (the colophon covers only the first section), and again there is no date. In all, four copies of the “Hathayogapradīpikā” are recorded in Berlin, and not one of them is dated.

The Lalchand Research Library (DAV College, Chandigarh) — the digital archive dav.splrarebooks.com. Our two manuscripts from it — M-2089 (= “pi-2”: 37 pages, Devanagari, from Bahawalpur) and M-6494 (= “delta-3”: 727 pages, Śāradā script, a collection called “Hathayogapradīpikādi-granthasaṅgraha”) — have no dates in their digital records. Other copies in the same library, however, do: M-1293 — 1911 of the “saṃvat” era (≈1854 CE), M-2082 — 1775 (≈1718), M-2091 — 1923 (≈1866), M-4530 — 1888 (≈1831).

The remaining witnesses (sixteen of the twenty-one manuscripts) have no dates. Undated are the Nepalese microfilms (NGMPP B 62-20, G 25-2, E-1528-1 / E-1527-7(4), A62-33), Sarasvatī Bhavan 30051, GOML 18885 / R17256/SR8080 / R3841/SR2190, BISM 64/919, “pi-1” (P11 in the project’s working list), Jodhpur 02235 and 02241.

For comparison: the Kaivalyadhāma edition of 1970 relied on only 9 manuscripts, and its oldest dated manuscript is from 1629 (Calcutta No. TH 321); the date assigned to the text itself is broad: “1350–1550”. A manuscript of 1496 in the new corpus is a qualitative step forward.

Where else to look for dates: the descriptive catalogues of the NGMCP (Hamburg) — for the Nepalese films (in 2026 its online search is unavailable, and the archived wiki copy preserved only one page); the descriptive catalogues of the Sarasvatī Bhavan manuscripts (Sampūrṇānanda, Vārāṇasī); the catalogues of RORI and MSPP (Jodhpur; OCR of their scans barely recognises Devanagari); the printed introduction to the edition (EFEO, Pondicherry, late 2026) — it will contain full descriptions of all the manuscripts.

Literal translation

Having stretched out both legs on the ground [straight] as a staff, having grasped the tips of both feet with the hands and placed the forehead on the knees, one should sit: this they call paścimatāṇa. Thus paścimatāṇa, the foremost of āsanas, makes the breath flow backwards, causes the fire of the belly to rise [and] gives people a slim belly and health.

Literary translation

Paścimatāṇa, the “back-stretch”. Seated on the ground, one stretches the legs out straight as a stick, takes hold of the toes and lays the forehead on the knees. This foremost of āsanas turns the breath towards the back — into the central channel — fans the digestive fire, makes the belly flat and keeps a person free of disease.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.28 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The reading dorbhyāṃ padāgradvitayaṃ is well attested but is somewhat strange because dos usually means ‘the arm’ rather than the hands. The variant dvābhyāṃ karābhyāṃ dvitayaṃ, “with both hands”, appears to be an attempt to remove dorbhyāṃ, but it introduces the problem of the toes not being mentioned.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.29 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The use of the word paścima to mean the central channel is found at Yogabīja 95 (paścimamārgataḥ), 108 (paścime pathi), 117 (paścimadvāramārgeṇa) and 121 (paścimaṃ). Cf. the usages of paścimamārga in Dattātreyayogaśāstra 140 and Śivasaṃhitā 3.110 (from which this verse is likely to be derived). Brahmānanda understands paścima as referring to the Suṣumṇā (Jyotsnā 1.29): paścimavāhinaṃ paścimena paścimamārgeṇa suṣumṇāmārgeṇa vahatīti paścimavāhī.
