Dating and Probable Place of Composition

The date of the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā is disputed; the dates of the manuscripts used for the printed edition do not in themselves establish the date of composition.

In the section “Provenience and Age” of his introduction, F. Otto Schrader associated the text with Kashmir. Among the arguments he adduces are the mention of yantra diagrams on birch bark in chapters 26 and 45; the image in chapter 39 of the sun emerging from an obstacle of snow; and the account of Muktāpīḍa in chapter 48. Schrader stressed that little can be said with certainty about the text’s age: he placed it in a group of Saṃhitās for which he proposed the eighth century CE as an upper limit. As a possible lower limit, he considered the text’s presentation of Buddhism as divided into three major schools; in Schrader’s view, this implies a date after the establishment of Mahāyāna, approximately after 300 CE. These are Schrader’s dating arguments, not an established date for the text. See the sections of his introduction on the manuscripts, provenance, and age, pages 94–99 6.

In a 2017 article, D. Wujastyk proposes a date after 1000 CE, and possibly South India, for the Ahirbudhnya-saṃhitā 5. Marion Rastelli’s 2018 article abstract likewise associates the text’s composition with South India, narrowing the hypothesis to the twelfth–thirteenth centuries 3. These later proposals differ substantially from Schrader’s earlier dating framework; without additional evidence, they cannot be collapsed into one precise date or presented as an agreed consensus. Accordingly, this article does not assign a date to the āsana section itself.

2.1. Lower limit: early authors do not quote the text (argument from silence)

“Chronology

The «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» can in no way be considered a very ancient work as ancient authors do not make any reference to the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā». For instance, Halāyudha (1050 of the Common Era) in his Brāhmaṇasarvasva and Lakṣmīdhara (1150 of the Common Era) in the Kalpataru do not mention «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» anywhere; nor do they quote any verse from the same whereas both the authors quote liberally from other sources. Were the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» prior to these authors, they would have certainly quoted from «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»; more so because (like the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā») both the authors advocate the doctrine of Jñānakarmasamuccaya (synthesis of Knowledge and Action) for the sake of salvation in support of which they quote from different works like the Yogayājñavalkya, Agnipurāṇa and so on The absence of such quotation of «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» in their works is an indication that «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is the later origin, that is after the 12th century of the Common Era”

Compiler's note: testing the argument.

  • Lakṣmīdhara. I checked the Mokṣakāṇḍa of the Kṛtyakalpataru in the electronic text by P. Olivelle (UT Austin, 2022; based on the edition by K. V. Rangaswami Aiyangar, Gaekwad’s Oriental Series CII, Baroda 1945; https://sites.utexas.edu/sanskrit/resources/dharmasastra/krtyakalpataru/). Results:
  • There are indeed no verses of the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» in it. The quotations marked “vasiṣṭhaḥ” are prose rules for hermits and ascetics, not verses of the yogic «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».
  • The Yogayājñavalkya is quoted precisely in defence of the combination of knowledge and action. Chapter 15 “atha jñānakarmopayogaḥ”, page 146 by the page markers of the e-text: “yogiyājñavalkyaḥ | … jñānakarmasamāyogāt param āpnoti pūruṣam | pṛthagbhāve na sidhyeta ubhe tasmāt samāśrayet ||” (“Yogiyājñavalkya: … From the union of knowledge and action one reaches the supreme Person; it is not achieved by either separately, therefore one should resort to both”). I have confirmed this part of the editors' claim.
  • A search of the Mokṣakāṇḍa found no quotations marked as from the Agnipurāṇa. The editors may have meant other parts of the Kalpataru, or Halāyudha; this has not been checked.
  • I did not examine the other volumes of the Kalpataru.
  • Halāyudha. I have not seen the text of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva (edited by D. Bhattacharyya, Sanskrit Sahitya Parishad Series 29, Calcutta 1960).
  • The date “1050 of the Common Era” conflicts with other sources. According to them, the author of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva was a minister of King Lakṣmaṇasena of Bengal, 12th century: the description of the 1960 edition on exoticindiaart.com (“the court of king Lakṣmaṇasena of Bengal in the 12th century”); Swami Harshananda on hindupedia.com (“CE 1160-1200”). The same source distinguishes the lexicographer Halāyudha (10th century) from the author of the Brāhmaṇasarvasva.
  • On these data Halāyudha lived after Lakṣmīdhara, not before him.
  • The argument itself is an argument from silence. According to the editors, both authors quote other works in support of jñānakarmasamuccaya; had the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» existed, they would have quoted it too. Birch 2018 (note 92) explicitly judges this argument to be inconclusive (see §4).

2.2. Upper limit: later authors quote the text

“That the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is not very recent becomes evident from the fact that authors like Nāgojībhaṭṭa (end of 17th century), Bhāvāgaṇeśa (1550-1600 of the Common Era) Vijñānabhikṣu (1550 of the Common Era) and so on, quote the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā», Mallīnātha (1450 of the Common Era) also, who preceded Vijñānabhikṣu quotes «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» in his commentaries on Raghuvaṃśa and Kumārasambhava.”

Wujastyk gives independent confirmation regarding Mallinātha (see §5.5). There Mallinātha is dated “flourished 1400” rather than 1450, and the passage is given as Raghuvaṃśa 13.52.

2.3. The calculation “about 1250” from the Haṭhapradīpikā

“The «Haṭhapradīpikā» also while describing the Āsanas qualifies them as recommended by sages like Vasiṣṭha and so on compare

"Vasiṣṭhādyaiśca munibhirmatsyendrādyaiśca yogibhiḥ

aṅgīkṛtānyāsanāni kathyante kānicinmayā" «Haṭhapradīpikā». I. 18

Thus, the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is a work prior to the «Haṭhapradīpikā» and if the «Haṭhapradīpikā» is assigned to about 1350 of the Common Era, the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» must be taken to have been ready at least before a hundred years. Thus, we can assign the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» to about 1250 of the Common Era”

  • «Haṭhapradīpikā» 1.18 names Vasiṣṭha among the sages who adopted āsanas. It does not refer explicitly to the work called the Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā; the conclusion “the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is older than the «Haṭhapradīpikā»” was drawn by the editors.

2.4. The editors' caveat: the text is multi-layered

“Yet we cannot definitely state that all the material that has come down to us in the extant «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» belonged to that period. Some of the material must belong to an earlier period and the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» must have attained the present form passing through various stages like the epic Jaya which attained the form of the Mahābhārata.”

Immediately after this the book has a paragraph on the lineages of teachers:

“The various traditions regarding the lineage of the teachers who transmitted that lore differ to a large extent. But all these (except the Mahābhārata, Śāntiparva) accounts have Vasiṣṭha and Śakti in common. The various accounts are given below.”

The lineage charts follow. In the online copy their words (Brahma, Vasistha, Sakti, Parasara, Vyasa, Suka, Gaudapada, Govinda, Sankara) were mixed into the text of the section. The printed text confirmed that the word “Sakti” is not part of the quotation about «Haṭhapradīpikā» 1.18: OCR had inserted it from the chart.

2.5. Manuscripts and early citations (section “Extent of the Text”)

“All the six manuscripts except one have eight chapters. The manuscript dated Samvat 1463 (that is 1406 of the Common Era), which is most authentic in this respect has eight chapters. Nrsimha Vajpeyi (1450 of the Common Era) quotes in his Nityacaradipa the following verse of Vasistha Yoga: […]”

(The verse itself is illegible in the OCR.)

“This verse happens to be the nineteenth verse of the eighth chapter of the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā». It can be inferred that upto about 1450 of the Common Era the work was recognised to comprise of at least eight chapters.”

Compiler's note. The manuscript dated 1406 by itself gives only a latest date for the existence of the text (not later than 1406).

2.6. Relation to the Yogayājñavalkya and the Haṭhapradīpikā

“None of the scholars, quoting the stanzas found to be common to both the works, ascribe them to Yogayajnavalkya but to Vasistha. Similarly Bhavaganesa and Nagojibhatta (on Yogasutra I. 33) while quoting on the process of the purification of Nadis without Kumbhaka, ascribe it clearly to «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» and not to «Yogayājñavalkya». The three types of Pranayama, namely Adhama, Madhyama and Uttama are also traced to Vasistha and not to «Yogayājñavalkya». From these it becomes clear that «Yogayājñavalkya» is a later work and that it seems to have borrowed from «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».”

“The verses 11-22, of chapter 5 of the «Yogayājñavalkya» which describe the purification of Nadis, occur almost verbatim in the second chapter of the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» (Vikrama era 58-70). Also the words (kecit vadanti) meaning 'some say' occurring prior to verse 10 of the Vth chapter of «Yogayājñavalkya» seem to indicate Vasistha. From this it can be inferred that «Yogayājñavalkya» has borrowed from «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».”

“But the «Haṭhapradīpikā» itself is a work later than the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» (the facts that lead us to this conclusion are given below). Hence, it can definitely be said that the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» is not a borrower.”

2.7. A discrepancy within the edition: “prior to 1150”

Checked against the printed edition. Below is the text transcribed from the printed book (section “Sūta Saṃhitā – Jñānayogakāṇḍa”). It confirms the reading “prior to 1150 of the Common Era”, that is this is not an OCR error. The beginning and end of the paragraph were missing in the online copy and are restored here.

“Sūta Saṃhitā- Jñānayogakāṇḍa

The Sūta Saṃhitā has been published by the Ānandāśrama, Pune. Whilst tracing the pedigree of teachers through whom the work is handed down, the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» states that Śakti derived the lore from Vasiṣṭha. From this it can be inferred that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» borrowed from the dialogue between Śakti and Vasiṣṭha. The Sūta Saṃhitā (Muktikhaṇḍa, VII. 50-55) also refers to Cakradhara Mahānubhāva and to his disciple Subhaga. Cakradhara is assigned to 1250 of the Common Era. But as the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» (as shown below) is prior to 1150 of the Common Era and as there is a period of hundred years intervening between the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» and Cakradhara, the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» is certainly the borrower from the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā». We can also point out to influence of Vedānta upon the Sūta Saṃhitā. This also proves that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» is later than the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā».”

This paragraph gives three arguments that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» is later than the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»:

3. In the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» lineage of teachers Śakti receives the teaching from Vasiṣṭha. From this the editors infer that the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» borrows from the dialogue of Vasiṣṭha and Śakti.

4. The Sūta Saṃhitā mentions Cakradhara Mahānubhāva, whom the editors assign to 1250. The «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā», according to them, is older than 1150.

5. The influence of Vedānta is noticeable in the Sūta Saṃhitā. No concrete examples are given in this paragraph.

Compiler's note.

  • Within the sentence the arithmetic is consistent: 1250 − 100 = 1150. So “1150” is not a misprint but an intended figure.
  • The reference “as shown below” points to the section “Chronology”. But there a different conclusion is reached: “after the 12th century of the Common Era” and “we can assign the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» to about 1250 of the Common Era” (§§2.1, 2.3). The same book thus gives two incompatible dates for the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā»: “prior to 1150” and “about 1250”. The book does not explain this discrepancy.
  • Why this happened cannot be established from the printed text of 2005. One could check whether these passages agree in the 1984 edition.
  • Argument (2) depends on the date. If the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» belongs to “about 1250”, it is contemporary with Cakradhara rather than a hundred years older. Then this argument for the «Jñānayogakāṇḍa of the Sūta Saṃhitā» borrowing from the «Vasiṣṭha Saṃhitā» loses its force. This is my observation, not the editors'.

Introduction. The Dating of the Mānasollāsa

2.1. The Starting Point: the Text Is Not Dated by Itself

No separate study of the dating of the "Mānasollāsa" exists. All existing datings are derivative: first the author, Sureśvarācārya, is dated, and then the date is transferred to the text. And the dating of Sureśvara depends on two things: which date for Śaṅkara is accepted, and whether the identity of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra is admitted.

This situation was recorded not in the scholarly literature but by the editor of the 19th century himself:

"Notwithstanding the attempts of several oriental scholars to fix the date of Śaṅkarācārya, there has been a certain amount of uncertainty regarding it, the date assigned to him varying from the sixth century before Christ to the eighth century of the Common Era. The date of Sureśvarācārya, closely connected as it is with that of his reputed teacher, has not been more satisfactorily settled."

— Preface to the 1895 edition, page 3 (keyed by the Ambuda portal)

And on the attribution as such — note this, it is a key fact:

"Nowhere in the hymn or the commentary is the author of either mentioned by name. The commentator refers in his Vārttika to the author of the hymn by mere general terms such as Kavi (author) and Guru (teacher). The style and the subject-matter, however, of the two works incline us to believe in their genuineness."

— there

That is: the author's name is never once stated in the text, and the attribution rests on style and subject-matter alone. This must be borne in mind when reading any dating.

2.5. The Dispute on Which Everything Depends: Sureśvara = Maṇḍana?

If they are one person, the text falls in the early 8th century. If they are different, it falls in the 8th–9th centuries.

Against the identification:

  • M. Hiriyanna, "Sureśvara and Maṇḍana-Miśra", JRAS 55/2 (April 1923), pages 259–263; continuation there, 56/1 (1924), pages 96–97. Arguments: doctrinal divergences, and the Śṛṅgeri tradition, in which Maṇḍana and Sureśvara are different persons.
  • S. Kuppuswami Sastri — introduction to the critical edition of the "Brahmasiddhi" (1937; reprinted 1984).
  • S. N. Dasgupta — A History of Indian Philosophy, volume 2 (1932).
  • S. S. Sharma, Advaita Tradition in Indian Philosophy (1997), pages 290–291 — sets out the arguments of Hiriyanna and Kuppuswami Sastri.

For the identification:

  • R. Balasubramanian: "there is no convincing evidence to prove that Maṇḍana … are different persons" (as reported in Wikipedia, article "Sureśvara").
  • The Śṛṅgeri Maṭha — officially, in the documents of the maṭha and in hagiographies.

What the divergences consist in (after Sharma and Wikipedia):

  • where avidyā (ignorance) is located: in Maṇḍana it is in the jīva (the individual being), in Sureśvara in Brahman. This reflects the two sub-schools of Advaita — Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa;
  • the role of meditation: for Maṇḍana, knowledge from the mahāvākya ("great sayings" of the Upaniṣads) alone is not enough, meditation is required; for Sureśvara knowledge liberates directly, and meditation is an aid;
  • the attitude to sphoṭa (the doctrine that the meaning of a word manifests in the sound as such): Maṇḍana accepts śabdādvaita, Sureśvara ignores it.

2.6. What the Text Itself Says

In the ninth ullāsa, in the commentary on verse 23½, the author refers to his own commentary on Maṇḍana's "Brahmasiddhi". Reading from the scan of the 2008 edition, page 119 (sources/scans/mss/2008-str119-celekom.png):

इति मण्डनसिद्धान्त एव सुरेश्वरस्यापि सम्मतः।तदनुषङ्गतः शाब्दापरोक्षवादोऽपि।विशदं चैतत् ब्रह्मसिद्धौ, तद्भूमिकायाञ्चास्मदीयायाम्॥२३१/२॥

"Thus, the doctrine of Maṇḍana is accepted by Sureśvara too; and, following from it, the doctrine of śabda-aparokṣa (immediate knowledge arising from the word). This is set out in detail in the 'Brahmasiddhi' and in our own bhūmikā to it."

Two circumstances must be kept in mind:

  1. The word अस्मदीया ("our") is the author's direct reference to his own commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi". This is internal evidence in favour of the identification, and it is usually quoted precisely in this dispute.
  2. It does not settle the question: the commentary on the "Brahmasiddhi" could also have been written by a pupil.

2.7. The Position of the Editors of 2008

The Hindi preface to the 2008 edition is a rare case of an editor setting out both the argument and the counter-argument.

«कुछ आधुनिकों ने इस स्तोत्र को अभिनवगुप्त के प्रत्यभिज्ञादर्शन का ग्रन्थ माना है।»

— "Some modern [scholars] have regarded this stotra as a text of Abhinavagupta's Pratyabhijñā-darśana" (page i). It was precisely to answer this that the commentary "Mānasollāsa-vardhinī" was commissioned.

«एतिहासिक दृष्टि से मण्डन आचार्य के पूर्ववर्ती थे।»

— "From the historical point of view Maṇḍana was a predecessor of the Ācārya [Śaṅkara]" (page ii).

The counter-argument against the identification, given in the same place: in the "Bṛhadāraṇyaka-vārttika" Maṇḍana is spoken of in the third person with the pejorative पण्डितम्मन्य ("one who fancies himself learned"), which no one writes of himself.

2.8. The Result on Dating

A formulation that can be defended:

The "Mānasollāsa" is dated by its author, Sureśvarācārya, and not by internal data of the text. Depending on the date of Śaṅkara accepted (788–820 or around 700) and on the outcome of the dispute over the identification of Sureśvara with Maṇḍana Miśra, the text is assigned either to the early 8th century (around 700–730) or to the 8th–9th centuries. There is no independent dating of the text on internal grounds in the literature.

A supporting boundary that does not depend on the dispute: Vācaspati Miśra, writing according to Dasgupta in 842, quotes Sureśvara — which gives an upper bound ("terminus ante quem") in the 9th century.

1.2. The Chronological Problem: Scholarly Arguments and Dating Debate

The historical dating of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī has been examined by leading Sanskritists and historians of yoga:

1. Arguments of Doctor M. L. Gharote and Doctor M. Venkata Reddy (Critical Edition, 2007):

  • Terminus post quem (Earliest possible date): Sundaradeva directly cites late seventeenth-century works:
  • «Haṭharatnāvalī» (Haṭharatnāvalī) by Śrīnivāsa (Śrīnivāsa), composed circa 1670–1695 CE. Sundaradeva adopts its specialized terminology, āsana lists, and quotes its verses;
  • «Kumbhakapaddhati» (Kumbhakapaddhati) by Raghuvīra (Raghuvīra), datable to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century;
  • «Yogacandrikā» (Yogacandrikā) and «Yogacintāmaṇi» (Yogacintāmaṇi) by Śivānanda Sarasvatī.
  • Terminus ante quem (Latest possible date): The oldest and only complete dated manuscript witness (manuscript number 4235, Scindia Oriental Institute, Ujjain) preserves a scribal colophon with the exact date of completion: Saṃvat 1846 (Vikrama Saṃvat), month of Vaiśākha, dark fortnight, 10th tithi, Tuesday, corresponding to April–May 1789 CE.
  • Gharote's Conclusion: The treatise was composed between 1700 and 1750 CE (first half of the eighteenth century).

2. Textological Arguments of Doctor Jason Birch (Oxford University / Haṭha Yoga Project):

  • Doctor Jason Birch points out that the Haṭhatattvakaumudī epitomizes the late medieval "encyclopedic turn" in yoga. This genre of comprehensive compendia, synthesizing hundreds of earlier sources (from Upaniṣads and Tantras to late medieval paddhatis), flourished among the scholarly Brahmin milieus of Vārāṇasī precisely in the first half of the eighteenth century.
  • Birch underscores that Sundaradeva's Sanskrit exhibits late classical syntax infused with the scientific medical lexicon of early modern India.

3. Iconographic and Structural Analysis of Professor Gudrun Bühnemann:

  • Professor Bühnemann examined the evolution of āsana classification schemes in Indian traditions. In Chapter 7 of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī, Sundaradeva retains the canonical core of 15 postures (inherited from Chapter 1 of Svātmārāma's Haṭhapradīpikā), yet reinterprets them within a therapeutic methodology and introduces distinctive technical nuances (for example, yonyāsana as an advanced modification of siddhāsana, specialized breath management during padmāsana). This marks a systematic transition before the proliferation of vast catalogs of 84 or more āsanas in late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century manuals.

4. Sociological Synthesis by Doctor Mark Singleton:

  • Mark Singleton emphasizes that Sundaradeva represents a notable synthesis of two social roles: the practicing yoga adept and the high-caste, university-educated vaidya-paṇḍita. Such integration was characteristic of cosmopolitan Vārāṇasī in the eighteenth century, where Haṭha yoga techniques were absorbed into the orthodox curriculum of health, longevity, and therapeutics.

Literal Translation (Verses 44–45)

Having placed properly both soles of the feet between the knees and thighs, seated with the body erect, that they call Svastika. This posture Svastika is declared by the lords of yogins as bestowing integration of the prāṇa, destroying all afflictions, always producing bliss, and praised by the masters. From its practice hosts of diseases go to destruction in an instant, a pure light blazes in the hearth of the belly, and supreme peace eternally abides for embodied beings.

Literary Translation (Verses 44–45)

Tucking the soles of both feet neatly into the creases between the thighs and calves of the opposite legs, the yogin sits with an upright, perfectly erect spine. Masters of yoga proclaim this stable posture to be Svastikāsana («the Auspicious Pose»). This blessed Svastikāsana is exalted by the supreme lords of yoga: it bestows total integration upon the vital prāṇic currents, uproots all bodily and spiritual afflictions (kleśas), and continually radiates sublime peace and joy. Through its faithful practice, hosts of diseases are annihilated in a single instant; a pure, radiant flame ignites within the furnace of the belly; and supreme, everlasting peace settles into the hearts of embodied beings.

Dates of the manuscripts: what is known

Manuscripts with a firm date. One manuscript is explicitly dated in the edition, Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī: in the comments on verses 1.24 and 1.56 the editors call it the “oldest dated manuscript”. The year is not printed in the reader; the project’s public materials give it as 1496 CE — only about a century from the supposed time of Svātmārāma’s authorship (the early 15th century). This manuscript (Sampūrṇānanda Library, number 30109) is known in the scholarly literature as well — it is referred to, for instance, in an article on the Amaraughaprabodha (Journal of Indian Philosophy, 2019).

In the other manuscripts the dates survive in the colophons — they are transcribed in the editors’ draft files. A systematic check of the edition’s sources (the archive of the project’s LaTeX files of 20 May 2026) yielded five more dated copies. The dates stand in scribal notes at the end of the text and are written in the figures of the “saṃvat” era (that is the Vikrama era; subtracting 57 gives the year CE):

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, number 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

Literal translation

Having properly placed both soles of the feet in the space between the knees and the thighs, and sitting with the body straight — that they call the svastika posture.

Literary translation

Svastikāsana, the “auspicious pose”. Both soles are tucked into the spaces between the thighs and the knees (close to the knees), and one sits with the body held straight: this posture is called svastika.

Editors’ commentary (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

One might wonder how the soles of the feet could be placed between the knees and thighs. Brahmānanda explains that the region of the shank near the knee should be understood by the word ‘knee’ in this verse (atra jānuśabdena jānusaṃnihito jaṅghāpradeśo grāhyaḥ jānusaṃnihito jaṅghāpradeśaḥ). This is consistent with the earliest known description of svastikāsana, found in the Pātañjalayogaśāstravivaraṇa (2.46), which states that the big toe of one foot is tucked in between the shank and thigh of the other so it is not seen (dakṣiṇaṃ pādāṅguṣṭhaṃ savyenorujaṅghena parigṛhyādṛśyaṃ kṛtvā tathā savyaṃ pādāṅguṣṭhaṃ dakṣiṇenorujaṅghenādṛśyaṃ parigṛhya yathā ca pārṣṇibhyāṃ vṛṣaṇayor apīḍaṇaṃ tathā yenāste tat svastikam āsanam). For a discussion of svastikāsana in the Pātañjalayoga tradition, see Maas 2018: 68–69. The descriptions of svastikāsana in early Śaiva Tantras do not mention the inserting of the toes between the knees and thighs (see Goodall 2004: 348–350, fn. 371).
