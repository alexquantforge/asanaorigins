Dates of the manuscripts: what is known

Manuscripts with a firm date. One manuscript is explicitly dated in the edition, Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī: in the comments on verses 1.24 and 1.56 the editors call it the “oldest dated manuscript”. The year is not printed in the reader; the project’s public materials give it as 1496 CE — only about a century from the supposed time of Svātmārāma’s authorship (the early 15th century). This manuscript (Sampūrṇānanda Library, number 30109) is known in the scholarly literature as well — it is referred to, for instance, in an article on the Amaraughaprabodha (Journal of Indian Philosophy, 2019).

In the other manuscripts the dates survive in the colophons — they are transcribed in the editors’ draft files. A systematic check of the edition’s sources (the archive of the project’s LaTeX files of 20 May 2026) yielded five more dated copies. The dates stand in scribal notes at the end of the text and are written in the figures of the “saṃvat” era (that is the Vikrama era; subtracting 57 gives the year CE):

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, number 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

All five notes are the editors’ remarks in the comments to the draft files of the edition (they are not in the published digital reader); the era is not stated in the colophons themselves, and the final dates, checked against the manuscripts, will appear in the introduction to the printed book. But the picture is already clear: the earliest dated copies are 1496 (30109), 1626/27 (Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur, manuscript 2230), 1636/37 (29899), 1650/51 (30069), 1667/68 (Jodhpur 23709), 1745/46 (Jodhpur 02237).

Several witnesses have colophons that survive without a date. Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, manuscript R17256/SR8080, Chennai (in the apparatus — “epsilon-1”): “śrī śivāya namaḥ … gaṇapati svahasta likhitaṃ” — “written by the hand of Gaṇapati himself”, no date. Lalchand M-2089, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “pi-2”): only an ownership note, “pustakam idaṃ jājīrāmavyāsasya”. Lalchand M-6494, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “delta-3”): “…iti śrīmadātmārāmaviracitāyāṃ … upadeśaḥ … śubham astu sarvajagatām”. Nepalese-German Manuscript Preservation Project, microfilm B 62-20, Nepal (in the apparatus — “alpha-1”): the brief “śrīsahajasadguruātmārāmārpaṇa saṃpūrṇam”. Nepalese-German Manuscript Preservation Project, microfilm E-1528-1 / E-1527-7(4) (in the apparatus — “zeta-2”) has no colophon at all: the text ends exactly on leaf 346, and another work begins on the next leaf.

The Berlin manuscripts are described in detail in the printed Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland catalogue. Our two Berlin manuscripts received no dating in the catalogue “Indian Manuscripts” (Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland). Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, Orientalia, folio 2166 (= “eta-omega”): Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland II, 8 (number 3417) — a paper manuscript, 53 leaves, Devanāgarī script, six lines to a page; explicitly marked “Undatiert” (“undated”), and the title stands in the colophon in the form “Hathapradipikā”. Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, or.

11056 (= “delta-2”): Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland II, 16 (number 5442) — a paper manuscript in Śāradā script, leaves 1–48 and 1–[22], ten lines; it contains an incomplete “Hathayogapradīpikā” (whose first verse is “śrīādināthāya namo ’stu…”) and the “Rudrayāmala-Vijñānabhairava”; no date is given in the description.

The same catalogue describes two more Berlin “Hathayogapradīpikās” — neither was collated for the edition, but it is curious that they too are undated: manuscript, Orientalia, octavo 659 (Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland II, 8, number 3416) — a paper manuscript, 43 leaves, Devanāgarī, “Undatiert”; and Hs Orientalia 13133 (Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland II, 15, number 5136.2) — palm leaves, Grantha script, badly damaged, in a collection together with the “Haṭharatnāvalī”; here the “Hathayogapradīpikā” is incomplete (the colophon covers only the first section), and again there is no date. In all, four copies of the “Hathayogapradīpikā” are recorded in Berlin, and not one of them is dated.

The Lalchand Research Library (Dayanand Anglo-Vedic College, Chandigarh) — the digital archive dav.splrarebooks.com. Our two manuscripts from it — M-2089 (= “pi-2”: 37 pages, Devanāgarī, from Bahawalpur) and M-6494 (= “delta-3”: 727 pages, Śāradā script, a collection called “Hathayogapradīpikādi-granthasaṅgraha”) — have no dates in their digital records. Other copies in the same library, however, do: M-1293 — 1911 of the “saṃvat” era (≈1854 CE), M-2082 — 1775 (≈1718), M-2091 — 1923 (≈1866), M-4530 — 1888 (≈1831).

The remaining witnesses (sixteen of the twenty-one manuscripts) have no dates. Undated are the Nepalese microfilms (Nepalese-German Manuscript Preservation Project, microfilm B 62-20, G 25-2, E-1528-1 / E-1527-7(4), A62-33), Sarasvatī Bhavan 30051, Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, manuscript 18885 / R17256/SR8080 / R3841/SR2190, Bharata Itihasa Samshodhaka Mandala, manuscript 64/919, “pi-1” (P11 in the project’s working list), Jodhpur 02235 and 02241.

For comparison: the Kaivalyadhāma edition of 1970 relied on only 9 manuscripts, and its oldest dated manuscript is from 1629 (Calcutta number TH 321); the date assigned to the text itself is broad: “1350–1550”. A manuscript of 1496 in the new corpus is a qualitative step forward.

Where else to look for dates: the descriptive catalogues of the Nepalese-German Manuscript Cataloguing Project (Hamburg) — for the Nepalese films (in 2026 its online search is unavailable, and the archived wiki copy preserved only one page); the descriptive catalogues of the Sarasvatī Bhavan manuscripts (Sampūrṇānanda, Vārāṇasī); the catalogues of RORI and Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur (Jodhpur; OCR of their scans barely recognises Devanāgarī); the printed introduction to the edition (École française d’Extrême-Orient, Pondicherry, late 2026) — it will contain full descriptions of all the manuscripts.

Verse 1.24

Verse page in the electronic edition: #hp01_024

कुक्कुटासनबन्धस्थो दोर्भ्यां संबध्य कन्धराम् ।शेते कूर्मवदुत्तान एतदुत्तानकूर्मकम् ॥

kukkuṭāsanabandhastho dorbhyāṃ saṃbadhya kandharām /śete kūrmavad uttāna etad uttānakūrmakam //

Edition translation (English)

While in the wild cock pose, [the yogi] binds the neck with the hands and lies [on his back] upturned like a tortoise. This is the upturned tortoise (uttānakūrmaka).

Our rendering

Uttānakūrmaka: from the cock pose the arms clasp the neck and one lies on the back, upturned like a tortoise.

Uttānakūrmaka, the “upturned tortoise”.

Literal translation

Standing in the binding of the cock pose, having clasped his neck with both arms, [the yogi] lies like a tortoise, upturned on his back: this is uttānakūrmaka.

Literary translation

Uttānakūrmaka, the “upturned tortoise”. From the cock pose one clasps the neck with the arms, lies back and stays still, like a tortoise turned on its shell.

Editors’ commentary (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

The oldest dated manuscript, Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī, has kukkuṭāsanavat kṛtvā, which is a simpler alternative to the widely attested reading kukkuṭāsanabandhasthaḥ (including Jodhpur 02235, Jodhpur and Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, manuscript 18885, Chennai), which we have accepted. Since there is no known source for this verse other than the Haṭhapradīpikā, it appears that the reading of Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī was an isolated attempt to simplify the syntax.

Dating of the text

  • The title page of the editionŚaka 1811; Saṃvat 1946not equated with a Gregorian yearBasis of the date (in the volume’s own words)The title page gives Śaka 1811 and Saṃvat 1946. Without further calendar clarification, these dates are not equated here with a single Gregorian year.

The conversion is our arithmetic, not the volume’s words. Śaka 1811: 1811 + 78 = 1889 for the months from the start of the year to December, and 1811 + 79 = 1890 for January–February. Saṃvat 1946: 1946 − 57 = 1889 and 1946 − 56 = 1890, assuming the same start of the year; in Gujarat the Vikrama saṃvat counted from Caitra is the usual reckoning, but the volume does not say so. Both dates give the same span, March 1889 to March 1890, and differ by exactly 135 years, as the eras themselves do: the title page does not contradict itself. But neither month nor day is printed on it, so no single year can be named.

Word-by-word analysis

ḍābā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ jamaṇo paga — the right foot at the base of the left thigh; ne jamaṇā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ ḍābo paga rākhī — and placing the left foot at the base of the right thigh; banne hāthathī — with both hands; ḍokane nīcī namāvavī — bend the neck down; te uttāna kūrmāsana kahevāya che — this is called Uttāna Kūrmāsana.

Literal translation (our interlinear)

At the base of the left thigh the right foot, and at the base of the right thigh the left foot placing, with both hands the neck to bend down — this is called Uttāna Kūrmāsana.

Literary translation (ours)

Place the right foot at the base of the left thigh and the left foot at the base of the right thigh; with both hands bend the neck downward. This is called Uttāna Kūrmāsana.

Textual note

The printed description does not specify inserting the arms between the thighs and shins or clasping the neck from behind; these details should not be treated as part of its wording.
