1.2. The Chronological Problem: Scholarly Arguments and Dating Debate

The historical dating of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī has been examined by leading Sanskritists and historians of yoga:

1. Arguments of Doctor M. L. Gharote and Doctor M. Venkata Reddy (Critical Edition, 2007):

  • Terminus post quem (Earliest possible date): Sundaradeva directly cites late seventeenth-century works:
  • «Haṭharatnāvalī» (Haṭharatnāvalī) by Śrīnivāsa (Śrīnivāsa), composed circa 1670–1695 CE. Sundaradeva adopts its specialized terminology, āsana lists, and quotes its verses;
  • «Kumbhakapaddhati» (Kumbhakapaddhati) by Raghuvīra (Raghuvīra), datable to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century;
  • «Yogacandrikā» (Yogacandrikā) and «Yogacintāmaṇi» (Yogacintāmaṇi) by Śivānanda Sarasvatī.
  • Terminus ante quem (Latest possible date): The oldest and only complete dated manuscript witness (manuscript number 4235, Scindia Oriental Institute, Ujjain) preserves a scribal colophon with the exact date of completion: Saṃvat 1846 (Vikrama Saṃvat), month of Vaiśākha, dark fortnight, 10th tithi, Tuesday, corresponding to April–May 1789 CE.
  • Gharote's Conclusion: The treatise was composed between 1700 and 1750 CE (first half of the eighteenth century).

2. Textological Arguments of Doctor Jason Birch (Oxford University / Haṭha Yoga Project):

  • Doctor Jason Birch points out that the Haṭhatattvakaumudī epitomizes the late medieval "encyclopedic turn" in yoga. This genre of comprehensive compendia, synthesizing hundreds of earlier sources (from Upaniṣads and Tantras to late medieval paddhatis), flourished among the scholarly Brahmin milieus of Vārāṇasī precisely in the first half of the eighteenth century.
  • Birch underscores that Sundaradeva's Sanskrit exhibits late classical syntax infused with the scientific medical lexicon of early modern India.

3. Iconographic and Structural Analysis of Professor Gudrun Bühnemann:

  • Professor Bühnemann examined the evolution of āsana classification schemes in Indian traditions. In Chapter 7 of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī, Sundaradeva retains the canonical core of 15 postures (inherited from Chapter 1 of Svātmārāma's Haṭhapradīpikā), yet reinterprets them within a therapeutic methodology and introduces distinctive technical nuances (for example, yonyāsana as an advanced modification of siddhāsana, specialized breath management during padmāsana). This marks a systematic transition before the proliferation of vast catalogs of 84 or more āsanas in late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century manuals.

4. Sociological Synthesis by Doctor Mark Singleton:

  • Mark Singleton emphasizes that Sundaradeva represents a notable synthesis of two social roles: the practicing yoga adept and the high-caste, university-educated vaidya-paṇḍita. Such integration was characteristic of cosmopolitan Vārāṇasī in the eighteenth century, where Haṭha yoga techniques were absorbed into the orthodox curriculum of health, longevity, and therapeutics.

Literal Translation (Verses 7–8)

At the root of the left thigh let him place the right foot joined with the left foot; having twisted the back, having stabilized the body, [this is] the renowned doctrine of formidable Matsyendra. Matsyendra's seat, being a weapon for shattering the multitude of fierce diseases, through practice verily bestows upon a man the kindling of the gastric fire, the awakening of Kuṇḍalinī, and the steadiness of the moon.

Literary Translation (Verses 7–8)

Placing the right foot at the base of the left thigh and positioning the left foot on the outside, the yogin rotates his spine and locks his body in a firm twist. This is the celebrated teaching of the mighty master Matsyendranātha. Matsyendra's seat (matsyendrapīṭha) acts as a shattering weapon against the host of virulent diseases. Through regular practice, it unfailingly fans the digestive fire of the belly, awakens the dormant Kuṇḍalinī, and eternally arrests the downward dissipation of the lunar nectar.

Textological Commentary and Critical Apparatus

  • Footnote 1 to Verse 7: Witness «b» interpolates the particle 'tha (atha), breaking the smooth rhythm of the Upajāti meter preserved in «a».
  • Footnote 2 to Verse 7: In «a», the neuter participle yuktam agrees with pādayugmam; in «b», the masculine yuktaḥ shifts agreement to the practitioner.
  • Footnote 4 to Verse 8: In «a», khaṇḍanāstram (-astram — "weapon of destruction"); in «b», khaṇḍanāntam (-antam — "terminating in destruction"). The martial metaphor of astra is typical of tantric yoga; reading «b» is a graphic scribal misreading of stra as nta.

Dates of the manuscripts: what is known

Manuscripts with a firm date. One manuscript is explicitly dated in the edition, Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30109, Vārāṇasī: in the comments on verses 1.24 and 1.56 the editors call it the “oldest dated manuscript”. The year is not printed in the reader; the project’s public materials give it as 1496 CE — only about a century from the supposed time of Svātmārāma’s authorship (the early 15th century). This manuscript (Sampūrṇānanda Library, number 30109) is known in the scholarly literature as well — it is referred to, for instance, in an article on the Amaraughaprabodha (Journal of Indian Philosophy, 2019).

In the other manuscripts the dates survive in the colophons — they are transcribed in the editors’ draft files. A systematic check of the edition’s sources (the archive of the project’s LaTeX files of 20 May 2026) yielded five more dated copies. The dates stand in scribal notes at the end of the text and are written in the figures of the “saṃvat” era (that is the Vikrama era; subtracting 57 gives the year CE):

Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, number 2230, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “eta-2”): “saṃvat 16|83” → 1683 of the Vikrama era, that is 1626–1627 CE (in the apparatus to chapter 1 this manuscript occurs 104 times — it is the second oldest dated copy in the whole corpus); Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 29899, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “pi-omega”): “…saṃvat 16|93 varṣe … liṣitaṃ jamadagnigirisanyāsī svātmapaṭhanārthaṃ” → 1693 of the Vikrama era, that is 1636–1637 CE; it was copied by the ascetic Jamadagni-giri “for his own reading”; Sarasvatī Bhavan, manuscript number 30069, Vārāṇasī (in the apparatus — “delta-1”, the most “talkative” manuscript of chapter 1, with 137 differences from the edition’s text): “…iti haṭhayogapradīpikāyāṃ … saṃvat 1707 jyeṣṭha kṛṣṇa 4 bhṛgau liṣitam idaṃ” → 1707 of the Vikrama era, that is 1650–1651 CE; the month of Jyeṣṭha, the fourth day of the dark fortnight; Jodhpur 02237, Jodhpur (in the apparatus — “delta-omega”; four chapters): at the end of the colophon it notes “saṃvat 1802” → 1745–1746 CE (the first leaf of the manuscript bears an ownership note: “this book belongs to Gulābarāi, son of Tulsīrāma-śarman”); Jodhpur 23709, Jodhpur — “saṃvat 1724” → 1667–1668 CE; this is a copy from the same project, but it was not collated for chapter 1.

All five notes are the editors’ remarks in the comments to the draft files of the edition (they are not in the published digital reader); the era is not stated in the colophons themselves, and the final dates, checked against the manuscripts, will appear in the introduction to the printed book. But the picture is already clear: the earliest dated copies are 1496 (30109), 1626/27 (Maharaja Man Singh Pustak Prakash, Mehrangarh Fort, Jodhpur, manuscript 2230), 1636/37 (29899), 1650/51 (30069), 1667/68 (Jodhpur 23709), 1745/46 (Jodhpur 02237).

Several witnesses have colophons that survive without a date. Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, manuscript R17256/SR8080, Chennai (in the apparatus — “epsilon-1”): “śrī śivāya namaḥ … gaṇapati svahasta likhitaṃ” — “written by the hand of Gaṇapati himself”, no date. Lalchand M-2089, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “pi-2”): only an ownership note, “pustakam idaṃ jājīrāmavyāsasya”. Lalchand M-6494, Chandigarh (in the apparatus — “delta-3”): “…iti śrīmadātmārāmaviracitāyāṃ … upadeśaḥ … śubham astu sarvajagatām”. Nepalese-German Manuscript Preservation Project, microfilm B 62-20, Nepal (in the apparatus — “alpha-1”): the brief “śrīsahajasadguruātmārāmārpaṇa saṃpūrṇam”. Nepalese-German Manuscript Preservation Project, microfilm E-1528-1 / E-1527-7(4) (in the apparatus — “zeta-2”) has no colophon at all: the text ends exactly on leaf 346, and another work begins on the next leaf.

11056 (= “delta-2”): Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland II, 16 (number 5442) — a paper manuscript in Śāradā script, leaves 1–48 and 1–[22], ten lines; it contains an incomplete “Hathayogapradīpikā” (whose first verse is “śrīādināthāya namo ’stu…”) and the “Rudrayāmala-Vijñānabhairava”; no date is given in the description.

The same catalogue describes two more Berlin “Hathayogapradīpikās” — neither was collated for the edition, but it is curious that they too are undated: manuscript, Orientalia, octavo 659 (Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland II, 8, number 3416) — a paper manuscript, 43 leaves, Devanagari, “Undatiert”; and Hs Orientalia 13133 (Verzeichnis der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland II, 15, number 5136.2) — palm leaves, Grantha script, badly damaged, in a collection together with the “Haṭharatnāvalī”; here the “Hathayogapradīpikā” is incomplete (the colophon covers only the first section), and again there is no date. In all, four copies of the “Hathayogapradīpikā” are recorded in Berlin, and not one of them is dated.

Literal translation

That [pose] in which the right foot is placed at the base of the left thigh and is held by the right arm wrapped around the outside of the knee, with the body turned aside — let it be the posture taught by the revered Matsyanātha. “Matsyendra’s seat” is a weapon that shatters the hosts of fierce tormenting diseases of the belly; through practice it gives men the awakening of Kuṇḍalinī and steadiness of the spine.

A caveat follows from this: neither “Matsyendra’s seat” nor “Matsyendra’s pose” describes the twist as it is practised today: here the foot is placed at the root of the thigh, and the arm encircles the knee from outside.

Literary translation

Matsyendrāsana — the pose taught by the revered Matsyanātha: the right foot is set at the root of the left thigh, the right arm, brought round the outside of the knee, holds [the foot], and the trunk is turned to the side. This is “Matsyendra’s seat”, a formidable weapon against the many wasting ailments of the belly. Persistent practice awakens Kuṇḍalinī and gives the body unshakeable steadiness.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.26 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

In the second verse quarter, most of the manuscript groups have a compound with doṣṇā at the end, as seen also in the Yogacintāmaṇi, Haṭharatnāvalī and Yuktabhavadeva. The instrumental ending (‘with the hand’) works well with the gerund (pragṛhya) in the third verse quarter and the object (dakṣpādaṃ) in the first quarter. This reading indicates that the right foot is held by the hand of the arm that is wrapped around the outside of the left leg, which would be the right hand (dakṣadoṣṇā) rather than the left (vāmadoṣṇā), as shown in Figure 1. Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, manuscript 18885, Chennai and most manuscripts of the Haṭharatnāvalī have dakṣapādo in the first pāda. This reading yields the same meaning as the adopted one if read with vāmapādaṃ in the second. However, Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, manuscript 18885, Chennai and manuscripts of the Haṭharatnāvalī read vāmadoṣṇā, which is not good because it leaves the gerund without an object. The version of this verse in Jyotsnā (1.26), which is supported by some manuscripts in two important groups, “eta” and “pi”, has two objects of the gerund, namely the left and right feet, without an instrumental or conjunctive particle. In his commentarial remarks, Brahmānanda proposes that the left foot is grasped by the right hand and the right foot by the left foot, as seen in Figure 2.

Editors’ commentary, on verse 1.27 (as in the edition, in English; manuscript names expanded to shelfmarks)

Here we understand jaṭhara, which usually refers to the stomach, to mean “tough” or “persistent” (compare Amarakośa 3.3.189: jaṭharaḥ kaṭhine’pi syād adhastād api cādharaḥ). In 1.27d, the compound daṇḍasthiratvaṃ (‘steadiness of the spine’) is attested by all the important manuscript groups and testimonia, so it was likely original. However, the Jyotsnā (1.27d) has candrasthiratvaṃ (‘steadiness of the moon’), and this reading is well-attested in many manuscripts that are lower on the stemma. Brahmānanda understands steadiness here as ‘the absence of flow’ (sthiratvaṃ kṣaraṇābhāvaṃ), a reference to the moon retaining its nectar.

ડાબા સાથળના મૂળમાં ધરેલા જે જમણો પગ તેની એડીને પીઠ પછવાડે ડાબો હાથ લઇ જઇને તેથી પકડી પછી જમણા પગના ઢીંચણ આગળ જમીનપર રાખેલા ડાબા પગના અંગુઠાને જમણા હાથથી પકડીને બેસવુ તે મત્સ્યેન્દ્રાસન કહેવાય છે.

ḍābā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ dharelā je jamaṇo paga tenī eḍīne pīṭha pachavāḍe ḍābo hātha laī jaīne tethī pakaḍī pachī jamaṇā paganā ḍhīñcaṇa āgaḷa jamīnapara rākhelā ḍābā paganā aṅguṭhāne jamaṇā hāthathī pakaḍīne besavu te matsyendrāsana kahevāya che.

д̣āбā сāтхал̣анā мӯл̣амāм̣ дхарелā дже джаман̣о пага тенӣ ед̣ӣне пӣт̣ха пачхавāд̣е д̣āбо хāтха лаӣ джаӣне тетхӣ пакад̣ӣ пачхӣ джаман̣ā паганā д̣хӣн̃чан̣а āгал̣а джамӣнапара рāкхелā д̣āбā паганā ан̇гут̣хāне джаман̣ā хāтхатхӣ пакад̣ӣне бесаву те матсйендрāсана кахевāйа чхе.

Word-by-word analysis

ḍābā sāthaḷanā mūḷamāṁ dharelā je jamaṇo paga — the right foot placed at the base of the left thigh; tenī eḍīne — its heel; pīṭha pachavāḍe ḍābo hātha laī jaīne — taking the left hand behind the back; tethī pakaḍī — grasping with it; pachī — then; jamaṇā paganā ḍhīñcaṇa āgaḷa — in front of the right knee; jamīnapara rākhelā — placed on the ground; ḍābā paganā aṅguṭhāne — the big toe of the left foot; jamaṇā hāthathī pakaḍīne besavu — grasp with the right hand and sit; te matsyendrāsana kahevāya che — this is called Matsyendrāsana.

English translation

Take the left hand behind the back and grasp the heel of the right foot, which is placed at the base of the left thigh. Then grasp with the right hand the big toe of the left foot, which is placed on the ground in front of the right knee, and sit. This is called Matsyendrāsana.

пага татхā хāтхане ӯлат̣ā бадалавāтхӣ ā āсанано бӣджо пракāра тхāйа чхе.

Additional passage — English

The book adds that reversing the legs and hands produces another form of the posture.

Printed footnote

મહાન યોગી મત્સ્યેન્દ્રનાથે આ આસનનો ચિરકાળ અભ્યાસ કર્યો હતો, તેથી તેમના નામથી આ આસન પ્રસિદ્ધિમાં આવ્યું છે.

mahāna yogī matsyendranāthe ā āsanano cirakāḷa abhyāsa karyo hato, tethī temanā nāmathī ā āsana prasiddhimāṁ āvyuṁ che.

махāна йогӣ матсйендранāтхе ā āсанано чиракāл̣а абхйāса карйо хато, тетхӣ теманā нāматхӣ ā āсана прасиддхимāм̣ āвйум̣ чхе.

Printed footnote — English

The great yogin Matsyendranātha practised this posture for a long time; it therefore became known by his name. This is the attribution made in the printed footnote.

Traditional effect

આ આસન ઘણા પ્રકારના રોગોને દૂર કરે છે, જઠરાગ્નિ પ્રદીપ્ત કરે છે, કુંડલીનીને જાગૃત કરે છે, નિદ્રાને નિયમમાં આણે છે, તથા તાળવાની ઉપરથી ઝરતા ચંદ્રામૃતને ત્યાંજ સ્થિર કરે છે.

ā āsana ghaṇā prakāranā rogone dūra kare che, jaṭharāgni pradīpta kare che, kuṃḍalīnīne jāgṛta kare che, nidrāne niyamamāṃ āṇe che, tathā tāḷavānī uparathī jharatā caṃdrāmṛtane tyāṃja sthira kare che.

ā āсана гхан̣ā пракāранā рогоне дӯра каре чхе, джат̣харāгни прадӣпта каре чхе, кум̣д̣алӣнӣне джāгр̣та каре чхе, нидрāне нийамамāм̣ āн̣е чхе, татхā тāл̣авāнӣ упаратхӣ джхаратā чам̣дрāмр̣тане тйāм̣джа стхира каре чхе.

Traditional effect — English

The book claims that this posture removes many kinds of disease, kindles digestive fire, awakens kuṇḍalinī, regulates sleep, and holds in place the lunar nectar said to drip from above the palate.
