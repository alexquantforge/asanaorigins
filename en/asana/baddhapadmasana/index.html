1.2. The Chronological Problem: Scholarly Arguments and Dating Debate

The historical dating of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī has been examined by leading Sanskritists and historians of yoga:

1. Arguments of Doctor M. L. Gharote and Doctor M. Venkata Reddy (Critical Edition, 2007):

  • Terminus post quem (Earliest possible date): Sundaradeva directly cites late seventeenth-century works:
  • «Haṭharatnāvalī» (Haṭharatnāvalī) by Śrīnivāsa (Śrīnivāsa), composed circa 1670–1695 CE. Sundaradeva adopts its specialized terminology, āsana lists, and quotes its verses;
  • «Kumbhakapaddhati» (Kumbhakapaddhati) by Raghuvīra (Raghuvīra), datable to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century;
  • «Yogacandrikā» (Yogacandrikā) and «Yogacintāmaṇi» (Yogacintāmaṇi) by Śivānanda Sarasvatī.
  • Terminus ante quem (Latest possible date): The oldest and only complete dated manuscript witness (manuscript number 4235, Scindia Oriental Institute, Ujjain) preserves a scribal colophon with the exact date of completion: Saṃvat 1846 (Vikrama Saṃvat), month of Vaiśākha, dark fortnight, 10th tithi, Tuesday, corresponding to April–May 1789 CE.
  • Gharote's Conclusion: The treatise was composed between 1700 and 1750 CE (first half of the eighteenth century).

2. Textological Arguments of Doctor Jason Birch (Oxford University / Haṭha Yoga Project):

  • Doctor Jason Birch points out that the Haṭhatattvakaumudī epitomizes the late medieval "encyclopedic turn" in yoga. This genre of comprehensive compendia, synthesizing hundreds of earlier sources (from Upaniṣads and Tantras to late medieval paddhatis), flourished among the scholarly Brahmin milieus of Vārāṇasī precisely in the first half of the eighteenth century.
  • Birch underscores that Sundaradeva's Sanskrit exhibits late classical syntax infused with the scientific medical lexicon of early modern India.

3. Iconographic and Structural Analysis of Professor Gudrun Bühnemann:

  • Professor Bühnemann examined the evolution of āsana classification schemes in Indian traditions. In Chapter 7 of the Haṭhatattvakaumudī, Sundaradeva retains the canonical core of 15 postures (inherited from Chapter 1 of Svātmārāma's Haṭhapradīpikā), yet reinterprets them within a therapeutic methodology and introduces distinctive technical nuances (for example, yonyāsana as an advanced modification of siddhāsana, specialized breath management during padmāsana). This marks a systematic transition before the proliferation of vast catalogs of 84 or more āsanas in late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century manuals.

4. Sociological Synthesis by Doctor Mark Singleton:

  • Mark Singleton emphasizes that Sundaradeva represents a notable synthesis of two social roles: the practicing yoga adept and the high-caste, university-educated vaidya-paṇḍita. Such integration was characteristic of cosmopolitan Vārāṇasī in the eighteenth century, where Haṭha yoga techniques were absorbed into the orthodox curriculum of health, longevity, and therapeutics.

Literal Translation (Verses 29–32)

Having established Padmāsana, with the left hand let the concentrated yogin grasp the right big toe from behind the back. And likewise from behind the back with the [other] hand having grasped the other big toe, this should be known as Baddhapadmāsana, which cures fevers of vāta and kapha. Having grasped the two big toes from behind with both hands, let him bind them. This Baddhapadmāsana is indeed the seat of the destruction of poisons and doṣas, destroying manifold diseases of men and bringing bliss to yogins. Thus is Baddhapadmāsana stated in the Yogacandrikā.

Literary Translation (Verses 29–32)

Assuming the Lotus Pose, the focused yogin reaches around his back with his left hand to grasp the big toe of the right foot, and reaches behind with his right hand to grasp the toe of the left foot. This locked posture is known as Baddhapadmāsana («the Bound Lotus»); clinically, it dispels stubborn mixed fevers caused by aggravated vāta and kapha. As affirmed in the authoritative Yogacandrikā: «Reaching behind the back with both arms, the practitioner tightly binds the toes of both feet. Verily, this Bound Lotus is an unfailing fountainhead for eradicating internal toxins and humors; it purges the manifold maladies of mortals and confers serene bliss upon the yogins».

Textological Commentary and Critical Apparatus

  • Footnote 1 to Verse 29: In «a», vāmapāṇinā ("with the left hand"); in «b», dakṣapāṇinā ("with the right hand"). Both manuscripts capture mirror executions of the arm lock behind the back.
  • Footnote 1 to Verse 31: In «a», the precise 9th-class optative verb nibadhnīyāt ("let him bind"); «b» dilutes this with the generic participle samāhitaḥ.
  • Footnote 2 to Verse 31: In «a», the evocative viṣadoṣaharāspadam ("abode of the destruction of toxins and doṣas"); «b» substitutes the cliché viṣadoṣaharaṃ smṛtam.

Hindi commentary (corrected text)

दक्षिण पाद वाम उरु के ऊपर और वाम पाद दक्षिण ऊरु के ऊपर स्थापन करके करद्वय द्वारा पीठ से घुमाकर चरणों के अंगूठे धारण करके चिबुक वक्षस्थल पर स्थापन करके नासाग्रभाग दर्शन करने से बद्ध पद्मासन हुआ करता है, इस आसन द्वारा नाना प्रकार की व्याधियों का नाश होता है।

Word-by-word analysis

vāma-ūru-upari — on the left thigh; dakṣiṇaṃ hi caraṇaṃ — right foot; saṃsthāpya — (gerund) having placed; vāmaṃ — left; tathā — Also; dakṣa-ūru-upari — on the right thigh; paścimena vidhinā — back method; dhṛtvā — capturing; karābhyāṃ — (instrumental dual) hands; dṛḍham — tightly; aṅguṣṭhau — (accusative dual) two big toes; hṛdaye — (locative singular) on the heart (chest); nidhāya — (gerund) putting; cibukaṃ — (accusative singular) chin; nāsā-agram — tip of the nose; ālokayet — should be contemplated; etat — This; vyādhi-vināśanaṃ — destroying diseases; sukha-karaṃ — bringing happiness; baddha-padmāsanaṃ — Baddhapadmāsana; procyate — called.

Translation

“Placing the right foot on the left thigh and the left foot on the right, firmly grasping the big toes with the hands [crossed] from behind; placing the chin on the chest and looking at the tip of the nose - this is called Baddhapadmāsana, destroying diseases and giving happiness.”

English translation of the Hindi commentary

“By placing the right foot on the left thigh and the left on the right, placing both hands behind the back and grasping the big toes, pressing the chin to the chest and looking at the tip of the nose, Baddhapadmāsana is performed. Thanks to this āsana, various types of diseases are destroyed.”
