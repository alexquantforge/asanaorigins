Verses: 2.47

2.47

अधस्तु शेते हृदयं निधाय भूमौ च पादौ च प्रसार्यमाणौ । शिरश्च धृत्वा करदण्डयुग्मे देहाग्निकारं मकरासनं स्यात् ॥ ४७ ॥

adhastu śete hṛdayaṃ nidhāya bhūmau ca pādau ca prasāryamāṇau | śiraśca dhṛtvā karadaṇḍayugme dehāgnikāraṃ makarāsanaṃ syāt || 47 ||

адхасту ш́ете хр̣дайам̣ нидхāйа бхӯмау ча пāдау ча прасāрйамāн̣ау | ш́ираш́ча дхр̣твā карадан̣д̣айугме дехāгникāрам̣ макарāсанам̣ сйāт || 47 ||

Hindi commentary (corrected text)

अधोमुख होकर शयन करके पृथिवी पर वक्षस्थल स्थापन करके पादद्वय विस्तार करते हुए करयुगल द्वारा मस्तक को धारण करने से मकरासन हुआ करता है, इस आसन के अभ्यास से शरीरस्थ तेज की वृद्धि हुआ करती है।

Word-by-word analysis

adhas tu śete — lies prone (face down); hṛdayaṃ nidhāya — putting your heart/chest; bhūmau — on the ground; ca — And; pādau — legs; ca — And; prasāryamāṇau — elongated; śiraḥ ca dhṛtvā — and holding your head; kara-daṇḍa-yugme — on both hands (forearms); deha-agni-kāraṃ — kindling the fire of the body; makarāsanaṃ syāt — this will be Makarāsana.

Translation

“Lying face down, placing the chest on the ground and extending the legs, holding the head with the hands is Makarāsana (Crocodile Pose), kindling the fire of the body.”

English translation of the Hindi commentary

“Lying face down, placing the chest on the ground, extending the legs and supporting the head with the hands, Makarāsana is performed. Through the practice of this āsana, the internal heat (tejas) of the body increases.”

Dating the Rudrayāmalottaratantra

There is no precise date, and the text itself contains no chronological indications. The most definite statements belong to T. Goudriaan and S. Gupta.

On the yāmalas as a genre (Goudriaan & Gupta 1981: 40)

“The time of their origin is difficult to assess. Their formative period in any case lies before A. D. 900.”

On our tantra (Goudriaan & Gupta 1981: 47)

“The text which is most often credited with being the Rudrayāmala proper, the Uttaratantra, is certainly much later than the period in which the old Yāmala literature flourished.”

The arguments for lateness listed there (page 47) apply in part directly to our section: the developed system of cakras; dependence on the Kulārṇava; the use of the term vāyavī for the kuṇḍalinī (in our text — vāyavī kalā in verse 25 and vāyavī-priyaḥ in Mālāsana); the enumeration of types of haṭhayoga. The same page notes that Pandey, Bagchi and Gonda likewise regard the Uttaratantra as a relatively late text (note 63; cited via Goudriaan).

Finally, J. Birch notes the only occurrence of the term haṭhayoga in Śaiva tantras — in the fifty-fifth chapter of the Rudrayāmalottaratantra — while conceding that the passage may be an interpolation (Birch 2011, note 90). The argument for dating stands either way: the technical use of the term is not attested before the eleventh century.

In sum. No precise date has been established, and there is no received century for the Uttaratantra. The yāmala genre took shape before 900 CE, but our text is “certainly much later”. The estimate “not earlier than the 11th–12th century”, which the article adopts, is an inference from the features listed above, not a quotation: none of the authors cited names such a century.

The layer of postures named after the social classes cannot date the text: their fruit is formulated socially, which differs markedly from verses 23–47, where the fruit is always yogic. The layer stands out reliably, but it offers no date.
